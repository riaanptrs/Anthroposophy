## Four reading maps and PDF-checked primary passages

Quotation typography below is transcribed from the PDF; line-wrap hyphens are joined. Excerpts are brief, and the complete assigned reading provides their context.

### 2.1 — Memory and the fruits of experience

**Central question:** What remains from an experience when its immediate perception has passed?

**Source:** original continuous Chapter II, PDF captures 32–34, ending after the opening paragraph of capture 34.

**Primary passage:** “The human spirit grows through assimilated experiences.” — PDF capture 34, first column, opening paragraph.

**Optional supporting passage:** “By remembrance the soul preserves the yesterday, by action it prepares the to-morrow.” — PDF capture 32, middle column.

**Argument:** The present bodily encounter → soul's continued conception/recognition → spirit's appropriation of fruits. Episodes can fade while acquired ability continues. Alongside this inward direction, action alters the world's subsequent course. The latter is introduced here and resumed for karma in 2.4.

**Source examples:** rose and its understood truth; remembered blue; recognizing someone; learning reading/writing; cut branch. Explain two decisive examples in the teaching, and leave the others in the assigned source rather than creating a parade of repeated examples.

**Important distinction:** a remembered episode and a retained capacity are different; neither is the original sensation remaining physically present. Avoid importing the detailed 1971 account of a bodily sign.

**Connection:** The source's next paragraph asks whether the history of spiritual form, like bodily form, can be bounded by a single lifetime.

### 2.2 — Biography and spiritual individuality

**Central question:** Why does Steiner argue that individuality calls for a spiritual history before birth?

**Source:** continuous Chapter II, PDF captures 34–36; precise boundaries are in the division table.

**Primary passage:** “As physical man, I repeat the shape of my forefathers. What do I repeat as spiritual man?” — PDF capture 35, middle column.

**Optional supporting passage:** “For as spiritual human being, each one is in fact his own species.” — PDF capture 36, first column, opening paragraph.

**Argument:** bodily form needs a preceding physical history → spiritual individuality also has a definite form, shown in predispositions and biography → neither general species membership nor parents' distinct spiritual biographies specifies this individual → Steiner infers a prior history of the same spirit → repeated embodiment is his proposed continuity. His rejection of a purely bodily explanation and trust in thinking belong to this argument's premises and defence.

**Source examples:** Mr. Smith of Crowcorner; lion/dove versus individual biography; Schiller; Goethe. Use Mr. Smith as the straightforward source example; retain the species analogy with its intended spiritual meaning.

**Important distinction:** “spiritual form” means an individual configuration and development, not another visible body. “His own species” does not create a human biological species for every person.

**Connection:** Having asserted that essential individuality is not inherited from parents, Steiner now explains which personal traits he does allow to be inherited and how spirit receives the fruits of learning.

### 2.3 — Inherited traits and capacities carried forward

**Central question:** How does Steiner distinguish inherited tendencies from an individuality's acquired spiritual capacities?

**Source:** continuous Chapter II, PDF captures 36–38; start with the right-column “In such observations…” paragraph of capture 36.

**Primary passage:** “These experiences themselves pass away, but their fruits remain.” — PDF capture 38, first column.

**Optional supporting passage:** “In each life the human spirit appears as a repetition of itself with the fruits of its former experiences in previous lives.” — PDF capture 38, right column.

**Argument:** soul-body/sentient-soul mediates bodily heredity and personal tendencies; consciousness-soul/spirit-self mediates experience's spiritual fruits. Learning turns experience into ability. Source examples of innate and acquired capacity motivate Steiner's extension from ordinary learning to fruits brought from prior lives. Life-spirit then names a continuity between personal existences, in contrast to life-body's continuity of physical form between generations.

**Source examples:** Goethe's parental traits; reading/learning revisited; Mozart's musical retention; Lessing's cultivated criticism. Goethe and the fruits-of-learning comparison illuminate different sides of the distinction; avoid adding several new course examples.

**Important distinction:** inherited personal traits are not excluded from the account; retained individuality is not an unchanged everyday personality or retained episodic memory of a prior life.

**Connection:** Inner fruits are only one lasting result of a soul's life. The next reading asks how its outward deeds also remain related to the individual.

### 2.4 — Deeds, destiny, and karma

**Central question:** How does Steiner connect the continuing effects of deeds with destiny across repeated lives?

**Source:** continuous Chapter II, PDF captures 39–41.

**Primary passage:** “For only the one part of my deed is in the outer world; the other is in myself.” — PDF capture 40, first column.

**Optional supporting passage:** “The body is subject to the laws of heredity; the soul is subject to the self-created fate. One calls this fate created by the man himself his karma.” — PDF capture 41, middle column.

**Argument:** an act persists outwardly in consequences and inwardly in capacities → interrupted activity does not sever the relation, as in sleep and waking → changed surroundings still meet developed abilities, as in migration → Steiner extends this relation to a re-embodied spirit and the world with which earlier actions have linked it → karma concerns that self-created relation of soul-life to circumstances, while reincarnation concerns the continuing spirit's embodiments. Present deeds create further relations, rather than making action useless.

**Source examples:** sleeping/waking; a built house; Kentucky cave animals; mechanic and bank clerk relocating to America. Prioritize sleep and migration for teaching; the cave example can be an optional historical note.

**Important distinction:** karma, reincarnation, and bodily inheritance are separate relations in the final threefold account. The source does not present a reward/punishment judge. Its strong claim about joys and sorrows must be attributed to the account; it does not tell the learner any particular person's supposed earlier actions.

**Connection:** Chapter III investigates the three worlds already presupposed by the distinction among body, soul, and spirit.
