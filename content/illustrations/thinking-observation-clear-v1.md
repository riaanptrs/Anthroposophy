## What the diagram explains

| Board element | Course explanation |
| --- | --- |
| THINKING → CONCEPT | Thinking actively forms and connects concepts. In the billiard example it supplies an intelligible relation among motion, impact and the conditions of the event. It does not produce the physical balls or their collision. |
| OBSERVATION → PERCEPT | A percept is a content encountered through observation. Observation does not manufacture that content. The lecturer borrows Chapter 4's term to organize the Chapter 3 discussion. |
| THE GIVEN | Contents can present themselves for examination, including inward experiences. An experienced dream or hallucination does not establish the existence of its apparent external object. |
| Concepts and ideas in the list | A previously formed concept or thought can itself become an observed content. Its presence in the list does not erase the activity that originally formed it. |
| (THINKING) | Turning to an earlier act of thinking makes that activity an object of fresh inquiry. The parentheses mark this exceptional shift of attention. Awareness while reasoning differs from making that same current production its own contemplative object. |
| The “not given” | In this lecture contrast, an actively developed conceptual connection calls for the thinker's participation rather than merely appearing as a received content. This label does not mean that thinking is forever inaccessible to observation. |

## Revised teaching notes

1. **Observation and thinking are two starting points.** Observation supplies what presents itself; thinking develops conceptual relations through which we seek to understand it. “Phenomenon” and “concept” should not be used interchangeably.
2. **Inward experiences also count as observed contents.** At sunset, colors and one's pleasure can both be noticed. Connecting the sunset with an explanation is a further conceptual task. A feeling's occurrence and the truth of a judgment about the event are different questions.
3. **Thinking is an activity; a thought can be its content or remembered result.** Thinking is not defined merely as revising earlier thoughts. Revision becomes a useful exercise when we reconstruct the reasons connecting the steps instead of only recalling an answer.
4. **The ordinarily unobserved element is the productive activity.** In everyday thinking, attention commonly goes to the problem or object. This does not mean the thinker must be unaware of what they are doing. Afterwards, the activity itself can become the object of investigation.
5. **Participation does not yet prove ethical freedom.** The opening explicitly postpones whether thinking's apparent independence establishes freedom. “I am in charge” should describe participation in developing a relation, without treating every thought as consciously chosen or the freedom question as already settled.
6. **Subject and object are concepts formed through thinking.** Steiner argues that investigating their distinction already uses thinking. This concerns the starting point for understanding, not a claim that thinking physically creates an observer or an external world.
7. **A clear connection can still be misapplied.** Understanding how one's concepts connect differs from possessing the right concepts for a particular event. Confidence alone does not establish correctness.

An appropriate existing course exercise is **((3 × 3) + 1) ÷ 2 = 5**: first perform the operations, then reconstruct why each step follows. The second task makes earlier thinking the object of new thinking. Keep the parentheses; omitting them changes the calculation.
