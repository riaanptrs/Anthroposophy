## 2. Course design decision

Keep the agreed starting point: **Theosophy itself supplies the sequence**. Use the existing study guide as Lesson 0, then begin with the opening encounter with flowers. Expand outward only as the book introduces its concepts.

Recommended structure: **21 book lessons, plus Lesson 0 and a final synthesis (Lesson 22)**. These are proposed teaching units, not a compulsory timetable. Aim for 30–45 minutes per session; split the longer readings into two sessions if beginners cannot explain the central distinction after the first. Each page range is a unit envelope, not a demand to read the whole range aloud at once.

The course must preserve three things together:

1. **Fidelity:** explain what Steiner actually claims, including that he regards spiritual worlds as real. Do not quietly convert the entire book into metaphor or generic mindfulness.
2. **Clarity:** distinguish descriptions of ordinary experience, philosophical arguments, analogies, and reports of supersensible perception. Understanding the text is not independent confirmation of its claims.
3. **Learner independence:** reward accurate explanation, careful questions, and reasoned disagreement. Neither belief nor reports of spiritual experiences are learning requirements.

Teach terminology progressively. Do not present all the threefold, fourfold, sevenfold, and ninefold classifications in Lesson 1. Do not equate body/soul/spirit with thinking/feeling/willing as a simple one-to-one correspondence.

## 3. Prefaces and introduction: how to approach the book

**Role in the argument:** Steiner explains what kind of knowledge he claims, why he thinks it matters for practical life, and how he expects the reader to participate. This is the reading contract behind the entire work.

### Key takeaways

- Slow, active reading matters: the prefaces ask readers to work through ideas rather than merely accumulate statements.
- Steiner distinguishes discovering supersensible realities from understanding another person's account of them. In his view, the latter can precede direct perception.
- Ordinary language is stretched to describe experiences he says are unlike physical objects. The revised-edition preface stresses mobile rather than rigid conceptions.
- He regards spiritual knowledge as a contribution to practical life, not an escape from it.
- His claims about the adequacy of his method are part of the book's position. A learner can reconstruct them without having to accept his criticisms of skeptics.

**Teaching use:** add a brief source-reading segment to Lesson 0. Ask students to mark a claim, an analogy, and a question in a short assigned paragraph. Revisit the longer methodological material with Chapter IV rather than making it a long preliminary hurdle.

**Check:** How does Steiner distinguish understanding a report from having the reported experience?

**Expected answer:** he says readers can thoughtfully understand a spiritual investigator's account before acquiring the capacities for firsthand perception. Understanding the account and independently verifying it are different achievements.

**Likely misunderstanding:** “If I question the text, I am unfit to study it.” Correct this explicitly. The course permits questioning and does not use the text's rhetoric about disbelief to dismiss students.

## 4. Chapter I — The Essential Nature of Man

**Central question:** What distinctions does Steiner think are needed to understand a human being?

**Chapter outcome:** the learner can explain the progression from ordinary experience to Steiner's more elaborate account of human constitution, and identify where new spiritual premises enter it.

### A. Opening: the threefold relationship to the world — pp. 1–6

**Key takeaways**

- The flower example separates the encountered object, the individual's pleasure and memory, and the knowledge sought about the object's enduring relations.
- Body initially names our bodily relationship to the environment; soul names individual inward experience; spirit names the relationship to truth and intelligible order beyond private liking.
- These are introductory definitions. Later descriptions extend them, and should not be imported prematurely into this opening.
- The same encounter can involve all three aspects. They are not three separate people or three sealed containers.

**Teach with:** a flower, leaf, or familiar object; three columns for perception, personal response, and an explanation that could be examined by another person. A question about growth is a starting point for inquiry, not already knowledge of a growth law.

**Misunderstanding to prevent:** “Anything I think is spirit, therefore whatever I think is true.” Steiner distinguishes a claim to truth from personal preference; the learner still needs reasons.

### B. Corporeal, soul, and spiritual nature — pp. 6–11

**Key takeaways**

- Steiner considers human embodiment alongside mineral, plant, and animal existence, then emphasizes the specifically human relation to thought.
- Sensory organs and the inward experience of a sensation are distinguished. Soul includes sensation, feeling, and willing; it does not mean emotion alone.
- Thinking can examine perceptions and guide actions. In this account it is not merely an instrument for satisfying desire.

**Teach with:** a fictional encounter with a bell: vibration and hearing, the listener's response, and inquiry into the sound or a decision about how to respond. State which distinctions come from the text.

**Misunderstanding to prevent:** “A description of private experience proves that the brain has no role.” The chapter itself discusses the brain as an instrument. The further philosophical conclusion requires its own examination.

**Editorial issue:** the Carus quotation on p. 7 uses demeaning disability terminology, and pp. 7 and 11 make claims about brain organization. Explain their historical place in the argument; do not turn them into judgments about human worth or present them as a current biology lesson. Any contemporary scientific comparison requires separate sourcing.

### C. Body, Soul and Spirit — pp. 11–39

This subsection is much longer than the first three and needs four lessons.

**1. Life and sensation, pp. 11–20**

- Physical constitution, formative life, and inward sensation are differentiated.
- Etheric/life body is Steiner's term for a proposed supersensible formative organization. It is not the physicist's ether, a chemical substance, or simply a synonym for metabolism.
- Read the long note on pp. 14–15 with Addendum 1: Steiner distinguishes his formative organization from an older vital-force explanation that changes inorganic laws.
- Soul body / sentient body and sentient soul are related but introduced as distinct. They should not be collapsed before their relationship is explained.

**2. Differentiations of soul, pp. 20–26**

- Sentient soul concerns sensation, desire, and impulse.
- Intellectual or mind soul brings thinking into the soul's life, including thinking in the service of desires.
- Consciousness soul concerns the soul's relation to truth and goodness beyond personal preference. It does not mean ordinary wakefulness or consciousness in every everyday sense.
- The distinctions describe interacting aspects, not labels for sorting classmates into superior and inferior kinds of people.

**3. The I and spiritual development, pp. 26–35**

- The I unifies experience and is described as acting through body and soul while receiving spiritual content.
- Self-reference, self-awareness, and selfishness are different concepts. The I is not simply egotism.
- Consciousness soul and spirit self are distinguished: openness to truth versus its individual appropriation in the I, in the chapter's account.
- Intuition has a technical meaning tied to spiritual content, not a casual hunch. Addendum 6 qualifies its use here.
- Spirit self, life spirit, and spirit man introduce a further spiritual differentiation. Explain their role before asking learners to recall names.

**4. Bringing the classifications together, pp. 35–39**

- Steiner gives different groupings of the same human constitution, combining closely related members for particular purposes.
- The ninefold account distinguishes physical body, life body, soul body, sentient soul, intellectual soul, consciousness soul, spirit self, life spirit, and spirit man. Its order and A–I labels are corroborated by the comparison edition; this is not a verified quotation of the 1971 scan.
- The text then combines soul body with sentient soul and consciousness soul with spirit self. The complete intermediate list recovered from the comparison is: physical body; ether or life body; sentient-soul-body; intellectual soul; spirit-filled consciousness soul; life spirit; spirit man. Attribute the recovery to the comparison edition and distinguish it from the subsequent sevenfold transformation list.
- The simplified account uses physical body, life body, astral body, and I; pp. 38–39 relate spiritual transformation to astral, life, and physical constitution.
- These are intended by Steiner as real distinctions perceived spiritually, not merely a convenient arbitrary taxonomy. Our diagram is a teaching model of his account, not evidence for it.

**Teach with:** a progressive concept map whose nodes are added across lessons. Use connecting arrows, not an anatomical cross-section pretending to locate invisible organs.

## 5. Chapter II — Re-embodiment of the Spirit and Destiny

**Central question:** What persists through experience, and why does Steiner extend his account beyond a single lifetime?

**Chapter outcome:** the learner can reconstruct the argument for repeated lives and karma, distinguish them, and explain the limits identified in Addendum 7.

### Key takeaways

1. **Memory and action connect different times.** Memory brings a past experience into present life; an action changes the world and creates continuing consequences (pp. 40–44).
2. **Remembering is not described as retrieving an unchanged picture.** Steiner describes a new present visualization arising through the effects of an earlier encounter (pp. 44–46).
3. **Remembered episodes and acquired capacities differ.** We can retain an ability without recalling each episode through which it developed. The reading-and-writing example anchors this distinction (pp. 46–47).
4. **The argument then changes scope.** Steiner extends from learning during one life to claims about dispositions carried from earlier lives. His appeal to individuality, biography, and heredity supplies the bridge (pp. 47–60). Make the added premises visible rather than treating this as a direct observation.
5. **Re-embodiment and karma do different work.** Re-embodiment concerns the recurring spiritual individuality; karma concerns the connections of deeds and destiny. His closing arrangement relates body to heredity, soul to destiny, and spirit to repeated lives (pp. 60–69).
6. **Read the qualification alongside the argument.** Addendum 7, especially p. 188, calls the reasoning a preparatory exercise giving a silhouette rather than a proof. The assertive language of the chapter and this qualification should both be taught, not smoothed into one another.

### Teaching method

Draw a two-part map. First: an ordinary experience, later recollection, acquired skill, and an action's continuing effect. Second: the additional claims required to extend this pattern across death and rebirth. Use a fictional biography so no learner has to expose private history.

**Important distinction:** an analogy may help a reader imagine the claim without establishing that the two processes share the same cause. The sleep/death analogy illustrates continuity; sleep is not an experiment demonstrating reincarnation.

Yes; the book distinguishes the fruits of experience from its remembered images. |
| 8. Individuality and repeated lives / Individualidade e vidas sucessivas | pp. 47–60; Addendum 7 | Map premises, analogies, and conclusion | Does unique biography by itself demonstrate a previous life? No; explain Steiner's additional argument and the addendum's qualification. |

One concerns the connection of deeds and destiny; the other the spiritual individuality's repeated earthly lives. |

**Likely misunderstandings:** karma as a simple reward scoreboard; reincarnation as unchanged personality returning; ability as proof of a particular past life; destiny as a reason to do nothing now.

**Teaching boundary:** the book makes strong claims about self-created destiny. Accurately explain those claims without attributing a real person's illness, abuse, poverty, or other suffering to supposed past deeds. No task asks students to infer anyone's past lives or deserved fate. Historical remarks about heredity and animals must not be passed off as a current genetics or animal-cognition curriculum.

**Chapter checkpoint:** a learner should be able to say both “Here is why Steiner argues this” and “Here is what the assigned argument does and does not establish.”

## 6. Chapter III — The Three Worlds

**Central question:** How does Steiner describe the physical, soul, and spiritual worlds and their relationship to human life?

**Chapter outcome:** the learner can explain the role of each world, trace the proposed after-death sequence, and interpret descriptive imagery without confusing it with physical geography.

This is the largest chapter. Its six subsections deserve separate attention. Students need a conceptual map before any detailed list of regions.

### III.1 — The Soul World, pp. 70–86

**Key takeaways**

- The worlds interpenetrate: they are not planets or locations outside the physical universe (especially p. 74).
- Spiritual organs, substances, space, and motion are described with words borrowed from physical experience. The text expressly qualifies that borrowing.
- Sympathy and antipathy are technical relational forces: attraction/union and repulsion/separation, not simply kindness and cruelty.
- The seven regions differentiate patterns of these forces. Grasp the changing relationships before memorizing their names.

**Reference map:** regions 1–3 concern differing proportions of attraction and separation; region 4 concerns the soul's inward liking/disliking; regions 5–7 describe more outward, enlivening activity. In the terminology of this edition:

| Region | Name | Main reading distinction |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Burning Desire | Attraction is constrained by predominant repulsion and self-seeking. |
| 2 | Mobile Sensitivity | The two forces balance; impressions are received with relative neutrality. |
| 3 | Wishes | Attraction predominates but still draws things into its own sphere. |
| 4 | Liking and Disliking | Sympathy acts within the formation; inner feeling comes to the foreground. |
| 5 | Soul Light | Receptivity to other beings becomes more outward and less self-enclosed. |
| 6 | Active Soul Force | The account develops the outward enlivening activity further. |
| 7 | Soul Life | Completes the description of the higher regions' enlivening participation. |

Do not invent detailed independent mechanisms for regions 6 and 7: their initial treatment is brief. The next subsection shows their role in the purification narrative.

**Activity:** compare attraction that wants to possess with interest in something for its own sake. Label the comparison as an analogy to the text, not a spiritual observation.

**Check:** Are these seven regions stacked above one another? No; pp. 85–86 explicitly describe interpenetration.

### III.2 — The Soul in the Soul World after Death, pp. 87–101

**Key takeaways**

- The soul is the intermediary between body and spirit. Its attachments can remain, in this account, when bodily means of gratification are no longer available.
- Purification describes the release of attachments to the completed bodily life so that the spirit can enter its own sphere.
- The narrative follows affinities with the regions; it is not a uniform timetable assigning everyone the same experience or duration.
- Read the distinction on p. 91: attachments tied to the just-completed incarnation are treated differently from connections addressed by karma through a later incarnation.
- Distinguish dissolution of bodily attachments from loss of the fruits that the spirit takes onward.

**Activity:** annotate a diagram of the proposed sequence: embodied life → release from body → release of bodily attachments → spiritual assimilation → renewed embodiment. Every stage beyond physical death is labelled as Steiner's account.

**Check:** What is supposed to disappear, and what is supposed to continue? Bodily-life attachments are relinquished; assimilable fruits of experience can remain with the spirit. This is more precise than saying “the whole person disappears” or “nothing changes.”

**Sensitive source passage:** p. 97 makes a specific claim about the postmortem suffering of people who die by suicide. Do not repeat it as a verified outcome, a threat, or a judgment on a deceased person. If included, discuss it explicitly as a claim in the historical text; do not build a quiz or role-play asking students to assign such suffering to anyone. The course is textual study, not bereavement advice.

### III.3 — The Spiritland, pp. 101–110

**Key takeaways**

- Archetypes are described as living creative beings, not merely abstract categories or static blueprints invented by humans.
- The book distinguishes ordinary thought from the fuller spiritual reality it says thought reflects.
- Continents, flowing life, atmosphere, colours, and music describe claimed spiritual experiences through comparisons. The claims are intended as real, even though the descriptions are not literal physical geography or acoustics.
- Regions 1–3 concern archetypes of physical forms, life, and soul formations; region 4 their broader coordination; regions 5–7 the sources of creative impulses and purposes.
- Addendum 9 distinguishes creative mobility from restless agitation. Addendum 10 warns against importing narrowly human meanings into spiritual purposes.

**Activity:** mark in a paragraph what is being compared and what the comparison helps explain. Then state what would go wrong if taken physically.

**Check:** Is the archetype simply a mental sketch in a human artist's head? No. The artist is an analogy; Steiner proposes a creative reality not produced by that human mind.

### III.4 — The Spirit in Spiritland after Death, pp. 110–127

**Key takeaways**

- Earthly life supplies experience and a field of action; the interval between lives is described as transforming its fruits into capacities and intentions.
- The architect illustration connects doing, learning, and preparing future activity. It is an explanatory analogy.
- The lower regions' descriptions connect family and friendship, reverence and wider belonging, and service to others with future capacities.
- The fourth region brings human cultural creativity into focus; the higher regions concern the enduring self and purposes.
- The book explicitly allows a subsequent life to be less perfect than an earlier one (pp. 113–114). Avoid promising a simple upward staircase from life to life.
- Ordinary acts can matter; spiritual significance is not equated with fame (p. 123).
- Addendum 12 clarifies the claimed difference between earthly wishes and a pre-birth will-like orientation toward difficult experience. This is a substantial metaphysical claim, not permission to explain another person's suffering.

**Activity:** build a two-column map, “earthly experiences” and “capacities the text says may be developed from them.” Use impersonal examples of friendship, learning, and service.

**Check:** Does the book say that each successive life must be visibly more successful? No; it qualifies the general developmental picture and does not identify spiritual value with public success.

### III.5 — The Physical World and Its Connection with the Soul and Spiritland, pp. 127–140

**Key takeaways**

- This section reconnects the previous descriptions with ordinary earthly existence: in Steiner's view, humans participate in the three worlds simultaneously.
- The ice/water comparison describes sensible things as a manifestation of soul-spiritual reality. Do not turn it into a physical phase-change claim about spirits.
- Mineral form, living formation, sentience, and human thought organize his account of the kingdoms.
- The three elementary kingdoms refer to proposed formative activity; they are not three additional ordinary biological categories.
- The later discussion extends the account to national/folk spirits, the spirit of an age, and elemental beings. These are spiritual beings in his account, not just poetic nicknames for observable social trends.
- The same passage insists that descriptions of elemental beings should not be treated as bodily creatures visible to ordinary senses.

**Activity:** follow one example—plant growth—through the observation, Steiner's proposed formative account, and the analogy used to express it. For the social passage, distinguish an observed shared custom from a claim about its spiritual agency.

**Check:** Does a shared custom establish a national spirit? No. The observable pattern and Steiner's proposed explanation are different levels of claim. Do not use this section to assign a fixed character or spiritual rank to individuals by nationality or ethnicity.

### III.6 — Thought Forms and the Human Aura, pp. 140–153

**Key takeaways**

- Steiner describes thoughts and feelings as having spiritually perceptible manifestations or effects.
- Read pp. 143–144 before discussing the colour descriptions: these are not ordinary colours appearing to physical sight.
- The three interpenetrating auras express bodily influence, soul life, and spiritual activity. They are not three separate coloured shells.
- Their meaning depends on context; the text does not support reducing the chapter to a simple personality colour chart.
- The closing paragraph rejects a questionable practice of reading character from hallucinatory auras.
- Addendum 13 explains why Steiner does not accept ordinary experimental demands as governing such perception. Teach this as a methodological position, not as experimental corroboration or a way to dismiss all questions.

**Activity:** analyse the language of two descriptions. Students may draw a labelled conceptual illustration, but must not diagnose, classify, or claim to read a classmate's aura.

**Check:** Would seeing an outline of colour around a person establish the experience described here? No; the text expressly distinguishes its account from ordinary visual colour and hallucinatory character reading.

## 7. Chapter IV — The Path of Knowledge

**Central question:** What changes in thinking, feeling, and conduct does Steiner say make spiritual knowledge possible?

**Chapter outcome:** the learner can explain the proposed path and distinguish attentive study from credulity, emotional numbness, fantasy, or withdrawal from everyday responsibility.

### Key takeaways

- **Thinking is the starting point** (pp. 154–159). The account does not present sensation-seeking or vague images as a substitute for disciplined thought.
- **Receptivity is not the abandonment of judgment** (pp. 159–161 and footnotes). Hear or observe before applying a habitual verdict; then examine carefully.
- **Feelings are to be transformed in their role, not erased** (pp. 162–165). The aim described is finer responsiveness, less automatic personal reaction.
- **Thought should have continuity and reasons** (pp. 165–168). Mathematics is offered as a possible discipline, not a requirement for every learner.
- **Conduct and motive matter** (pp. 168–171). The proposed path includes ethical action rather than intellectual accumulation alone.
- **Ordinary life remains important** (pp. 172–178). The end of the chapter rejects making a temporary research disposition into permanent withdrawal from living.
- **Freedom is distinguished from arbitrary self-will** (pp. 174–175), and spiritual union is distinguished from erasure of individuality (p. 177).
- The promises of spiritual perception and beneficial effects remain the author's claims. Course completion does not certify spiritual attainment or guarantee experiences.

| Lesson | Reading | Task | Check and expected response |

| 18. Thoughtful receptivity / Receptividade e pensamento | pp. 154–161 | Explain a short passage fairly before evaluating it | Is open-mindedness blind belief? No; the footnotes distinguish receiving an impression from substituting premature judgment, without requiring blind faith. |
| 19. Feeling without automatic reaction / Sentir sem reagir automaticamente | pp. 161–165 | Examine a fictional mild disagreement: feeling, interpretation, possible response | Does equanimity mean feeling nothing? No; Steiner describes transformed and finer feeling rather than numbness. |
| 20. Coherent thinking and responsible action / Pensamento coerente e ação responsável | pp. 165–171 | Write a short chain of reasons and identify where a preference interrupts it; consider one ordinary act of responsibility | Is mathematics mandatory? No; the text allows other routes to disciplined thinking. |
| 21. Freedom, individuality, and everyday life / Liberdade, individualidade e vida cotidiana | pp. 171–178 | Compare self-will, action from principle, and loss of individuality | Does the conclusion demand withdrawal from ordinary life? No; the final qualification says the special research attitude is not a general demand over all life. |

**Practical adaptation:** use brief, optional observation, attentive listening, and reflective writing. Label these as classroom adaptations; they are not a complete initiation method. A student may choose a fictional example instead of personal disclosure. Do not score spiritual experiences, prescribe strenuous practices, or interpret distress as evidence of advancement.

**Chapter checkpoint:** write a realistic study routine and explain its relation to the assigned chapter. Include how questions will be examined and how ordinary responsibilities will remain intact. Assessment concerns the explanation, not private religious practice.

## 9. Make understanding visible

No course can guarantee understanding by presenting material alone. Build opportunities to explain, receive feedback, correct, and return to earlier concepts.

### Repeatable lesson pattern

1. **Retrieve:** ask one question about the previous lesson before showing the answer.
2. **Read:** assign a short passage within the unit's page range, with no more than a few new technical terms at a time.
3. **Explain:** ask for a two-sentence paraphrase before supplying the teaching explanation.
4. **Distinguish:** use one example and one misleading example.
5. **Examine the argument:** identify an observation, inference, analogy, or supersensible claim.
6. **Apply:** transfer the distinction to a fresh example.
7. **Exit check:** one explained answer and one unresolved question; provide a precise passage to reread if needed.

This is our proposed teaching method, not a verbatim instruction from Steiner.

### Assessment rubric

Score each dimension 0–2 for feedback, not for measuring spiritual development.

| Dimension | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Accurate meaning | Major confusion | Partly accurate | Accurate explanation in the student's own words |
| Distinctions | Collapses key terms | Distinguishes with prompts | Distinguishes independently and identifies an exception or boundary |
| Source use | No support or wrong passage | Relevant but vague support | Correct passage and clear explanation of its relevance |
| Claim awareness and transfer | Treats every statement as the same kind of evidence | Notices a distinction | Identifies the kind of claim and applies it to a new example |

Suggested readiness rule: at least 6/8, with no zero for accurate meaning or key distinctions. This threshold is a course-design proposal, not a validated test. Offer rereading and another example rather than locking a learner out. Multiple-choice recognition alone is insufficient; include one short explanation at every checkpoint.

### Useful misconception questions and feedback

- **“The soul means feelings and nothing else.”** Return to pp. 8–9: sensation and willing are also part of the discussion.
- **“If I forget the lesson, I cannot retain anything from it.”** Return to p. 47: distinguish episodic recall from an acquired capacity.
- **“A convincing analogy proves reincarnation.”** Return to Addendum 7: identify its preparatory rather than demonstrative role.
- **“Seven regions means seven physical places.”** Return to p. 86 and p. 119: interpenetration and changes of capacity matter.
- **“Aura colour is a visible personality test.”** Return to pp. 143–144 and 153, and Addendum 13.
- **“To study correctly I must stop questioning.”** Return to the footnotes on pp. 160–161 and distinguish attentive reception from blind faith.
- **“Spiritual development erases individuality.”** Return to p. 177: the conclusion explicitly distinguishes union from annihilation.

### Lesson 22 — Final synthesis / Síntese final

Ask students for a concept map and a short written or spoken explanation answering four questions:

1. What does Chapter I distinguish within the human being?
2. How does Chapter II connect experience, individuality, and destiny?
3. What different roles do the worlds and postmortem processes play in Chapter III?
4. Why does Chapter IV return to thought, feeling, conduct, and ordinary life?

Require one example of an analogy with its limits, one addendum that changes or qualifies a first reading, and one question the learner still considers unresolved. A well-supported disagreement can receive full marks.

## 10. First lesson: a concrete starting draft plan

**Reading:** printed pp. 1–5 (scan pages 29–33). The opening closes just before subsection 1 on printed p. 6. The short transition at the top of p. 6 can be included when preparing the final reading extract.

**English working title:** Encountering the world: body, soul, and spirit.

**Portuguese working title:** Nosso encontro com o mundo: corpo, alma e espírito.

**By the end, a learner should be able to:**

- distinguish an observed feature, a personal response, and a reasoned claim about the object;
- explain the three introductory terms through Steiner's flower example;
- say why the observation does not establish all later claims in the book.

**30-minute session:**

- 0–3: recall Lesson 0 and introduce the source and the question of how we encounter the world.
- 3–7: observe a flower or leaf, or use a described object if visual observation is not accessible. Record features separately from feelings and interpretations.
- 7–15: guided reading of selected parts of pp. 1–5, including the returning-to-the-meadow example and the definitions on p. 4. Allow rereading rather than covering every sentence at speed.
- 15–22: compare two people's responses and introduce the three terms with a simple relationship map.
- 22–27: apply the distinction to a different object or sound. Ask for a reason that another person could examine, not just a personal judgment.
- 27–30: exit questions and feedback.

**Exit questions with answer guidance:**

1. Is “this flower makes me happy” the same kind of statement as “this flower has five petals”? No: one reports personal response; the other an observed feature, subject to checking.
2. Is asking why the plant grows already knowledge of its growth? No: it is a question; developing and examining an explanation is the next step.
3. In this passage, why is spirit not just another word for emotion? Steiner uses it for the relation to truth or intelligible order beyond private feeling.
4. Does noticing these distinctions demonstrate a separate spiritual world? No. The activity helps interpret the starting distinction; later claims need their own study.

**Take-home task:** bring three notes about a new encounter and one question about the reading. No spiritual experience is expected.
