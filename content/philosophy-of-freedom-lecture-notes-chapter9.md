## Essential conceptual sequence

1. **Cognition and action have different tasks.** PDF 94 begins with a concept appropriate to an existing tree; PDF 101 distinguishes the cognitive concept of a situation from the moral idea of a response. Understanding what is present does not automatically supply what ought to be done. The lecture's invention comparison helps visualize an idea directing a deed, provided originality is not made the test.
2. **Motive differs from driving force.** PDF 96–97 calls the conceptual aim the motive and distinguishes the individual's force making it effective. The lecture's coffee offer makes the distinction easy to observe hypothetically. Relatively lasting representations and feelings form characterological disposition; the same proposed aim need not move everyone alike.
3. **Keep feeling, represented pleasure and practical experience distinct.** PDF 97–99 separates feeling as a force from imagined future pleasure as a motive. Learned action patterns can become tact. Skill explains aspects of enactment but cannot establish a deed's moral freedom.
4. **Inner authority still needs examination.** PDF 99 moves from external or internal command to understanding a maxim's reason. A remembered parent's voice is a helpful lecture example. Merely relocating authority inside the person does not complete that advance; accepting advice after understanding its reason is compatible with authorship.
5. **Pure thinking supplies the highest proposed ground.** PDF 98 and 100–102 make the ideal content of intuition effective, with motive and driving force coinciding. Its content may be familiar or newly apprehended. A chosen outcome, feeling of certainty, apparent goodness, originality or enthusiasm alone does not meet the criterion.
6. **Ethical individuality includes moral context and development.** PDF 102–104 asks about a loving intuition's place in the wider intuitively understood world and distinguishes blind criminal drives from that intuition. It grants norms a developmental role and recognizes mixed determinants. The question concerns the particular deed, not assigning a permanent label to a person.
7. **Shared ideas support individual contributions.** PDF 104–105 argues that people receive differing intuitions from one world of ideas. Steiner's stronger harmony claim depends on genuinely intuitive action. The practical exercise is to understand another's aim faithfully, then evaluate proposals and constraints, without demanding assent or using disagreement to diagnose unfreedom.
8. **Freedom develops among other people.** PDF 106–108 contrasts a plant's given development with the person's active realization of the free self. Nature and society prepare conditions; social life can support the individual. The lecture's ending makes this reciprocal relation accessible.

## Course additions

The separate bilingual guide for lesson 12 clarifies motive and driving force with the coffee example, internal authority with the remembered parental command, and cognition versus a deed guided by an idea. It explicitly keeps novelty, skill, feeling and outcome distinct from the book's freedom criterion. The original worked example compares possible reasons behind preparing an accessible reading handout without asserting knowledge of an actual person's inner life.

The lesson 13 guide uses the lecture's conflicting pictures of welfare to lead into the book's shared world of ideas and social maxim. Its original audio-versus-written-guide example asks each person to explain the other's aim before comparing plans. It retains room for disagreement, moral evaluation and practical constraints. Cooperation can support individuality; no instant agreement is required.
