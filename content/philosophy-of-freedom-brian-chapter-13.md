## Corrections and missing steps

**Hartmann must be represented fairly.** At Basis 110 and 115, Steiner presents a system demanding continued participation in a world process, explicitly explaining why individual suicide hinders its proposed purpose. This directly contradicts the transcript's claim that Hartmann tells each person to kill themselves. The distinction is also supported by the introduction and primary-text selections in the University of Utah's [Ethics of Suicide Digital Archive](https://ethicsofsuicide.lib.utah.edu/selections/eduard-von-hartmann/). That archive distinguishes rejection of individual suicide from Hartmann's collective metaphysical endpoint. This is historical exposition, not endorsement of either judgment.

The checked [Deutsche Biographie entry](https://www.deutsche-biographie.de/sfz39337.html) gives his death as **5 June 1906**, not 1912. Neither this date nor the consulted archive establishes the lecture's suicide claim or its story that Steiner deliberately kept him alive through argument. Leave those assertions out rather than invent a replacement cause of death. Alleged followers, terrorist connections and adverse spiritual forces are likewise unsupported by the supplied evidence.

**Striving is not the whole explanation of pain.** The lecture's useful point that trying can be enjoyed needs the qualification at Basis 111: illness and unexpected pleasure need not arise from prior desire. Avoid teaching that all suffering is merely an unsatisfied wish. The book itself also makes broad psychological claims around 114 and 119; these are not used as clinical explanations or classroom diagnoses.

**Felt pleasure and mistaken belief differ.** Restore 112–114. A later discovery that praise was mistaken does not erase earlier pleasure. Correcting a distorted memory is a different operation. Experiential occurrence, assessment of its magnitude, and moral approval of its cause must not be conflated.

**Do not replace the book with “calculation is impossible.”** Basis 120–121 grants comparison in principle and recognizes cases where alternatives are interchangeable. Its central objection is to deriving the direction or worth of willing from an abstract pleasure surplus alone.

**Keep two ratios distinct.** Basis 117 illustrates pleasure experienced relative to the needs in question; Basis 123 discusses achievement relative to aims in its ethical conclusion. The lecture's number-of-books example belongs with the latter. Neither licenses productivity scores for human worth. Basis 117–118 also qualifies excess pleasure by the changing desire; a larger portion of food is not automatically more satisfaction.

**Restore particularity, ethical content and the addition.** Basis 118–121 explains why one particular satisfaction cannot always be substituted with an equal amount of a different pleasure. Basis 122–124 reconnects this account to moral imagination and intuitive thinking. Intensity of desire and endurance alone do not establish freedom or goodness. Pleasure in fulfilling an ethical ideal does not automatically make the deed selfish.
