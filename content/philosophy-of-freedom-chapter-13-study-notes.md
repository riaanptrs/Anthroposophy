## The positions Steiner presents

| Position in the opening | Representatives | Account as presented in the chapter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Optimism | Shaftesbury and Leibniz; Leibniz's argument is developed here | The world is the best possible. A good and wise God would create that world; human action should cooperate with its order. The opening's account interprets apparent evil within this position. |
| Schopenhauer's pessimism | Arthur Schopenhauer | Blind willing continually renews unsatisfied craving; satisfaction is brief and its absence brings suffering or boredom. The proposed response is to quiet or negate willing. |
| Hartmann's pessimism | Eduard von Hartmann | Pain is held to exceed pleasure, but will is joined with wisdom and a metaphysical purpose. Humans should actively contribute to a world process understood as the redemption of a suffering primal being. |

These are Steiner's presentations of the positions, not a declaration that he endorses the best possible world, that evil is unreal, or that people should simply discover externally assigned divine tasks. Nor are optimism and pessimism used here as personality diagnoses. The spelling **Leibnitz** appears in the uploaded translation; **Leibniz** is used in these notes.

## Corrected English summary

Chapter 13 begins with optimism and pessimism, then investigates whether their assessment of pleasure and pain determines the value or direction of human willing. Steiner distinguishes Schopenhauer's proposed withdrawal from willing from Hartmann's demand for active participation in a metaphysical world task. Merely grouping them as pessimists obscures their different ethical conclusions.

Steiner challenges the claim that striving is itself pain. Hope for a wanted result can bring enjoyment during effort; fulfillment adds another enjoyment. Failure can bring disappointment without retrospectively erasing all enjoyment in trying. He also recognizes pleasure and pain unrelated to a preceding desire, using an unexpected inheritance and illness as examples. Thus neither all pleasure nor all suffering can be explained by fulfillment or frustration alone.

The chapter then examines how a pleasure/pain account could be made. Distorted recollection of an experience's intensity differs from discovering that its object rested on an illusion. Pleasure actually felt remains an experienced fact even if the belief or object is later reassessed. That does not make the belief true or the enjoyment ethically justified. Steiner allows approximate comparison of pleasures and pains and pleasure-based choices among genuinely interchangeable alternatives. His objection concerns treating an abstract surplus as the sole explanation of every concrete act of willing. Note 3 on capture 162 stresses actual experience over an imagined total balance.

A particular satisfaction has value relative to the desire it meets. Partial fulfillment can still have value, while unmet needs remain real; an excess can become displeasure when it exceeds what is wanted. A walk does not satisfy hunger, and watching a film does not fulfill the aim of repairing a friend's book. Steiner therefore asks how strongly a particular result is wanted in relation to the hindrances involved. Difficulties can weaken or overwhelm desire. He also argues that hard-won enjoyment can receive a higher relative value, but this is not a rule that obstacles always improve a goal or that suffering is necessary for value.

The positive ethical account concerns a will sustained by ideal intuitions. An ethical aim can be understood and wanted for its own content; enjoyment in realizing it does not automatically make the deed selfish. Equally, strong desire, persistence or success alone does not establish goodness or freedom. The closing passage measures achievements against personally apprehended ethical aims, and the 1918 addition insists that free willing must be sustained by intuitive thinking. This connects the chapter to moral intuition, imagination and technique rather than making an optimistic mood its conclusion.

## Resumo corrigido em português

O capítulo 13 começa com otimismo e pessimismo e depois investiga se suas avaliações de prazer e dor determinam o valor ou a direção do querer humano. Steiner distingue a renúncia ao querer proposta por Schopenhauer da exigência de Hartmann de participar ativamente de uma tarefa metafísica do mundo. Agrupá-los apenas como pessimistas oculta suas diferentes conclusões éticas.

Steiner questiona a afirmação de que o próprio empenho seja dor. A esperança de um resultado desejado pode proporcionar satisfação durante o esforço; a realização acrescenta outra satisfação. O fracasso pode trazer decepção sem apagar retrospectivamente toda a satisfação de tentar. Ele também reconhece prazer e dor sem um desejo anterior, usando como exemplos uma herança inesperada e a doença. Portanto, nem todo prazer nem todo sofrimento se explica apenas pela realização ou frustração de desejos.

O capítulo examina então como avaliar prazer e dor. A lembrança distorcida da intensidade de uma experiência difere da descoberta de que seu objeto se baseava numa ilusão. O prazer efetivamente sentido continua sendo um fato vivenciado, mesmo se a crença ou o objeto for reavaliado depois. Isso não torna verdadeira a crença nem eticamente justificado o prazer. Steiner admite comparações aproximadas entre prazeres e dores e escolhas baseadas em prazer entre alternativas efetivamente intercambiáveis. Sua objeção é tratar um saldo abstrato como explicação exclusiva de todo ato concreto de querer. A nota 3 da captura 162 enfatiza a experiência real em vez de um balanço total imaginado.

Uma satisfação particular tem valor em relação ao desejo que atende. Uma realização parcial ainda pode ter valor, enquanto as necessidades não atendidas continuam reais; um excesso pode tornar-se desprazer quando ultrapassa o desejado. Uma caminhada não satisfaz a fome, e assistir a um filme não realiza o objetivo de reparar o livro de um amigo. Steiner pergunta, portanto, pela intensidade com que um resultado particular é desejado em relação aos obstáculos envolvidos. As dificuldades podem enfraquecer ou superar o desejo. Ele também argumenta que uma satisfação conquistada com esforço pode receber um valor relativo maior, mas isso não é uma regra segundo a qual os obstáculos sempre melhoram um objetivo ou o sofrimento é necessário ao valor.

A explicação ética positiva trata de uma vontade sustentada por intuições ideais. Um objetivo ético pode ser compreendido e desejado por seu próprio conteúdo; a satisfação em realizá-lo não torna automaticamente egoísta a ação. Da mesma forma, desejo intenso, persistência ou sucesso não estabelece, sozinho, bondade ou liberdade. O encerramento mede as realizações em relação a objetivos éticos apreendidos pessoalmente, e o adendo de 1918 insiste que o querer livre precisa ser sustentado pelo pensar intuitivo. Isso relaciona o capítulo à intuição, imaginação e técnica moral, em vez de fazer de uma disposição otimista sua conclusão.

## Corrections to the user's notes

| Supplied statement or emphasis | More accurate formulation |
| --- | --- |
| Evil is not genuinely real; it is a lesser degree of good | The opening reports this within its exposition of optimism. It is not presented as Steiner's final resolution of suffering or evil. |
| Human action should follow God's counsels | This is the consequence drawn from the Leibniz position being examined. Keep it distinct from the book's ethical individualism and critique of externally fixed purposes. |
| Schopenhauer and Hartmann both say non-existence is preferable | Distinguish their proposed responses: withdrawal from willing versus active participation in a world process. Hartmann is not represented by the book as recommending individual suicide; capture 134 explicitly says that it would hinder his proposed task. |
| Striving is hopeful and pleasurable, but unfulfilled striving is not pleasurable | Separate enjoyment during striving, fulfillment and disappointment. Failure does not necessarily erase the earlier enjoyment. Not all pain originates in an unfulfilled desire. |
| Obstacles make us value our goals | Steiner's claim about hard-won satisfaction is contextual. Obstacles may weaken or overwhelm desire; more difficulty is not automatically more value. |
| People are filled with ideas, so those ideas sustain action | The ethical conclusion concerns ideal content apprehended and made effective through intuitive thinking. Any idea, intense wish or persistent effort does not thereby meet the criterion. |
| Value is the surplus of pleasure over pain | The chapter examines this proposed measure, then explains why particular desires and ethical aims cannot all be replaced by an abstract pleasure surplus. |

## Two comparisons to keep distinct

| Comparison | Source | What it explains |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pleasure actually experienced relative to the needs in question | Captures 135–137; explicit fraction on 136 | Partial satisfaction retains value, but the same amount meets a smaller part of a larger demand. The fraction is a philosophical illustration, not a validated score of life's worth. |
| Achievements measured against wanted ethical aims | Capture 142 | The closing ethical account appraises realization in relation to personally apprehended aims. A count of completed projects is not a measure of human dignity or a proof of freedom. |

The lecture's number-of-books example belongs with the second comparison. It should not replace the hunger-and-satisfaction discussion or collapse the two measures into one formula.
