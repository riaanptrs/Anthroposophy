Part 3 of IV · Synthesis

Training Thought · synthesis

Key ideas

  • Choosing a subject deliberately gives ordinary thinking a direction beyond its habitual concerns.
  • Steiner's memory exercise completes gaps through imagination; remembered and invented details have different origins.
  • Practical judgment begins by picturing more than one possible course of action.

How they connect

A chosen subject holds attention together. A detailed mental picture gives that attention something specific to work on. Comparing possible actions then directs these capacities toward a decision. The next part completes the movement from prepared alternatives through a pause to a reasoned conclusion.

One deeper exercise · course application

Course application: choose one small improvement to a familiar space, such as making workshop tools easier to find. Spend a short, deliberately chosen period recalling its present arrangement. Make a complete sketch, labelling details remembered, unknown or imagined, then check it against the space. Picture two possible changes and what each would involve. Keep both possibilities open for the pause introduced in Part IV.

Optional model

I chose to think about the workshop shelf instead of the unfinished job. I remembered the scissors but imagined their position; checking showed they were on the other side. I pictured keeping the single sign or adding shelf labels. The first requires less work; the second may make each tool easier to locate. I have prepared two actions and have not yet chosen between them.
