Further study and retained applications

Habit or a reason? · additional examples

GA 108 · §§ 2, 7–8

A clever plan still needs contact with the situation

A plan can sound reasonable because its parts fit your assumptions. Practical thinking also asks whether those assumptions fit the actual circumstances. In Steiner’s railway-truck comparison, effort within the imagined arrangement is mistaken for a means of propelling the whole arrangement. The teaching point is to inspect what your explanation leaves out.

Course example: “If I print the instructions in larger letters, everyone will understand.” Larger letters may help someone who cannot read the sign at its present size. They will not explain an unfamiliar word. Ask what difficulty the visitor actually has before deciding which change addresses it.

An attitude of inquiry · additional examples

GA 108 · §§ 9–13; compare §§ 46–48

Confidence in understanding is different from confidence in your first answer

Steiner’s spiritual premise is that the world contains relationships that thought can discover. It does not follow that whatever first occurs to you must express those relationships. You still have to observe, compare and revise. His call for confidence can therefore support patient inquiry without settling the answer in advance.

Course example: “There must be a reason this door keeps sticking” leaves the cause open. “The cause must be the one I already imagined” closes the inquiry prematurely. The first attitude motivates investigation; the second substitutes certainty for it.

A guided example: from seeing clouds to understanding

Work through one situation before starting the independent exercise. These steps are a teaching adaptation. They connect two readings without claiming that Steiner identifies a percept with the astral body. You may use the imagined scene below or observe clouds from a comfortable place.

Observe: what is a percept?

Imagine a broad grey cloud with a bright edge. You watch its outline change. A percept is the content given in observation: here, the particular colours, shapes and changes you see. Percept names what is perceived; perception names the activity of perceiving. We begin with a visual example, although Steiner’s use of percept is broader than sight.

Remember: what is a mental picture?

Look away and recall this particular cloud. That remembered image is a mental picture. Compare it with the scene when you look back: did you remember the bright edge or invent a darker centre? If the cloud has changed meanwhile, that change limits the comparison. In The Philosophy of Freedom, Steiner explains a mental picture as a concept retaining its connection to a particular percept. It is more than a photograph stored inside the head.

Understand: what does a concept contribute?

A concept is an intelligible connection grasped through thinking. For example, understanding change involves relating an earlier condition to a later one. Two observations of the cloud give you something to compare. Thinking connects them as a change in outline. Explaining why that change happened requires more than naming it: an attractive explanation about wind is still a hypothesis until adequately supported.

Read Steiner’s spiritual interpretation

In GA 108 § 16 Steiner refers to invisible vehicles or members of the human being. In § 17 he explicitly names the astral body as a bearer of thought and mental imagery. Vehicle here means a bearer or means of activity. In § 18 he claims that faithfully forming images of events, while holding back arbitrary speculation, lets the thoughts active in the universe impress themselves upon the astral body. This is his spiritual explanation of the exercise; an accurate memory alone does not establish it.

Connect the two readings

The Philosophy of Freedom, chapter VI §§ 4–6, distinguishes percept, concept and mental picture to explain knowing and individual experience. Practical Training in Thought, §§ 14–18, gives an observation exercise and its spiritual interpretation. Our teaching comparison is that accurate observation and faithful recollection provide material for understanding. The chapter in The Philosophy of Freedom does not establish the lecture’s claim about the astral body merely by distinguishing those three terms.

Try a short complete explanation

Before practising independently, complete these sentences aloud or in your notebook: “What I observed was …”; “What I remembered was …”; “The connection I understood was …”; “What I still do not know is …”. Then explain in a separate sentence what Steiner claims about the astral body. You do not need to report a spiritual experience.
