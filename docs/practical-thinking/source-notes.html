Sources and further study

Primary witness

Rudolf Steiner · Practical Training In Thought · GA 108 · 18 January 1909, Carlsruhe as printed by this witness · George (Kaufmann) Adams.

English excerpts reproduce the Adams wording. Portuguese excerpts are new course study translations. Explanations, part titles, modern cases, checks and Try it practices are original course material. Actual source exercises are identified separately. The four parts and twelve lessons organize this one continuous lecture; they are not Steiner’s original section divisions.

Historical stories and scientific models illustrate Steiner’s argument; they are not independent historical verification or present-day scientific conclusions. His spiritual explanations remain attributed claims. Trying an exercise does not establish those claims.

¶1–2 · Everyday practicality versus inherited habits

Anthroposophy is presented as guidance for ordinary life. Supposedly practical people often merely follow inherited opinions and professional habits.

Establish that practical thinking means meeting actual circumstances, not conforming to a trade’s familiar outlook.

Discriminating genuine thought from unexamined habit → Examples show familiar expertise resisting real changes.

¶3–6 · Postage stamps and railway opposition

Rowland Hill’s postal reform and objections to railways illustrate the difference between real-life development and inflexible prior expectations.

Make the opening contrast concrete.

Noticing how conclusions depend on fixed assumptions → The thought-machine anecdote shows cleverness can also fail to meet reality.

¶7–8 · Clever design and pushing from inside the truck

A student’s proposed machine is likened to moving a railway truck by pushing from inside it; coherent ingenuity can ignore actual conditions.

Show that merely clever thought can enclose itself and remain impractical.

Bringing thought back to real operative conditions → Steiner asks what attitude makes true thinking possible.

¶9–10 · Thought in things and the watchmaker

Steiner says thinking is not merely an activity inside the head: thoughts can be drawn from things because thought already structures them. A watch’s maker illustrates purposeful thought entering an object; he extends this to nature and spiritual beings.

Ground practical exercises in an explicitly spiritual account of thought and reality.

Receptive attention to the thought-content Steiner says belongs to things → The oil-drop demonstration exposes an omitted condition in a model.

¶11–12 · The experimenter and thought-built reality

In an oil-drop planetary demonstration, the experimenter rotates the drop. Steiner criticizes an explanation that transfers the result to nature while omitting that condition, and concludes that the world is built by thoughts.

Connect metaphysical attitude with completeness of explanatory thought.

Attention to the conditions of a model and its limits → A bridge invites repeated concrete practice.

¶13–13 · Repeated practice

Steiner calls the following indications practical principles; repeated application, he says, changes thought and brings fresh experiences.

Set expectations: capacities develop through repeated practice, not a single intellectual assent.

Regularity and willingness to practise → First observe an accessible natural process across successive days.

¶14–16 · Precise successive pictures before speculation

Observe clouds and sunset, hold a distinct detailed image, then observe again the next day. Compare the two clear pictures and let one change into the other without immediately forecasting weather or inventing the unknown connection.

Counter vague pictures and let a real process, rather than arbitrary speculation, guide thinking.

Exact observation, retention, temporal comparison and pictorial continuity → Steiner explains this in terms of the astral body and then distinguishes processes understood sufficiently for a provisional forecast.

¶17–18 · Astral body and the thought-process of the world

Steiner attributes the exercise’s effects to the astral body’s relation to the universe. Clear faithful images without arbitrary Ego-directed speculation allow world-thoughts to work beyond consciousness.

Explain why he believes the preceding pictorial method strengthens flexibility and thought.

Flexibility and strength of thought, within Steiner’s account → Where events are understood, deliberately imagine a likely future and then correct it.

¶19–21 · Predict and check where the process is understood

Picture what a neighbour’s present action may lead to tomorrow. Wait for what actually happens, compare it with the expectation, inspect any mistake and avoid boasting if correct. Steiner calls this attunement to the inner necessity of things.

Train thought to follow a process beyond the present and remain correctable by events.

Prospective causal imagination, testing and self-correction → The next exercise traces today’s event back to yesterday’s possible cause.

¶22–23 · Construct a possible past cause and inquire

A child’s conduct leads to a conjecture about earlier events. The learner then makes inquiries into what really occurred and compares the reconstructed thought-process with reality, recognizing mistakes.

Train backward reasoning with an external check rather than stop at a plausible story.

Retrospective causal reasoning and corrigibility → Steiner frames these methods as entering the inner life of things, illustrated by Goethe.

¶24–27 · Thinking within things; Goethe

Steiner describes entering the life of things through thought. Heinroth’s description of Goethe as an objective thinker and Goethe’s weather forecasts illustrate thought inseparable from attentive contemplation. He says the training can alter attitude and conduct.

Synthesize the observation and causal exercises before introducing a different practice.

Reality-oriented flexibility and a wider attentive outlook → Chosen thought trains freedom from habitual daily interests.

¶28–28 · Freely chosen sustained thought

During an available rest interval, choose something outside everyday preoccupations and think about it for perhaps five minutes, excluding other topics. Examples: a walk from two years earlier, a passage from an opened book, a morning detail otherwise overlooked. Steiner says repeated practice makes thought mobile and helps the right idea occur at the right time.

Counter automatic thought-wandering and absorption in habitual worry; train self-directed, mobile thought.

Voluntary attention, sustained thought and readiness of ideas → In the same long web paragraph he begins a second, distinct exercise in detailed recall.

¶28–32 · Detailed recall, deliberate completion and future observation

Recall a person and setting from yesterday in detail, notice the missing features, then deliberately fill the picture with imagined details, even though incorrect. Steiner says the effort to form a complete image prompts more accurate future observation and that faithful observation improves memory.

Use the effort and gaps in reconstruction to stimulate more exact future observation; develop memory through observation.

Detailed mental representation, awareness of recall gaps, future observation and memory → The next exercise turns from pictures to deliberation, alternatives and patience.

¶33–36 · Sketch alternatives, set aside and return

Steiner criticizes rushing to a result. First imagine the consequences of two or more courses, then consciously set the matter aside. If circumstances permit, defer to the next day, return to the pictures and form the decision after their further working. Sleeping on it alone is insufficient without first considering alternatives.

Counter impatience and premature certainty; let the thought-process mature rather than force its first result.

Concrete comparative judgment, patience, ability to set aside, renewed conclusion → The lecture illustrates why these practices matter in ordinary life and causal explanation.

¶37–38 · Everyday relevance and reversing cause/effect

The practices serve ordinary work across occupations. A man falls from a tree and dies; the fall may have caused death, or a fatal heart attack may have caused the fall. The observed sequence does not alone settle the causal direction.

Show the practical importance of comparing possibilities and avoid an apparent causal explanation overriding inquiry.

Recognizing alternative causal structures → A further historical example connects thought’s reach with Steiner’s spiritual premises.

¶39–41 · Ape/human concept and the supersensible

Steiner argues that a thinker who could not derive the concept human from the observed concept ape must suppose a supersensible factor in any account of transformation. This continues the spiritual premise rather than a neutral modern scientific method.

Show what he regards as omitted explanatory content in materialistic accounts.

Awareness of assumptions and conceptual incompleteness in Steiner’s framework → He distinguishes criticism of thinking from appreciation of science’s observations.

¶42–45 · Habits and feelings beneath stated reasons

Steiner appreciates science’s observations while criticizing thought-habits. He says feelings and habits often shape conviction beneath its stated arguments; anthroposophical group work broadens thinking and feeling.

Explain why logic alone may not change the way a person thinks and why sustained formation matters.

Noticing affective habits and widening outlook → The conclusion calls for love of objective reality and nature-based practice.

¶46–48 · Love of reality, nature-based practice and practical fruit

Learn to love the logic in things, rather than preference for a favored thought. Steiner recommends these exercises with natural things least altered by civilization; practical insight and activity matter more than merely accepting a doctrine or theorizing beyond the senses.

State the lecture’s outcome and the orientation for continued practice.

Objective thought made into a practical habit; perception and penetration of actual circumstances → End of translated lecture.
