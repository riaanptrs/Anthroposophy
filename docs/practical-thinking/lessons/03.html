Follow Change Through Time

How can clear successive pictures help thinking follow a real process?

Steiner source

Read Steiner

Rudolf Steiner · Practical Training In Thought · GA 108 · 18 January 1909

Read the numbered web paragraphs 14–21. One continuous lecture; the lesson titles are course divisions.

Yesterday the thing was so; to-day it is so.

George (Kaufmann) Adams translation; excerpt from paragraph 15.

Open the full lecture → · Source and edition notes

Course explanation

What Steiner is saying

A process cannot be followed through one snapshot alone. Steiner asks for a detailed image of the sky on one day and another on the following day. Both must remain clear enough to place beside one another. He contrasts this with retaining only a vague, confused picture: if the details disappear, thinking has less chance to follow how the conditions are related.

Letting one image gradually change into the other is an inner activity. It connects the observed states without turning the imagined interval into something you witnessed. You may recognize a direction of change while its cause remains unknown. The discipline is to let the real observations guide the comparison, rather than supply a convenient story that hides what was not observed.

Paragraphs 19–21 then introduce a different starting condition: an event the learner believes is sufficiently understood to picture what may follow. That expectation must be compared with the actual outcome and corrected where mistaken. It does not replace the earlier no-speculation task. Following an unfamiliar natural process and testing a prepared expectation train related capacities under different conditions.

Key idea

Keep both states clear: a faithful comparison can reveal change without claiming to have observed every intermediate step or established its cause.

Steiner’s exercise

Continue Steiner’s natural-observation exercise. Return on a following day, at approximately the same time or another time, and form another exact image. Bring both images vividly before the mind: yesterday it was so; today it is so. Place them beside one another, then let the first gradually change into the second. Where you do not understand the connection, remain with these pictures instead of making an immediate prediction.

Course application

At the first observation, a leaf is folded and presents a narrow surface. Later it is more open, but you did not watch the interval. This retained course example applies Steiner’s sky exercise to another accessible natural process. Compare outline, position and visible surfaces. Imagining a smooth opening helps relate the states; it does not prove that this exact movement occurred.

Try it

Return to the natural process chosen in the previous lesson. Recall your first image, observe again and describe one change, one continuity and one unanswered question. Identify any transition you imagined as imagined. This short written comparison is a course aid; accurate images and their relation remain the central practice.

Check your understanding

What does imagining a transition between two observations establish?

Reveal the explanation

The inner transition relates the pictures. It does not supply missing observations or automatically identify a cause.

When does Steiner introduce forecasting as a different exercise?

Reveal the explanation

Paragraphs 19–21 distinguish understood events from the unknown processes treated earlier. Errors are examined and corrected rather than hidden.

Connection

After distinguishing the observed sequence from an imagined interval, we can investigate a possible earlier cause and compare that account with what really happened.
