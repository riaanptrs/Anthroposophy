Understand the key distinction more deeply

GA 108 · §§ 14–18; contrast §§ 19–23 · Adams English translation

Separate the observed sequence from the unobserved interval

A sequence is an ordered set of observations: first this, later that. The interval between them may contain events you did not see. When Steiner asks you to let the pictures pass into one another, keep the observed endpoints clear; an imagined transition does not become a record of what occurred.

Course example: at one observation a leaf is tightly folded; at the next it is more open. Compare the outline, position and visible surfaces. You can describe a direction of change without claiming to have seen every intermediate stage.

If you imagine the leaf opening smoothly, have you observed that exact movement?

A hint

Ask whether you watched the interval or reconstructed it afterward.

Show an explained answer

No. You observed the endpoints. The imagined transition helps compare them but does not supply missing observations.

Choose the exercise that fits what you know

In this exercise the connection is still unclear, so observation and comparison come first. In the following exercise Steiner turns to cases where you have enough understanding to propose an expectation. These instructions address different starting conditions.

For now, write “I do not yet know why.” Keep a possible explanation in a separate question for later, rather than treating it as the task you must solve today. This is active attention to what you have, not indifference to explanation.

Must you produce two causes before this exercise counts as complete?

A hint

The reading asks for faithful successive pictures before speculation.

Show an explained answer

No. A precise comparison with an honestly unanswered question can complete this stage. Causal inquiry is developed in the following lessons.

See the idea in an example

At the first observation, a leaf is folded and presents a narrow surface. At the next, it is more open. You did not watch the interval between the observations.

Preserve both states before proposing a cause. Compare outline, position and visible surfaces. As you relate the pictures mentally, distinguish the observed change from an imagined transition. At this stage you do not need to explain why it occurred.

Practise across three occasions

On three occasions, observe the same plant or accessible natural process. Record the time and three concrete details. Place the records side by side and describe a change, a continuity and something still unknown. Do not invent intermediate stages or require yourself to give a cause. Check your understanding

Is imagining the transition between two observations the same as having observed it?

Show a suggested answer

No. Preserve the observed states and identify the transition as imagined. The comparison can be precise even without a causal explanation.

How to assess your response

Explain the concept accurately, identify its source, distinguish an observation from an inference and show what changed after practice. A reasoned disagreement can demonstrate understanding. Compare a specific early entry with a later one; no change is also a result worth examining.
