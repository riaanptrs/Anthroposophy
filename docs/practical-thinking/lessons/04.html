Make a Prediction You Can Check

You know when a nearby shop normally opens and expect its door to be open tomorrow morning. State the expectation clearly enough that you can compare it with what actually happens.

Steiner introduces prediction where we believe we understand a process sufficiently. This differs from the earlier exercise of observing an unfamiliar natural connection without forecasting it.

If the door is closed, inquire rather than invent an excuse: was the schedule different, or was there an exception? Correct the expectation in light of what you learn. If it is open, avoid treating one match as proof that every assumption was right.

The aim is thought that learns from events, not a score for being correct. A definite mismatch can teach more than a vague prediction that seems to fit almost anything.

Course explanation

What Steiner is saying

After observing processes whose connection is unclear, Steiner turns to events we believe we understand sufficiently. His neighbour example starts with an action today, imagines what it may lead to tomorrow, and then waits to see what happens. The expectation is definite enough to compare with an event, rather than an indefinite hope that things will somehow improve.

If the outcome differs, Steiner asks where the mistake lay and why the event developed as it did. If the expectation matches, he warns against self-congratulation. The faculty being trained is thought that can follow and learn from a process. Getting one result right is not the whole purpose; a mismatch provides something definite to examine and correct.

Steiner connects this method with the inner necessity and thought-forces he says work within things. The ordinary course practice lets you observe your own expectation and revision without treating a result as proof of that wider account. We also distinguish a matching outcome from proof of a unique cause: different circumstances can sometimes produce the same result.

Key idea

Forecast where you have a basis, then let the actual event correct the picture. Neither a confident guess nor a boast completes the practice.

Steiner’s exercise

Select an event you believe you understand. Picture what may follow, then wait and observe what actually follows. Compare the event with the expectation. If you were mistaken, examine the error and correct the thought; if you were right, avoid boasting. This is Steiner’s forward-looking exercise, separate from the earlier instruction not to forecast an unfamiliar natural connection.

Course application

You move the workshop sign beside the tool shelf and expect fewer visitors to ask where things belong. This retained modern course example gives the expectation a visible outcome. Visitor numbers, prior experience and help from volunteers also matter. If questions decrease, consider those conditions before deciding what the sign contributed. The additional check of comparable conditions is a course application.

Try it

For one low-stakes event in your own routine, state the expected result and its reason before it happens. Afterwards compare the actual result and identify one condition or mistaken assumption that matters. Keep the earlier expectation unchanged so the revision remains visible. This concise record is the course’s aid; repeat the exercise on another occasion if useful.

Check your understanding

Which starting condition belongs to this forecasting exercise?

Reveal the explanation

The lecture separates forecasting understood events from its first exercise with unfamiliar connections. The forecast remains open to correction.

What does Steiner ask when the result differs from the expectation?

Reveal the explanation

The mismatch helps expose how the thought departed from the event. Inquiry and correction serve the practice more than defending the prediction.

You predict fewer requests for help after adding labels. Requests fall from 8 to 4, but visitors fall from 20 to 5. Does this establish improvement?

Reveal the explanation

The raw count fell, but the rate rose from 40% to 80%. Neither figure alone isolates the labels’ effect because the visitor groups may differ. Compare the expectation with the actual conditions, not only the number that confirms it.
