Sustain a Chosen Thought

What changes when you choose your subject instead of following whatever enters your mind?

Course explanation

What Steiner is saying

After describing Goethe's thought as remaining with the things themselves, Steiner turns to a difficulty in ordinary life: the right idea does not always arrive when needed. During a rest, thoughts may scatter or return automatically to a particular worry. The difficulty is not simply having too many thoughts. Our attention follows the interests already occupying us, while we have little practice directing it elsewhere.

His exercise begins with a voluntary choice. The subject should stand outside the usual web of interests: an earlier walk, a scene normally overlooked or something read on a page that catches the eye. He mentions half an hour available for rest, but suggests even only five minutes of chosen thinking. The half hour describes the opportunity, not a required period of concentration. Nor does he require practising before sleep or finding a new interpretation of the past.

Steiner connects repeated practice with mobility of thought and the ability to find an idea at the right moment. The faculty being trained is self-directed ordinary thinking. The subject can be simple; its difficulty is less important than choosing it and staying with it. A recalled scene can serve this exercise without being reconstructed in every detail. That second task belongs to the memory exercise which follows.

Key idea

Choose the subject deliberately, then give it sustained attention.

Steiner’s exercise

In the first part of paragraph 28, Steiner describes using time available for rest to choose something outside everyday preoccupations. Bring an earlier experience, an overlooked scene or a suitable reading into thought, and think about it for a chosen interval, even only five minutes. Set the other concerns aside during that interval. He recommends repeating the exercise; the aim is to direct thought voluntarily rather than leave its subject to passing circumstances.

Course application

During an afternoon break, your mind returns to an unfinished task. You choose the building entrance you noticed that morning: the door's position, the steps and how people enter. When the task returns to mind, notice the diversion and resume the entrance. This course application uses returning to the chosen subject as something observable. It does not ask for perfect recall of the entrance.

Try it

During one quiet break, name an ordinary subject outside your present concerns. Think about it for up to five minutes. Afterwards, notice one point you followed and, if attention wandered, how you returned. Repeat on another occasion.

Check your understanding

What is central to Steiner's chosen-thought exercise?

Reveal the explanation

The first part of paragraph 28 trains voluntary direction of thought. Detailed reconstruction is the next exercise, and no particular time of day is required.

What do the half hour and five minutes refer to?

Reveal the explanation

Steiner describes an opportunity to rest and then suggests even only five minutes for a deliberately selected subject.

Connection

You have practised choosing what to think about. Next, keep that direction while asking a different question: how complete and accurate is the mental picture you can form of an experience?
