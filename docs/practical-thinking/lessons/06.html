Choose and sustain a line of thought

Deliberately choose a subject outside habitual concerns; distinguish this exercise from detailed recollection.

1 · Read the passage

Begin with the book

Rudolf Steiner · Practical Training in Thought · GA 108 · § 28

Whenever I have time, I will think about something I choose myself, something I bring into consciousness through my own deliberate choice.

Original German text; new English and Brazilian Portuguese study translations.

Read the original chapter or lecture (German) →

Compare with the German passage

What this passage means

Key distinction: choose what you will think about, instead of letting the next worry or association choose for you. The exercise is not primarily about giving a past event a different meaning. A memory is one possible subject; a short passage from a book can also serve.

Choose an ordinary subject outside your current worries and routine interests. For example, during a quiet afternoon break, deliberately think about the entrance of a building you passed this morning. Consider what you noticed and how its parts relate. When another concern takes over, notice it and return to your chosen subject.

Steiner mentions having half an hour available to rest and spending perhaps five minutes on the chosen thought. He does not require bedtime or a half-hour concentration session. A quiet period before sleep is possible, but the essential point is voluntary direction of attention.

Two exercises follow one another in the reading: choosing and sustaining a subject, then reconstructing a memory in detail. They can involve the same scene, but their aims differ. In the first, ask ‘Did I choose and return to my subject?’ In the second, ask ‘Which details do I recall, and which are missing or imagined?’ The next lesson teaches the memory exercise separately.

Understand the key distinction more deeply

GA 108 · § 28 · Adams English translation

Voluntary direction is not a battle to have no other thoughts

The practical distinction is between being carried along by whatever appears and deliberately returning to a selected subject. For this course, noticing a diversion and returning is part of practice, not a reason to judge yourself as incapable. A thought can arise without determining the whole session.

Choose a quiet, ordinary subject outside the worry currently occupying you. “I will think about that footbridge’s shape and use” gives you something to return to. “I must not think about work” keeps work at the centre and does not specify a new subject.

See the idea in an example

During an afternoon break, your mind returns to an unfinished task. You decide to spend five minutes thinking about a building entrance you noticed that morning: the door’s position, the steps and the route people take inside.

The main change is how the subject is selected: you choose it deliberately instead of following the automatic worry. You do not need to discover a different meaning in the scene or remember every detail. When the unfinished task returns to mind, notice and resume your chosen subject. The next exercise addresses precision of recollection.

Practise across three occasions

On three occasions, choose a quiet break. Name an ordinary subject outside your current concerns: an overlooked scene or a short reading passage. Give it up to five minutes; begin with less time if needed. Afterwards record the subject, one diversion and how you returned. The time of day is your choice, including before sleep if convenient. Assess your choice and return to the subject, without turning the exercise into a search for new meanings or perfect recall.

Check your understanding

Is a distraction evidence that the exercise failed?

Show a suggested answer

No. Noticing and deliberately returning is part of the practice. Explain where the thought departed from the chosen question.

Must you review your day differently before going to sleep?

Show a suggested answer

No. The essential task is to choose a subject outside habitual concerns and direct attention to it. No particular time or new interpretation of the past is required.

Does recalling a scene make the two exercises identical?

Show a suggested answer

No. The same scene can serve both, but the first trains choosing and sustaining a subject; the second trains reconstruction of details.

Are the thirty minutes mentioned a required concentration time?

Show a suggested answer

No. Steiner describes half an hour available for rest and suggests even just five minutes on the chosen subject.
