Compare Possibilities

A shared garden needs watering. One option gives each person a fixed day; another lets people sign up each week. Before choosing, picture how each arrangement would work during a busy week.

Steiner asks us to consider different courses of action and their consequences before settling the question. These are possible actions, not competing explanations of something already past.

Make each possibility concrete enough to examine: who acts, what happens if someone is absent, and what others need to know. Imagining consequences clarifies the choice without guaranteeing those consequences.

After this comparison, set the question aside if circumstances permit. The pause developed next has something prepared to work upon. It is not a substitute for actually considering the alternatives.

Course explanation

What Steiner is saying

Steiner begins this instruction with a familiar urge: when considering an action, we want a definite answer quickly. Having a conclusion can feel more useful than continuing to consider the situation. He argues that hurrying thought in this way does not make it practical. Patience allows the possibilities themselves to be considered before a preference closes the question.

The possibilities here are different ways of acting. They are not just competing explanations of an event already observed. Steiner asks what would happen if the task were done one way and then another. Each action must become specific enough to picture, with its likely consequences. Imagining that course does not prove what will happen, but it shows what the choice involves and gives reasons something definite to address.

He acknowledges that reasons will favour one action over another, yet asks the learner to refrain from deciding immediately. First sketch the alternatives; then set the question aside. This preparatory comparison gives the later pause something to work upon. The source does not prescribe a numerical decision table or require a perfect list of every possibility. Its practical demand is that thought genuinely considers more than the first attractive course.

Key idea

Prepare the alternatives before closing the decision.

Steiner’s exercise

Choose something that could be done in more than one way. Following paragraphs 33–34, picture what would happen if you took one course, then what would happen if you took another. Sketch both possibilities instead of making up your mind at once. Steiner then tells the learner to stop thinking about the question for a time. The next lesson develops that pause and the return to the prepared alternatives.

Course application

The workshop can keep one large sign or put labels on the shelves. You prefer the sign, but first picture both actions. A sign gives one overview and requires less work; labels put guidance beside each tool and take time to make. Ask what you have actually observed about newcomers finding tools and what remains uncertain. These concrete questions are the course's application of Steiner's comparison to the existing workshop example.

Try it

Choose one ordinary decision that can wait. Name two possible actions and picture what each would involve. For each, identify one reason grounded in the situation and one uncertainty. Keep the alternatives available for the pause in the next lesson.

What prepares the pause that follows?

A notice can be emailed or posted by the entrance. Email reaches members at home; the entrance board reaches visitors without email. What should be pictured before deciding?

Reveal the explanation

These are alternative future actions, not simply rival accounts of a past event. Making their consequences concrete prepares a decision; a third combined option may emerge, but imagining reach does not prove actual delivery.
