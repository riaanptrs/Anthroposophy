Build an Accurate Mental Picture

How can an initially inaccurate picture help train more accurate observation?

Steiner source

Read Steiner

Rudolf Steiner · Practical Training In Thought · GA 108 · 18 January 1909

Read the numbered web paragraphs 28–32. One continuous lecture; the lesson titles are course divisions.

From “Another exercise is especially adapted to work on the memory.” in paragraph 28 through paragraph 32.

The picture will, of course, be incorrect, but by the effort to gain a complete picture you will be stimulated to observe more accurately in the future.

George (Kaufmann) Adams translation; excerpt from paragraph 31.

Open the full lecture → · Source and edition notes

Course explanation

What Steiner is saying

Steiner's memory example begins in the later part of paragraph 28 with an encounter on the previous day. Knowing a person's name is not the same as recalling the person clearly, together with clothing and surroundings. Trying to form that detailed picture reveals how much was never noticed or has been forgotten. In the lecture, faithful observation is the foundation of memory: a vague encounter gives recollection little definite material to retain.

His instruction is unusual. When a detail cannot be remembered, he asks the learner to invent it and complete the picture. A forgotten coat can be imagined as grey; surroundings can also be added. He explicitly says that the resulting picture will be incorrect. The aim of the title is future accuracy of observation and memory, not a claim that imagined additions make the present recollection accurate.

Steiner argues that the effort to form a complete picture stimulates more attentive observation on later occasions. His strong expectation of improvement through repetition should be distinguished from what a particular attempt actually shows. The course adds the labels remembered, unknown and imagined, together with an immediate check where possible. These additions help track the origin of each detail; they are course adaptations, not a prohibition on the imaginative completion he instructs.

Key idea

Completing a picture can train attention; completeness and accurate recollection are different.

Steiner’s exercise

Recall a person and setting from an earlier encounter as clearly as possible. Include clothing, positions and surrounding details. In paragraphs 30–32, Steiner then asks you to fill the gaps deliberately through imagination, even though those additions will probably be wrong, until you have a complete picture. He proposes repeating this exercise to sharpen later observation and, through it, memory. His initial picture includes invention; it is not a factual record of the encounter.

Course application

Without looking, you describe a familiar tool shelf. You remember the scissors but are unsure of their position, so you imagine them on the right. Checking shows them on the left. The course's immediate comparison makes the difference visible. Keep the original remembered and imagined labels when adding corrections. Words, spatial relations or a sketch can make details specific enough to compare.

Try it

Recall one small arrangement you saw today. Describe what you remember, mark unknown details, then choose imagined details to complete it. If the arrangement is available, check it and add corrections without replacing your first account. Notice one detail to observe next time.

Check your understanding

Does Steiner forbid inventing missing details in this exercise?

Reveal the explanation

Paragraphs 30–32 explicitly propose filling gaps through imagination. The stated purpose is to stimulate more accurate future observation.

Which element is added by this course?

Reveal the explanation

The labels and immediate object check are course adaptations that keep remembered and imagined material distinguishable.

Connection
