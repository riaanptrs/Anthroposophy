What this passage means

This is the memory exercise, distinct from deliberately choosing a subject in the previous lesson. Begin with an ordinary encounter and reconstruct its particular details: where it happened, what the surroundings were like and what the person wore. Merely remembering a name is less specific than reconstructing the encounter.

Example: ‘I met my neighbour beside the gate. I remember a blue jacket, but not what they were carrying.’ The missing detail is a gap in the recollection. A plausible bag is not thereby something you remember seeing.

Read the continuation carefully: in §§ 30–32 of the Adams translation, Steiner explicitly asks for invented additions where memory fails, even though the resulting picture is inaccurate. He presents this as an exercise intended to sharpen future observation. Our course asks you to label remembered and imagined details separately, so you can understand his instruction without treating invention as evidence of what happened.

Bedtime is not required here either. The later advice to consider alternatives and then ‘sleep on’ a decision belongs to another exercise, in §§ 33–36. Keep deliberate choice of subject, detailed recollection and delayed decision-making distinct.

See the idea in an example

Without looking, you describe the tool shelf. You are certain the scissors are on the right; a check shows them on the left.

Confidence and accuracy are different things. A mental picture can combine remembered details with reasonable guesses. When you check, keep your original description visible and annotate it instead of replacing it. This reveals both omissions and additions. You may work in words, spatial relationships or a sketch; vivid visual imagery is not required for this course. The point is to make an account specific enough to compare with the object. The immediate object comparison used in this course is a checkable adaptation. In the source, Steiner starts with a past encounter and then proposes imaginatively completing missing details. The passage explanation above distinguishes that proposal from what you actually remember.

Practise across three occasions

Observe a small arrangement of ordinary objects. Look away and describe it, marking uncertain details. Check and annotate. Repeat on three days with a slightly different arrangement. Compare the kinds of omissions, not just the number of correct details.

Check your understanding

Should you fill an uncertain gap to make the picture complete?

Show a suggested answer

Label it as a guess. A fluent description can hide uncertainty; a visible gap gives you something precise to check.

What changes from the preceding exercise?

Show a suggested answer

Previously, the focus was choosing a subject and returning to it. Now it is reconstructing the details of an experience and noticing the quality and gaps of recollection.

Does Steiner prohibit invented details in this passage?

Show a suggested answer

No. In §§ 30–32 he proposes imaginative completion while acknowledging the picture will be inaccurate. In this course we identify these additions as imagined so they are not confused with memories.

Is the advice to sleep on a decision this same exercise?

Show a suggested answer

No. That belongs to comparing alternatives in §§ 33–36. The memory exercise does not require bedtime.
