2 · Work through the explanation

A worked example

At the workshop, a person reads the sign, pauses, and asks for help. You write: “The sign confused them.”

Reading, pausing and asking are described actions. Confusion is an interpretation that might be right, but the person might instead be asking permission. Create two columns: what happened and what you infer. Descriptions are selective too: record enough detail that another reader can understand the situation. The aim is not a perfectly neutral mind. It is a visible distinction between the information available and the explanation you add, so that both can be corrected.

Practise across three occasions

Choose a cup, plant or familiar workspace. Observe for two minutes on three days. Write five concrete details before adding an explanation. Compare the entries: what changed in the object, and what might simply have escaped your earlier attention?

How to assess your response

Explain the concept accurately, identify its source, distinguish an observation from an inference and show what changed after practice. A reasoned disagreement can demonstrate understanding. Compare a specific early entry with a later one; no change is also a result worth examining.
