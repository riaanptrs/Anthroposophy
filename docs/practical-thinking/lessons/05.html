Investigate Causes Without Guessing

A child leaves a game abruptly. “They wanted to spoil it” is one account, but discomfort, confusion or a disagreement could also explain the departure. The departure is observed; the motive is not yet known.

Steiner's backward-looking exercise begins by constructing a possible earlier cause. It then requires inquiry into what actually happened. The aim is not to avoid every hypothesis, but to stop treating a hypothesis as a recovered fact.

Ask what information would distinguish the possibilities. Listen to the people involved, and compare their account with the situation. Evidence may support a revision or leave the cause unsettled.

Try the sequence with a modest event: describe it, propose a cause, investigate, then correct the account. Finding your first story mistaken is part of the exercise's success, not a reason to defend it.

Does this exercise forbid proposing causes?

Course explanation

What Steiner is saying

Steiner now works backward from a present event. His example is a child’s conduct, described in the lecture as “naughty.” He asks what earlier events may have led to it and constructs causes he does not yet know. The title of this lesson therefore means not stopping with an unchecked guess. It does not mean that the learner must never propose a possibility.

The next instruction is decisive: make inquiries and find out what really happened. Compare the development of your thought with the course of the actual events. If your account is mistaken, see the error clearly. A fluent inner story is not a recovered memory; its relation to the event must be investigated. Evidence can correct the story or leave the question open.

For Steiner, this is another way of learning to take thought from the inner nature of things instead of thinking arbitrarily. Paragraph 24 describes thinking as entering the life of things, rather than remaining inside our own shell. The practical sequence gives that aim a definite form: possible cause, inquiry, comparison and correction. The course adds a clear distinction between describing an event and judging another person.

Key idea

A possible cause is a starting point for inquiry. Its coherence does not replace finding out what actually happened.

Steiner’s exercise

In Steiner’s backward-looking exercise, begin with an event today and construct what may have led to it yesterday or earlier. Then make inquiries into what really occurred. Compare your imagined thought-process with the real course of events. Recognize a mistake instead of defending the account simply because it sounded convincing. The source’s child example illustrates this sequence; the sequence is not limited to that example.

Course application

Yesterday a tray held six pencils; today it holds four. Borrowing is one possible cause, while being put in another drawer is another. This retained course application uses a small, checkable event. Look for a borrowing record, check the relevant locations or ask a neutral question. Finding the pencils elsewhere changes the borrowing explanation. If information is missing, say that the cause remains unknown.

Try it

Choose one ordinary event whose earlier circumstances can reasonably be checked. State what you observed, one possible cause and one available inquiry. Make that inquiry, then record whether your account needs correction or remains uncertain. These short prompts are the course’s format for practising the source method; they are not a worksheet dictated by Steiner.

Check your understanding

What follows constructing an unknown earlier cause in Steiner’s exercise?

Reveal the explanation

Steiner explicitly combines a constructed possibility with inquiry and comparison. An unchecked explanation does not complete the exercise.

What should happen if the inquiry contradicts the proposed cause?

Reveal the explanation

The purpose is to bring thought into better relation with the event. A correction serves that purpose more directly than defending a pleasing story.

Two mugs are missing from a shelf. A dated note says they were moved to a cupboard, where you find both. What should happen to the initial borrowing explanation?

Reveal the explanation

The note and recovered mugs distinguish these alternatives in this case. Correct the account locally. Discovering what happened here does not justify a universal claim about all borrowing.
