Understand the key distinction more deeply

GA 108 · §§ 22–26; compare § 38 · Adams English translation

Working backward means proposing a cause and checking it

Steiner now starts with a present event and asks what may have led to it. This differs from forecasting a later event. A hypothesis is a possible explanation awaiting examination. It is not a recovered memory, even when you can picture it vividly.

Course example: yesterday the tray held six pencils; today it holds four. “Two were borrowed” and “two were put in a different drawer” are hypotheses. A dated borrowing note or finding the pencils in that drawer would bear on them. Your ability to tell either story smoothly does not establish it.

What makes this more than imagining a convincing past?

A hint

Look for the step after you propose the possible cause.

Show an explained answer

You look for what actually happened, compare it with your proposal and correct the proposal. Without that check it remains an unverified account.

Earlier does not automatically mean causal

A cause contributes to producing an event; merely occurring earlier is not enough. In § 38 Steiner illustrates how a reader may even reverse cause and effect. Our course uses an ordinary example to practise keeping the direction open until there is evidence.

You notice a wet patch under a vase and conclude the vase leaked. Perhaps water was spilled before the vase was placed there. Check the vase, the surrounding surface and any available account of the sequence. If the evidence is missing, retain the uncertainty.

Can a neat timetable prove why a real event happened?

A hint

Distinguish planning how tasks could fit together from investigating what occurred.

Show an explained answer

No. A timetable can clarify dependencies, but a past event needs evidence about its actual circumstances. This lesson’s main task is causal inquiry, not scheduling.

See the idea in an example

Yesterday the tray held six pencils; today it holds four. You imagine that two were borrowed, but have not checked.

The count is an observation; borrowing is a hypothesis. Look for a record, check relevant locations or ask someone who may know a neutral question. If you find the pencils in another drawer, revise the borrowing story. If evidence is missing, explain why the cause remains open.

Practise across three occasions

On three occasions, choose a small, checkable event from your routine. Record what you know, one possible cause, another possibility and a check that could distinguish them. Make the available check and record the result before revising. Do not turn a hypothesis about someone into an accusation; keep the question open when evidence is unavailable.
