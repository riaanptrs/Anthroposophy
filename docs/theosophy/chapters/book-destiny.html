  • Individual biography

  • Reincarnation
  • Karma

Major distinctions

  • Remembering an episode differs from retaining an acquired capacity.
  • Inherited personal tendencies are distinguished from essential spiritual individuality.
  • A deed’s inner capacity and its outward consequences are related but different.
  • Reincarnation concerns spirit’s embodiment; karma concerns action and destiny.

Reconstructing the argument

Steiner first asks how a passing encounter becomes enduring. Soul preserves the past for spirit, while spirit assimilates the fruits as capacities. Action supplies an outward counterpart by changing the world. These two directions establish the chapter’s problem before its scope extends beyond a single life.

He then compares a body’s history in physical descent with the history called for by an individual spiritual form. Biography and definite predispositions lead, in his argument, to a prior history of the same spirit. The return to the members from Chapter I separates inherited tendencies from learning received by spirit-self and extended through life-spirit.

The final movement asks how a continuing individuality remains related to its deeds. Sleep and migration illustrate interrupted activity and changed surroundings without complete loss of an earlier relation. Steiner extends those patterns across lives and distinguishes bodily heredity, the soul’s karma and the spirit’s reincarnation. The chapter ends by stating his confidence in thinking and spiritual perception, then directs the reader to the three worlds.

Show a model response

A faithful reconstruction begins with experience: a present perception can pass while soul retains its significance and spirit assimilates its fruits as capacity. Steiner asks whether the individuality developing those capacities has a history beyond this life. Comparing bodily form with spiritual form, he treats an individual biography and definite dispositions as requiring a preceding history of that same spirit. He distinguishes this continuity from inherited bodily form and personal tendencies. Learning becomes his model for fruits carried through spirit-self and life-spirit. A deed also leaves outer effects and inward capacities. Sleep shows interruption without erasing earlier conditions; migration shows that new surroundings can still be met through former development. Steiner extends these comparisons to a re-embodied spirit related to its earlier actions. The final distinctions therefore concern body and heredity, soul and self-created fate or karma, and spirit and reincarnation. The cross-life steps are his explanatory extensions, rather than direct consequences of observing an ordinary memory, skill or change of country.

This reconstructs Steiner’s argument; other faithful wordings are possible.
