The life of the soul

The sentient soul

The Question

How does a sensation become part of your inward life?

Begin with Experience

The smell of fresh bread reaches you. You enjoy it and want a slice. Smelling, enjoying and wanting are connected, but we can notice what each contributes.

Steiner’s Idea

Steiner uses sentient soul for this inwardly lived response: sensation, pleasure, aversion, desire and impulse. The smell is not only a physical event in the air; it is experienced by someone and becomes significant in that person's life.

Recall the blue cup: physical structure, living maintenance, bodily openness to sensation, and inward experience were four different questions. The same distinction applies to smelling bread. Steiner calls the bodily member connecting sensory conditions with inner life sentient body; enjoying and wanting the bread belong to sentient soul. Dependence on bodily conditions does not make those two descriptions identical.

Wanting the bread does not make reaching for it inevitable. You may remember that it belongs to someone else and wait. Thought can enter the same encounter; the soul functions are not separate kinds of people. This example clarifies the role of sentient soul without establishing Steiner's claim that it is a supersensible member.

See the Relationship

Sentient soul

The inward activity of sensation and related pleasures, aversions, desires, impulses and passions in Steiner’s account.

  • Sentient body

    The sentient soul’s activity depends on bodily mediation.

  • Soul

    Sentient soul differentiates the inward life already introduced as soul.

  • Astral body

    The fourfold astral member joins sentient soul with sentient body.

  • Feeling

    Pleasure and aversion give sensation personal significance.

  • Willing

    An inward impulse can find expression in action.

  • Intellectual or mind soul

    Thinking can enter this life and organize its responses.

Important Distinction

Sentient soul is not a temperament label or a judgment that emotion is inferior. It names a necessary dimension of inward experience that can be permeated by thought and spiritual content.

Apply It

How do smell, pleasure, desire and action differ in the bakery example?

Consider an approach

The smell is experienced; pleasure gives it personal significance; desire inclines toward an outcome; action expresses an impulse outwardly. These aspects can be closely connected without being identical.

A reason to wait shows how thinking can enter the response. No diagnosis of a soul member follows from a single observed action.

Optional: try the related practice

Check Your Understanding

Does sentient mean only detecting a stimulus?

Explore the reasoning

No. Here it includes the inward experience and the pleasure, aversion or desire that can arise. The concept concerns lived response.

Does waiting for permission mean the desire never occurred?

Explore the reasoning

No. Desire can remain while understanding changes the action. Several soul functions can participate in one event.

Bringing the whole picture together

Useful terminology

Sentient soul
The inward activity of sensation and related pleasures, aversions, desires, impulses and passions in Steiner’s account.
Sentient body
The living bodily organization in its role of opening to sensation and connecting bodily conditions with the sentient soul, in Steiner’s account.
Soul
A person’s inward life, in which sensation, feeling, desire, thinking and willing make the world individually experienced.

Points to revisit

  • Sentient soul includes sensation and related inward stirrings.
  • Bodily dependence does not erase the distinction from sentient body.
  • Desire and the action that may express it are different.
  • The three soul functions can work within one person and encounter.

Source questions and qualifications

An inward world and another person

Steiner distinguishes having one’s own sensations from spiritually seeing another’s. Ordinary conversation can improve an interpretation, but it is not the higher organ he describes. A careful course response leaves room for correction: an apparent dislike may concern an association or circumstance that the observer has not understood.
