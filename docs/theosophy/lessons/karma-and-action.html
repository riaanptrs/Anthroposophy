Begin with experience

Keeping a promise can change both a relationship and your own habit of reliability. Even after the conversation ends, others may act differently because of what you did.

Steiner’s account

The central idea

Karma is Steiner’s account of the connection between a person’s deeds and the destiny they later encounter, including consequences extending across earthly lives.

Useful terminology

Karma
The relation between a person’s deeds and the destiny later encountered, which Steiner extends across earthly lives.
Reincarnation
Steiner’s account of one spiritual individuality living successive earthly lives in different bodily constitutions.
Willing
The activity of directing oneself toward doing and carrying a purpose into action.

Building the concept

An action has an inward and an outward history. Inwardly, doing something can change what you have learned, what you are ready to attempt or the habits you carry. Outwardly, it changes a shared world: a repaired object, an injured relationship or a fulfilled promise remains relevant after the action itself is over. These two histories are related without being identical.

Continuity within one lifetime makes this distinction easy to approach. After sleep, you return to a world that still includes yesterday’s deeds. Steiner uses this familiar interruption to introduce a further claim: death does not erase the relation between a spiritual individuality and the world its actions helped shape. Reincarnation names the continuing lives; karma names the relation of deeds to later destiny.

The source makes a strong claim, connecting later joys, sorrows and wishes with actions of earlier lives. It presents this as a spiritual law, not merely advice to behave well or a metaphor for habits. Recognizing the force of that claim is part of understanding Steiner. Our everyday observation of consequences, however, does not establish the unseen origin of a particular hardship or justify a verdict about another person’s past.

For ordinary practice, the useful question is concrete: what did my action change, and what response is possible now? Responsibility can include repair, assistance and reconsideration. Neither a difficulty nor a success gives us access to another person’s spiritual history. Reflection on karma can therefore remain a study of the proposed relation while attention to present consequences supports thoughtful action.

Keep this distinction

Reincarnation concerns the continuing individuality; karma concerns the consequences of deeds in destiny. Karma is broader than immediate reward or punishment and cannot be diagnosed from someone’s present circumstances.

Try it in life

Trace an action’s visible effects

Course exercise

Separate what an ordinary action changes inwardly from what it changes in a shared situation.

Optional course suggestion: about five minutes, with a later revisit only if useful.

  1. Choose one small action from today or yesterday, such as keeping a promise, lending an object or leaving a task unfinished.
  2. Identify one visible change in the shared situation. Separate what you know from what you are only guessing about others.
  3. Notice any learning, habit or change of readiness within you. Keep this distinct from the outward effect.
  4. Ask whether a helpful next action is available. Reflect silently or keep a private note if you wish.

What remains of the action outside you, and what remains as a change within you?

Reveal course notes

If you repair a shared shelf, the outward effect is a shelf others can use more reliably. The inward fruit may be a practical skill, greater care in planning or a better sense of when to ask for help. The usable shelf belongs to the changed shared situation; the acquired capacity belongs to what the action has changed in you.

A later problem with the repair calls for examining the actual cause and deciding whether to fix it. That response continues the visible history of the deed. Steiner extends the relation of deeds and destiny across lives, but this comparison stays with the inward and outward effects you can distinguish in the present case.

Chapter II distinguishes inward fruits from deeds that continue to affect the world. This course comparison uses accessible effects within one life to clarify the distinction, rather than verify the source’s extension across incarnations.

Reflect in your own words

In one ordinary action, what changed within you and what changed in the situation shared with others?

Reveal course notes

Returning a borrowed book when promised has an outward effect: the other person can use it, and the agreement has been fulfilled. It may also have an inward effect: you learn how to arrange your tasks, strengthen a habit of reliability or recognize what helped you keep the promise. The book’s availability and your acquired habit are different results of one action.

If the book was returned late, the next response might include acknowledging the delay and arranging a useful repair. Those are accessible consequences within this life. Steiner’s karma extends the relation between deeds and later destiny across earthly lives; the example clarifies inward fruits and outward effects without disclosing an unseen cause of someone’s circumstances.

What to remember

  • A deed can leave both inward fruits and outward consequences.
  • Steiner connects deeds with later destiny across earthly lives.
  • The source’s spiritual claim is stronger than the observable fact that actions have consequences.
  • Present responsibility includes the possibility of helping and repairing.
  • Karma does not make another person’s former deeds known from their present suffering.

Optional deeper study

The three continuities at the end of Chapter II

Steiner closes Chapter II by associating bodily continuity with heredity, the soul’s destiny with karma and spiritual continuity with reincarnation. This is a coordinated account of body, soul and spirit, not three interchangeable names for fate.

His examples move from familiar consequences to a supersensible continuation of those relations. Keeping the steps of that argument visible prevents an ordinary analogy from being treated as proof of a specific unseen cause.
