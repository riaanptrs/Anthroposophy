Appendix II: reconstructing the four-body-type hypothesis

How does Childs’s final Appendix II model produce four body types?

1 · Read the passage

Begin with the book

Gilbert Childs · Understand Your Temperament! A Guide to the Four Temperaments · Appendix II, PDF capture 94, column 3; printed folio not visible.

for there are in reality only two somatotypes: one severe and one moderate of each type.

Gilbert Childs, Understand Your Temperament!, Sophia Books / Rudolf Steiner Press, published 2012; first published 1995, copyright Gilbert Childs 1995. Selected wording from the complete supplied PDF; line breaks normalized.

What this passage means

This excerpt states Childs's final two-by-two scheme: two broad categories, each in extreme and moderate forms. In the preceding paragraph, four temperaments are said to fit four somatotypes. The apparent two/four wording depends on this distinction between broad category and degree; it is his proposed model, not independent biological proof.

The focus of this lesson

Follow the stages of Childs's proposed model and distinguish correspondence from supporting evidence.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

The reading’s key idea

Childs returns to the mismatch between four temperaments and three somatotypes. After discussing three embryonic layers and Martigny's fourth, chordoblastic category, he proposes two broad builds, leptomorphic and eurymorphic, each with a moderate and an extreme form. His final arrangement is melancholic as extreme leptomorph, sanguine as moderate leptomorph, choleric as moderate eurymorph and phlegmatic as extreme eurymorph. He assigns head, lungs, heart/blood and digestion to these four respectively, with mesodermal interfaces supposed to explain the moderate forms.

The supplied figures and tables are readable and reveal the stages of the argument. Earlier tables retain mesomorphic terminology that the final model rejects, and some prose changes the correspondences again. Keep those stages distinct. Childs explicitly offers suggestions about embryological interfaces; the proposed temperament causation is not demonstrated by a drawing or by matching the number of categories. The course uses a reconstruction of his model and an evidence question instead of classifying a learner's body.

Reading Appendix II: the stages of a proposed correspondence

Why Childs returns to embryology

The appendix resumes the question of four temperaments and three somatotypes: ectomorphic, mesomorphic and endomorphic. Childs describes their proposed connection with ectoderm, mesoderm and endoderm, then uses cited observations about cell division, migration and differentiation to challenge rigid assignments. He also returns to an older contrast between slender and stockier builds. The biological terms and the temperament hypothesis are different parts of the discussion. In particular, the insistence that the two classifications must match one-to-one is an assumption of his project. Arithmetic alone does not establish that assumption or demonstrate a causal relationship.

Appendix II, PDF captures 83–85; cited references in 96.

Martigny's fourth category and shifting names

Childs introduces Martigny's chordoblastic category alongside mesoblastic, entoblastic and ectoblastic. The comparison table associates these with sanguine, choleric, phlegmatic and melancholic respectively, retaining mesomorphic terminology for the muscular choleric form. Subsequent prose reassigns the middle form and describes choleric and phlegmatic physiques together as endomorphic. Figures 3 and 4 make the proposed rearrangement visible. These stages should be read separately: the table, the criticism of other authors and Childs's eventual terminology are not one stable classification. Even Figure 3's caption refers to three main types while the illustration distinguishes four forms.

Appendix II, PDF captures 85 column 3–88 column 1; Figures 3–4.

Two broad builds, each with two degrees

The final scheme uses extreme and moderate forms of two broad physiques. Melancholic is extreme leptomorphic, sanguine moderate leptomorphic, choleric moderate eurymorphic and phlegmatic extreme eurymorphic. Figure 5 proposes mesodermal interfaces to moderate the ectoderm-related and endoderm-related categories. The Composite Table calls both moderate types mesodermal in its Origin row. It also combines introverted, convergent extreme types with extroverted, divergent moderate types; stable phlegmatic and sanguine with unstable choleric and melancholic. Its other rows link inward/outward direction, personal/behavioural difficulties, antipathy/sympathy and attitudes to time: phlegmatic present comfort, choleric future events, sanguine present mental activity and melancholic past inner concerns. These additional correspondences remain parts of his model. Childs's words indicating a suggestion matter here: he is proposing how these correspondences arise, rather than reporting a demonstrated link between embryology and temperament.

Appendix II, PDF capture 88 columns 2–3, 89 column 1 and 94 columns 2–3.

Four organ correspondences and what they establish

Childs extends threefolding to senses and head, chest rhythm and digestion, stressing the interdependence of the systems. Figures 6 and 7 then place melancholic with head, sanguine with lungs, choleric with heart and blood, and phlegmatic with digestion. Having assigned the extreme forms to head and digestion, he argues for the remaining forms through chest organs and interfaces. At the conclusion he rejects a separate mesomorphic physique. Reconstruct this progression faithfully, then ask what evidence would test it independently. The references identify texts he consulted; the symmetry of his diagram does not verify all the deductions he adds.

Appendix II, PDF captures 89–95, especially Figures 6–7 in 91–92; references in 96.

Connect the parts of the reading

How does Childs reach four types, and what remains an explanatory hypothesis?

An explained answer: His final scheme divides leptomorphic and eurymorphic builds into extreme and moderate forms, yielding four temperament correspondences. The derivation through germ-layer interfaces and the assignment to head, lungs, heart/blood and digestion remain his proposed explanations, requiring independent supporting evidence.

Examine the argument

  1. Question: why are there four types in one classification and three in another?

Words for the reading

Somatotype: category of bodily build. Leptomorphic/eurymorphic: Childs's slender and stockier broad categories. Germ layers: embryonic tissue layers. Interface: a proposed meeting of layers used to explain moderate forms. Correspondence: an asserted relation that still requires evidence.

See the idea in an example

A library sorts books into three sizes and four subjects. Nobody needs to invent a fourth size merely because there are four subjects.

The analogy isolates the logical issue: a numerical mismatch is not automatically a contradiction. Whether two classifications are connected needs independent evidence.

How does Childs's final model produce four body types?

Show a suggested answer

He proposes two broad physiques, leptomorphic and eurymorphic, each in an extreme and a moderate form. These become melancholic/sanguine and phlegmatic/choleric respectively; he rejects a separate mesomorphic category at the end.

What role do the mesoderm and its interfaces play in his hypothesis?

Show a suggested answer

He proposes that interfaces with ectoderm and endoderm moderate the two broad categories, producing sanguine and choleric forms. He further links these with lungs and heart/blood. These are his speculative explanatory steps.

Why does the numerical mismatch not prove the proposed causal relation?

Show a suggested answer

It assumes that temperament categories and build categories must correspond one-to-one. That assumption requires justification, and the biological relationship would need evidence independent of the categories and diagrams used to describe it.
