Chapter 8: compatibility as a question, not a verdict

By the end of this lesson: Explain an interaction pattern and propose a mutual agreement without predicting a relationship’s fate.

Start with a situation

One partner enjoys spontaneous outings while another wants advance notice. Calling the pairing incompatible settles nothing about what each needs or what they can agree. Four labels give sixteen ordered choices if the first and second positions matter. Childs treats A with B as the same pairing as B with A: the twelve unlike ordered choices become six pairs, while the four same-type pairs remain. That yields ten sketches. This counting convention says nothing about whether two partners have identical roles. In the outing scene, useful discussion still specifies notice, freedom to decline and how shared plans are made. A symmetric table is not evidence of equal needs or a prediction of the relationship’s fate.

Understand the concept

Compatibility asks how two people’s ways of responding interact. Similar tendencies may make understanding easier while reinforcing a shared difficulty; different tendencies may complement each other while creating friction. There are ten unordered pairings of four main temperaments when same-type pairs are included: four matching pairs and six different pairs. The book presents these through deliberate verbal caricatures.

Follow the relationship

A pairing becomes understandable when you name the repeated sequence. One person proposes a change; the other asks for preparation; the first hears rejection and presses harder; the second withdraws. The sequence can be changed by an agreement about notice, choice and shared responsibilities. A pair of names alone does not determine whether that agreement is possible.

Apply the distinction

There are ten unordered pairings of four types: four pairs of the same type and six mixed pairs. Counting possible combinations organizes Childs’s discussion; it cannot predict a relationship. Whether an invitation is welcome depends on the people’s wishes and circumstances, including their freedom to decline.

The book’s detailed argument — optional

Connect with the book

Childs considers four same-type and six different-type pairings. He explicitly calls the sketches deliberately skewed verbal caricatures and says secondary tendencies, personality, health, upbringing, education and unexpected responses qualify prediction. Within that frame he explores shared force and rivalry, lively ideas and staying power, methodical restraint, conflicting control, doubled changeability, grounded counterbalance, divergent needs for novelty and security, doubled inertia, manipulation and resentment, and sensitivity seeking reciprocity. Similarity and difference can each help or intensify difficulty in his account.

A worked example, step by step

1. The situation

One partner enjoys spontaneous visits; the other needs advance notice and time alone.

2. What helps explain it

Novelty and continuity need coordination. If each treats their own preference as obvious, the disagreement can be misread as lack of affection.

3. A possible response

Agree which visits can be spontaneous, which need notice and how either person can decline. Evaluate the agreement by mutual respect and actual experience, rather than a compatibility verdict.

Original course example for understanding the idea; not a book quotation or a classification test.

Two questions — optional

Why are there ten unordered pairings of four main temperaments, including same-type pairs?

See an explanation

There are four matching pairs and six different pairs. Reversing the order of a mixed pair does not create a new unordered pairing. This count organizes the book’s discussion, not a relationship prediction.

Why can both similarity and difference create difficulties?

See an explanation

Similarity can double an unhelpful habit; difference can produce mismatched expectations. Both can also support cooperation. What matters is the actual pattern and how the people respond to it.

Reading the historical claims

The retained readings contain historical claims about bodily appearance, physiology, illness and ways of educating or handling people. These are attributed claims, not established diagnostic criteria or instructions to follow. Do not infer temperament from body shape, eye colour, ethnicity, sex, disability or birthday. Melancholic does not mean depression, and sanguine does not mean ADHD.

Constitutional charts are source- and age-specific: the 1909 pairings must not be applied universally to children. GA 295 explicitly gives different child pairings. Historical proposals involving fabricated distress, deliberate frustration, deprivation or coercion are not adopted as course practices. Use honest, respectful and revisable responses.

Read all ten caricatured partnerships

The author’s opening qualification governs every pair

Childs begins with all four temperaments mixed within each person and explains the ten unordered combinations: four same-type and six different-type pairs. He calls the coming sketches deliberately skewed verbal caricatures. Secondary temperament, personality, health, upbringing, education and unexpected reactions complicate prediction. These qualifications do not erase the forceful judgments in the sketches. Read them as the frame within which he contrasts association, complementarity and friction, rather than as a success ranking. The four pairings beginning with choleric

Choleric/choleric magnifies rival leadership and volatile will, but shared direction, exchanged responsibilities and remembered fellow-feeling can support cooperation. Choleric/sanguine combines practical determination with rapid creative ideas; shared pace excites both, while weak staying power or inner resources may undermine later years. Choleric/phlegmatic opposes heat and water: methodical restraint can temper enterprise, and divided tasks can help, provided tolerances and goodwill develop. Choleric/melancholic instead caricatures two inflated egos seeking control by force or strategy. The domestic scene lets each partner tell a story of devotion and sacrifice while perpetuating the other’s difficulties. It is explicitly exaggerated, not an ordinary description of every couple.

PDF captures 54–58.

The three remaining pairings beginning with sanguine

Sanguine/sanguine doubles changing interests, timing conflicts and mutual admiration; Childs also expects maturity to add grace and consideration, with secondary tendencies supporting an underdeveloped inner life. Sanguine/phlegmatic pairs airy imagination with patient grounding and practical steadiness, but stubbornness and excess can create friction. His gendered shopping and finance scenes illustrate the caricature, including a reversal of the sexes. Sanguine/melancholic contrasts novelty and flexibility with reserve and security. The concert-versus-book dispute dramatizes wounded attention, withdrawal and reconciliation; time and secondary mixtures may help, but Childs predicts persistent tension.

PDF captures 58–63.

The final three inward-facing combinations

Phlegmatic/phlegmatic offers stability, comfort and routine, with doubled inertia; secondary sanguine or choleric movement may provide rhythm. Phlegmatic/melancholic is the starkest control-and-resentment sketch: demands corner an accommodating partner until the author imagines melodramatic violence, while the other intensifies self-torment. Melancholic/melancholic combines sensitivity and compassion with disappointed expectations of solicitude, pride and mutual suffering; secondary tendencies mitigate extremes, and tribulation may yield later wisdom. Preserve these actual claims when comparing the course’s questions. A negotiated agreement in a fictional situation neither confirms the prediction nor makes coercion or violence something to endure because of a label.
