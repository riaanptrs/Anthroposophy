Chapter 1: the same, only different

By the end of this lesson: Distinguish temperament, individual use, expressed personality and habitual character in Childs’s terminology.

Start with a situation

Two people act quickly. One checks who will be affected before volunteering; the other promises on everyone’s behalf. Speed alone does not tell us the quality of their choices. The same broad tendency can be expressed differently. Observe the action and its circumstances before praising or blaming a type.

Understand the concept

Childs separates the tendencies a person brings from the way that person uses them. His card-game image makes the distinction memorable: the hand is the initial disposition; playing the hand is the individual use. Personality appears in deeds and outlook. Character, in this passage, refers to characteristic habitual responses, rather than simply moral goodness.

Follow the relationship

Separate pace, purpose and responsibility. Two people can act equally quickly while one consults those affected and the other commits them without asking. Their speed may look alike, but the actions differ in judgment and regard for others. A temperament description therefore cannot do the work of describing a whole personality or evaluating an action.

Apply the distinction

A person may sustain attention while repairing something and lose patience during a meeting. Describe both situations before deciding that one trait explains the person. Childs’s distinction between shared tendencies and individual use invites us to look for how a quality operates, rather than replace a person’s history with a category.

A worked example, step by step

1. The situation

Two volunteers offer to organize an event. One asks about the group’s needs; the other announces everyone’s jobs without discussion.

2. What helps explain it

Both show initiative. Their different use of it explains why one action creates cooperation while the other may create resentment.

3. A possible response

Describe the actual choice each made. If you use the card image, name both the shared tendency and the individual manner of playing it.

Reading the historical claims

The retained readings contain historical claims about bodily appearance, physiology, illness and ways of educating or handling people. These are attributed claims, not established diagnostic criteria or instructions to follow. Do not infer temperament from body shape, eye colour, ethnicity, sex, disability or birthday. Melancholic does not mean depression, and sanguine does not mean ADHD.

Constitutional charts are source- and age-specific: the 1909 pairings must not be applied universally to children. GA 295 explicitly gives different child pairings. Historical proposals involving fabricated distress, deliberate frustration, deprivation or coercion are not adopted as course practices. Use honest, respectful and revisable responses.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

The reading’s key idea

Childs treats temperament as basic tendencies dealt at birth like cards, individuality as the way the cards are played, personality as expressed in deeds and outlook, and character as habitual characteristic responses rather than moral respectability in this distinction. His historical table joins four temperaments with elements and humours. None is best: all four occur in unequal proportions. Yet he also claims visible bodily constitution and reliable prediction, and supplies an attention/perseverance chart as a clue.

The chapter contrasts three somatotypes with four temperaments and proposes four physical categories. Its polarities are melancholic/sanguine and phlegmatic/choleric. The exact chart gives melancholic attention not easily aroused but strongly persevering; phlegmatic least easily aroused but strongly persevering; choleric most easily aroused and most strongly persevering; sanguine easily aroused with little perseverance. These are Childs’s proposed models. Our two fast-acting people illustrate different individual uses of a tendency, rather than establish appearance-based classification.

Read sameness, difference and the proposed clues

Cards, personality and character

The first chapter moves from familiar behaviour to three working distinctions. Temperament gives basic tendencies, pictured as cards dealt at birth. Individuality concerns how those cards are played. Personality appears in what people actually do, in their outlook, opinions and philosophy of life. Character directs attention to habitual reactions prompted by feeling, thought or duty. In this context Childs uses character to mean what imparts characteristics, not a moral ranking of respectable and criminal lives. These distinctions let him combine shared patterns with differences in the quality and direction of a person’s actions.

Native PDF captures 9, column 3; 10, columns 1–3.

The historical fourfold vocabulary

The traditional table pairs choleric with fire and yellow bile, sanguine with air and blood, phlegmatic with water and body fluids, and melancholic with earth and black bile. Childs distinguishes these historical associations from the very brief dictionary meanings, which he regards as only a small part of each portrait. He nevertheless defends the doctrine’s longevity and expected practical success against scientific objections. Shakespeare and composers provide recognizable illustrations, not independent demonstrations that a humour or an element causes a personality.

Native PDF capture 11, columns 1–3.

From physique to attention

Childs treats three somatotypes as inadequate for four temperaments and proposes two kinds each of tall/thin and short/heavier form. He claims bodily constitution makes temperament visible and prediction tests recognition. His attention chart adds a different proposed clue: melancholic attention is not easily aroused but strongly persevering; phlegmatic is least easily aroused but strongly persevering; choleric is most easily aroused and most strongly persevering; sanguine is easily aroused with little perseverance. Preserve this exact chart, especially phlegmatic perseverance, while identifying it as Childs’s model rather than an established assessment.

Native PDF captures 12, columns 1–3; 13, column 1.

Mixture and a reflective qualification

No temperament is best: each has desirable and difficult qualities. Childs says all four occur in unequal proportions, generally with two conspicuous tendencies, a trace of a third and a fourth overpowered by the others. His polarities are melancholic/sanguine and phlegmatic/choleric. He recommends longer observation yet also promises clues after minutes of conversation. The chapter ends with possible gains in tolerance and limits of self-knowledge. Our two fast-acting people illustrate individuality’s different use of a tendency; adding circumstances and a counterexample is a course application, not a replacement for the author’s stronger prediction claim.

Connect the parts of the reading

What distinguishes the resources of temperament from the person’s use of them in Childs’s first chapter?

An explained answer: The birth-cards analogy presents temperament as a basic disposition. Individuality plays those cards, and personality is expressed in deeds and outlook. Character concerns characteristic responses. This permits shared tendencies and individual differences, even though Childs also claims temperament can be recognized bodily and used to predict behaviour.
