What this passage means

The surrounding argument calls sanguine inconsistency itself a consistent pattern. Stable therefore has a particular meaning in this model; it should not become reliable task completion or a mental-health judgment.

The focus of this lesson

Separate the axes Childs combines and examine what each is meant to explain.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

The reading’s key idea

Childs joins three classifications: phlegmatic and melancholic are introvert/reflective and convergent; choleric and sanguine are extrovert/active and divergent. Phlegmatic and sanguine are stable, choleric and melancholic unstable. Stable sanguinity means a consistent pattern of changeability. His combined table associates introverts with personality difficulties and extroverts with behavioural difficulties. He then develops polarities, child guidance and age overlays. The course reads these as linked authorial claims, not interchangeable measurements of intelligence or mental health.

Childs distinguishes inner/outer orientation from thinking traits and claims no appreciable intelligence difference. His diagrams combine these axes with stability and the elements, while his rhetoric sometimes strains the labels. He says children’s temperament should be understood rather than changed: offer choleric challenges with ready help, seek lasting sanguine interests and attachment, introduce phlegmatic peers’ interests, and tell melancholics prolonged tragic stories to encourage future sympathy. Childhood, adolescence, middle age and later life are assigned sanguine, choleric, melancholic and phlegmatic overlays. Our project exercise separately tests idea generation and evaluation as a later application.

Keep Childs’s psychological axes distinct

Three classifications joined together

Childs first distinguishes a public outer world from a private inner world. Introverts are reflective and inwardly active; extroverts are active in outward company. He next associates concentration, retentive memory and logical precision with convergent thinking, imagination and intuitive solutions with divergent thinking. His pairings are phlegmatic/melancholic with introversion and convergence, choleric/sanguine with extroversion and divergence. He claims no appreciable intelligence difference, but different examination strengths: convergents with closed questions, divergents with open-ended and multiple-choice questions. A third classification makes phlegmatic/sanguine stable and choleric/melancholic unstable. Sanguine stability means consistency of its characteristic inconsistency, not dependable follow-through.

Models of difficulties and polarities

The next table associates inwardly directed introverts with personality difficulties and outwardly directed extroverts with behavioural difficulties. These are Childs’s inferences, not a diagnostic table. Figure 1 combines the axes and four elements, with stable above unstable; the later quadrant reverses that vertical orientation while preserving the pairings. Strength becoming weakness in excess explains his earth, water, fire and air imagery. His rhetoric sometimes conflicts with formal labels: sanguine is also called instability personified. The refuge sentence in capture 16 likewise repeats the same direction despite the surrounding people/objects contrast.

The author’s four routes for children

Childs says temperament is determined at birth and should be understood rather than changed. For choleric children he proposes tasks just beyond capability, immediate help if difficulty was misjudged, demonstrated skill and no breaking the will. For sanguines he urges close observation of varied activities until a lasting interest appears, plus attachment to a sympathetic person. Phlegmatics should meet patient, accomplished peers willing to share interests. For melancholics he opposes forced cheerfulness and proposes prolonged tragic stories, preferably true, hoping vicarious suffering will later inspire charitable action. These are the actual author proposals; a manageable caring activity is a different course adaptation.

Native PDF captures 21, columns 1–3; 22, columns 1–3; 23, columns 1–3; 24, column 1.

Age overlays and a practical comparison

The basic disposition remains underneath a developmental overlay: sanguine childhood to about ten or eleven, choleric adolescence, melancholic middle age and phlegmatic later life. Childs also expects bodily recognition to become easier with age. These are broad, sometimes caricatured developmental claims, not an obligatory timetable. Our project exercise separates generating options from testing them against criteria. It makes his thinking distinction a subject of comparison without equating sociability, creativity, intelligence and mental health. The quiet inventive learner and sociable procedural learner supply concrete counterexamples to a rigid equation.

Connect the parts of the reading

Why can a sanguine pattern be stable in Childs’s classification while showing little persistence?

An explained answer: He calls a recurring pattern stable even when changeability is part of that pattern. Stable therefore concerns the consistency of the disposition, not finishing a particular task. Attention/perseverance, inward/outward orientation and thinking style must still be understood as distinct classifications that Childs then links.

Words for the reading

Introversion/extroversion: inward or outward orientation in this model. Convergent/divergent: Childs’s linked thinking portraits, including precision versus imaginative alternatives. Stable: consistency of a characteristic pattern, even when changeable. Personality/behavioural difficulties: his inward/outward problem distinction. Overlay: age-related colouring over a basic disposition.

See the idea in an example

A quiet learner proposes many possible designs, then carefully evaluates one. Another enjoys groups but prefers a known procedure.

These examples challenge a rigid equation between sociability and a particular way of thinking. A person can generate alternatives and evaluate them at different stages of one task.

Why can a changeable sanguine pattern be called stable?

Show a suggested answer

Childs treats its characteristic inconsistency as a consistent disposition. Stable is not the same as reliable follow-through or good judgment.

Which age overlays does Childs propose, and what remains underneath?

Show a suggested answer

Sanguine childhood, choleric adolescence, melancholic middle age and phlegmatic later life, over a basic temperament. These are broad authorial claims, not an obligatory timetable for every person.
