Central question

What does thinking add to an encounter that observation by itself does not supply?

Why this matters

Earlier lessons used concepts to describe the human being and the three worlds. We now ask what a concept contributes to knowing. This prepares the distinction between receiving an impression and understanding a relationship.

Course explanation

What Steiner is saying

In The Philosophy of Freedom, Steiner begins with an event and its conceptual counterpart. A billiard ball moves another ball whether or not a spectator thinks about it. When the spectator relates impact, motion and elasticity, a second process takes place in thinking. That reflection seeks to understand the event; it does not physically cause the collision.

He uses percept for the content encountered through observation, rather than the process of observing. A color can be a percept, but so can a feeling we become aware of within ourselves. A concept supplies intelligible content or a relationship. The distinction concerns two sides of knowing; a percept is not automatically the same as a remembered mental picture.

The quoted sentence brings these sides together. In Steiner’s account, knowing joins what observation encounters with the conceptual content apprehended in thinking. We do not understand an event merely by receiving disconnected impressions. Equally, proposing a concept does not guarantee that we have understood this event correctly: its relation to the actual observation still needs examination. Seeing a second ball move and understanding the collision are distinguishable achievements. A spectator who did not see the impact might imagine several explanations; the concept must be related to what actually occurred. Thinking supplies connections, not permission to ignore the observation. Reflecting later on how that explanation was formed also gives thinking a new object: its own earlier activity.

Thinking has a distinctive place in this inquiry because the thinker participates in its production. Normally we attend to the object, leaving our thinking unobserved. To examine a previous act of thinking, we carry out a new act directed toward it. Steiner does not describe watching the very same act from outside while we are performing it.

In this book, intuition names the inward apprehension of conceptual content in thinking. It means more than a guess. Keep this use of intuition in mind: the ethical use in the next lesson and the higher Intuition described in Lesson 18 have different contexts.

Key concept: Percept and concept

In Steiner’s account, a percept is encountered through observation; a concept provides intelligible content. Knowing joins the two through thinking.

Course example

A shadow becomes shorter as a lamp is moved. The observed shape is one side of the encounter; understanding the relation among the light source, object and surface is another. This illustrates observation and explanation without guaranteeing that every explanation we propose is correct.

Important distinction

Thinking supplies the conceptual counterpart of an event; it does not make that external event happen. A percept is what is observed, including inward experience, rather than the act of observation or necessarily a remembered image.

Check your understanding

What does Steiner call a percept?

Reveal the explanation

The term names the observed content, not the act of observing; it includes more than external sensory objects.

What does reflection add to the billiard event?

Reveal the explanation

The conceptual process depends on the thinker, while the physical occurrence proceeds independently of that reflection.

How does Steiner describe observing one’s own thinking?

Reveal the explanation

He distinguishes carrying out thinking from subsequently making that activity an object of reflection.
