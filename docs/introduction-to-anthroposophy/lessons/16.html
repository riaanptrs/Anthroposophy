Freedom and Moral Intuition

Central question

When is an action one’s own in Steiner’s sense, rather than the execution of an impulse or command?

Why this matters

Understanding a situation does not yet tell us what to do in it. Steiner connects freedom with the origin of an ethical motive. We also need to distinguish choosing an aim from imagining and carrying out its particular form.

Course explanation

What Steiner is saying

In the passage surrounding the quotation, “such an action” is determined by ideal content rather than by an external rule or an unexamined impulse. Steiner asks whether the individual actively apprehends the ethical idea that becomes the deed’s motive. He calls this apprehension moral intuition. It gives the action a consciously understood source within the individual.

This does not mean that any personal preference becomes right merely because it is mine. Steiner distinguishes an ethical idea apprehended in thinking from blind instinct. He treats freedom as a developing capacity: actual deeds can contain mixed motives, and an action is free to the extent that intuitive ethical content determines it.

Moral intuition does not automatically supply every detail of a deed. Moral imagination gives the ethical idea a concrete possible expression in a particular situation. Understanding an aim and finding an action that expresses it are related but different achievements.

Moral technique concerns practical realization. To change a situation, we need to understand its actual conditions and what can be done within them. A person may imagine a worthwhile action yet need someone else’s practical skill to carry it out. Conversely, being able to execute a plan does not by itself establish that its ethical motive was freely apprehended.

These terms belong to GA 4’s account of ethical action. Moral imagination means finding a concrete ethical possibility; it is not the supersensible Imagination discussed in Lesson 18. Moral intuition likewise should not be treated as an ordinary hunch or as proof that someone already possesses higher Intuition.

Key concept: Freedom and moral intuition

Steiner calls a deed free to the extent that its motive arises from an ethical idea individually apprehended through intuition, rather than merely from an imposed command or blind impulse.

Course example

Someone understands inclusion as an ethical aim, imagines an accessible meeting arrangement and checks participants’ actual needs and the available resources. These illustrate ethical idea, concrete form and practical realization. The illustration does not certify every proposed arrangement as morally right or every deed of its organizer as free.

Important distinction

Having an ethical idea, imagining its concrete expression and knowing how to realize it have different roles. Doing whatever one wants, or successfully carrying out someone else’s plan, is not by itself Steiner’s definition of freedom.
