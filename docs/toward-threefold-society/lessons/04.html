Course explanation

Understand the idea

He denies that the social question has suddenly appeared and can be settled permanently by a few people or by a parliamentary body. In his account, it belongs to modern civilization and must be resolved anew at each moment of historical development. Social institutions continually produce antisocial tendencies; an initial arrangement does not end the work of responding to them.

The hunger analogy makes this point concrete. A satiated organism becomes hungry again; no food removes hunger forever. Similarly, social order can move toward disorder, and no universal social panacea settles every future situation. The comparison concerns renewal over time. It does not provide a rule for assigning society’s institutions to anatomical organs.

His answer is to encourage communities through which people can continuously direct their activities socially. The self-governing spiritual-cultural sphere is one such community. Shared experience and the ability to judge according to its own values matter here: the institution should sustain people’s capacity to meet new situations, rather than merely preserve an initially convincing theory.

The paragraph then opens the economic question. Steiner distinguishes free self-administration in culture from associative work in economic life. Production, circulation and consumption satisfy needs, but each participant initially sees only part of the process. He separates knowing one’s own needs from judging what one should contribute through insight into the whole. This is why the next reading needs relationships among different economic participants.

Key concept

The social question is a recurring task of social renewal, not a condition abolished once by a perfect institutional formula.

Course example

Imagine a cultural community that makes a fair arrangement for its members, then welcomes people with different needs several years later. Its members must listen and judge again. This invented example illustrates why continuing participation matters even when the earlier arrangement was thoughtfully made.

Keep this distinction

Saying that problems return does not mean every arrangement is equally good or that action is futile. In Steiner’s argument it makes communities capable of repeated social judgment especially important.
