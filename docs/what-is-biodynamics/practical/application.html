Field application: quantities, stirring and spraying

Distinguish preparation dose, carrier-water volume and treated area. Then match equipment, plant stage and weather to the preparation.

Three quantities that must not be confused

Dose means the amount of mature preparation for an area. Carrier water is the liquid used to distribute that dose. Area is the ground surface being treated. Increasing carrier water does not automatically mean increasing preparation. Neither 500 nor 501 is irrigation or a bulk nutrient application.

For the worked metric example here, use 500 at 100 g/ha and 501 at 4 g/ha, each in 50 L/ha of carrier water. These are later manual rates, not universal values dictated in the 1924 lecture. The Bucher manual allows 25–50 L/ha; this calculator consistently uses its 50 L end. Brazilian ABD guidance also gives 4 g/ha in 50 L for 501.

Work the area calculation before mixing

One hectare is 10,000 m². Multiply the per-hectare quantity by your area divided by 10,000. A rectangular bed 5 m by 20 m is 100 m², or 0.01 ha. Keep units written beside the numbers so grams are not mistaken for millilitres.

The table and calculator below are arithmetic teaching aids for this one metric protocol. They are not a recommendation to measure a nearly invisible quantity with a kitchen spoon. For tiny silica doses, obtain an accurately measured portion, use a suitable precision balance, or work with a group treating a larger measured area. Keep the full stirring time even when the area is smaller.

Measure the area first. An acre is not a hectare. Retain the full one-hour stirring time.

Preparation amount: 10 g

Carrier water: 5 L

Calibrate with clean water first

  1. Mark a known area and fill the sprayer with a measured volume of clean water. Use the actual nozzle, pressure, walking speed and target pattern you intend to use.
  2. Spray the test area evenly and measure the water consumed. Scale this to the whole treated area. A pressure setting alone does not establish a volume per hectare.
  3. For 500, arrange a coarse soil-directed pattern; for 501, a fine mist directed at established foliage. Keep people, animals, nearby crops and water supplies out of drift.
  4. Check that the planned carrier volume can actually cover the site with your equipment. If it cannot, adjust equipment or use the supplier’s suitable carrier-volume guidance; do not silently change preparation dose.
  5. Clean the sprayer and filter, check for leaks and reserve enough time to apply the batch promptly after stirring. Equipment used for pesticides or other incompatible products requires appropriate decontamination; dedicated clean equipment avoids that problem.

The one-hour stirring sequence for 500 and 501

  1. Prepare clean water in a roomy stable vessel with enough space for vigorous movement without splashing out the measured dose. Use mildly warm rather than hot water, as the chosen maker directs.
  2. Add the separately measured preparation. Start the timer when the actual stirring sequence begins.
  3. Stir in one direction until a deep rotating funnel forms. Reverse decisively, breaking that pattern, then build a funnel in the opposite direction. Continue alternating formation and reversal.
  4. Continue for one full hour. Smaller areas change dose and carrier volume, not the duration. Mixing two preparations together is a separate protocol; do not infer permission from the fact both are stirred.
  5. Filter as required by the nozzle, maintaining the intended mixture. Apply promptly; do not plan to leave a stirred batch overnight or treat it as stored concentrate.
  6. Compost-valerian and horsetail methods have their own shorter stirring instructions. “All preparations need an hour” is incorrect.

When the conditions fit

  • 500: moist workable soil, commonly late afternoon or early evening around soil preparation, sowing, planting or seasonal transition. Do not spray edible surfaces or create runoff.
  • 501: established roots and appropriate foliage, commonly a calm early morning. Check crop restrictions; avoid frost risk, impending rain and severe heat or water stress.
  • For both: distinguish a typical practice window from a guarantee. A forecast, soil observation and crop stage are more useful than following a calendar date while ignoring unsuitable conditions.
  • Follow the actual product’s directions on repeat frequency and permitted use. Disease symptoms, nutrient shortage or water stress need their own diagnosis and management.

Why another supplier may give another amount

JPI describes 500 in a quarter-cup unit per acre and 501 in a teaspoon unit, each with a US-gallon carrier recipe. Those are product-specific volumetric directions. This guide’s metric example uses mass per hectare. A teaspoon is not automatically 4 g and a cup of moist manure does not have a universal mass. Follow one coherent protocol for the supplied product.

Prepared 500, 500P, barrel compound and proprietary starters may contain other materials. Their doses are not automatically those of plain horn manure. Likewise, a supplied packet of compost preparations is not defined by the mass of an arbitrary pinch.

Record what was actually done

  • Date, location, measured area, crop and growth stage; preparation identity, maker and batch.
  • Protocol, measured dose, carrier-water volume, water source, stirring start/end and spraying start/end.
  • Weather, wind, soil moisture, sprayer/nozzle and coverage; other management done at the same time.
  • Later observations, including no visible difference, adverse effects and a comparable untreated area where feasible. Keep interpretation separate from the observation.
