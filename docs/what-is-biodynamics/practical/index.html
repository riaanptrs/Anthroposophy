Practical guide: preparations 500–508

Learn what to make, when to make it, how to store it, and where and when to apply it. Begin with the three preparation families, then follow the dedicated method for each material.

Choose a preparation

500: horn manure — making and soil application

A manure-filled cow horn is matured underground through the local winter. The recovered material is later stirred in water for a soil-directed spray.

501: horn silica — making and foliar application

Quartz in a cow horn is exposed through the local summer. Its very small application dose is used as a fine mist on established plant growth.

502: yarrow in a stag bladder

Yarrow flowers pass through a summer exposure and then winter burial. The matured contents become a solid compost preparation.

503: chamomile in cattle intestine

Identified chamomile flowers are enclosed in prepared cattle intestine and matured through winter burial.

504: nettle — the full-year preparation

Nettle is the classical compost preparation without an animal sheath. Its distinguishing maturation period is a full year underground.

505: oak bark in a prepared animal skull

The classical method encloses crushed oak bark in a skull and exposes it to a wet, organic environment through winter. This requires a controlled making site.

506: dandelion in bovine mesentery

Dandelion flowers are wrapped in the specified bovine membrane and matured through winter burial. The membrane is not the stomach lining called tripe.

507: valerian flower liquid

Valerian is the liquid member of the compost set. It is made from flowers, not valerian-root medicine, and it is not buried in an animal organ.

508: horsetail — fresh and fermented decoctions

Learn two distinct horsetail methods. “Fresh tea” means freshly made decoction; it does not necessarily mean the starting herb was freshly harvested.

Start with the task, not the number

Making a preparation and making a spray are different operations. A winter-matured horn-manure preparation may spend months underground; its spray is mixed in water on the day of application. Dried chamomile flowers are an ingredient for 503, not the finished preparation. This guide keeps ingredients, mature preparations and application mixtures distinct.

The field preparations are horn manure 500 for soil and horn silica 501 for aerial plant parts. Preparations 502–507 form the compost family: five matured solids and one valerian liquid. Horsetail 508 is a separate herbal decoction, with fresh and fermented versions. Do not give all nine the same stirring time, target surface or storage treatment.

A practical learning sequence

  1. Read application and quantities first. Learn area, dose, carrier water, stirring and sprayer calibration before mixing a batch.
  2. Read the page for the preparation you intend to use. Make a materials list and identify the season, maturation period and application target.
  3. Read the shared storage page before obtaining finished material. A prepared substance can be spoiled by the wrong storage even when it was made well.
  4. For a first season, obtaining mature preparations from a reliable maker lets you learn application immediately. Making them in a regional group teaches identification, animal-sheath handling and assessment of maturity.
  5. Keep a dated record and a comparison area where practicable. Record ordinary soil care too. A crop response alone does not identify a spiritual or material cause.

Set up the work area

  • Use dedicated labelled containers, clean stirring tools, a timer, a measuring jug, a suitable balance, a strainer and a sprayer whose output you have measured with clean water.
  • Use gloves for manure, animal tissues and nettles; cover cuts, wash hands, and keep tools out of food preparation. Obtain animal materials through a lawful, traceable source and protect buried material from wildlife.
  • Use clean water and prevent runoff to wells, streams and irrigation supplies. A manure-derived preparation is not automatically hygienically safe because it looks earthy or has little smell. Check the applicable produce-safety and certification requirements before use on food crops.
  • Fine quartz dust can harm lungs. Obtain ready-made 501 or use a trained preparation-making group with appropriate dust controls. Do not dry-grind quartz in a kitchen blender, make airborne dust, or rely on an ordinary cloth face covering.
  • Check plant identity before harvest. Horsetail species are not interchangeable; the drawing in the course is an explanation, not an identification key.

What these instructions establish

These pages teach the classical methods described in the supplied books and identify later practitioner protocols where numerical examples are used. A detailed recipe explains a practice; it does not by itself establish that the claimed spiritual mechanism or a disease-control effect is true. Ordinary fertility, water management, plant diagnosis and food hygiene remain part of the work.

The guide is not a certification standard. Product concentrations, local seasons, permitted animal materials and crop restrictions can differ. Follow the protocol and label for the preparation actually in your hand. Where the books give broad indications rather than a fixed specification, the guide says so instead of inventing a universal value.
