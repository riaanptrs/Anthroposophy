500: horn manure — making and soil application

A manure-filled cow horn is matured underground through the local winter. The recovered material is later stirred in water for a soil-directed spray.

Materials and the intended role

Classical horn manure uses fresh cow manure and a sound cow horn. It is intended to support the soil/root side of farming within Steiner’s account. The small field dose is an addition to ordinary fertility management, not enough material to replace the crop’s nutrient supply.

  • Fresh, well-formed cow dung with straw, stones and other foreign matter removed. Record the animal source; do not substitute manure species casually.
  • Sound empty cow horns from a traceable preparation-making source; gloves, a dedicated filling tool and a labelled batch container.
  • A marked burial site with suitable soil, a site sketch and a retrieval record. Choose a place outside drainage channels and waterlogged ground.

Fill, bury and recover

  1. Prepare the horn and manure with the maker’s hygiene procedure. Fill to the tip with manure, using a narrow tool and gentle tapping to reduce empty cavities. Fill consistently without adding soil or compost to the contents.
  2. Label the batch and count the horns. Bury in autumn for the local winter; use the seasonal calendar rather than copying European months into the Southern Hemisphere.
  3. Choose depth with the local maker according to fertile soil depth, drainage and frost. As a later reference, the Bucher manual describes a pit around 50–60 cm in suitable soil; the supplied translations give differing historical depths. This is not a reason to dig into a saturated or unsuitable subsoil.
  4. Surround the horns with soil, backfill and mark the site so every horn can be found. Use the orientation shown by the local making protocol so water does not collect inside open horns.
  5. Leave through winter. In spring, uncover carefully, reconcile the count and empty each horn into a clean labelled container. Keep outside soil and sheath fragments out of the preparation.
  6. Have the first batch assessed by an experienced maker. Mature material is usually dark, cohesive and earthy; recognizably raw, green or strongly manure-like material needs further assessment. Colour and smell are quality observations, not pathogen tests.

Store the concentrate, not a diluted spray

Keep the recovered material cool, dark and moist in its own labelled non-food container, protected from drying, freezing and contamination. Do not leave it in a sunlit window with 501. Follow the maker’s lid and storage-box instructions. Write down the retrieval date and inspect regularly. The shared storage page distinguishes moisture from waterlogging.

How and when to apply

  1. Check that 500 is suitable for the intended food-crop use and observe applicable manure-derived-input restrictions. Keep it off edible plant surfaces.
  2. Measure the treated area and use the application guide’s clearly identified metric example or the actual supplier’s directions. Do not translate a spoonful into grams without knowing the material’s density.
  3. Add the measured concentrate to clean, mildly warm water in a roomy vessel. Stir with repeated vortex formation and reversal for one full hour. Filter for the nozzle after stirring.
  4. Apply promptly to soil in coarse droplets, preferably late afternoon or early evening when soil is moist and conditions allow coverage without drift or runoff. Typical opportunities are soil preparation, sowing, planting and the start or end of the growing season.
  5. Use the target and carrier-water volume to calibrate the equipment. The aim is even soil coverage, not washing the plants or irrigating the plot. If heavy rain, frozen ground, drought or saturated soil make the application unsuitable, choose a better window.

Worked decision: a dry seedbed

If a seedbed is powder-dry, choosing the right calendar symbol does not solve the moisture problem. First address soil and water conditions. Then calculate the dose for the actual area and plan a suitable evening application. Do not double the preparation because the soil is dry or omit ordinary organic-material management.
