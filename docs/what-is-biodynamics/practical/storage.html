Storage, batch quality and working records

Keep mature preparations distinct from raw ingredients and diluted mixtures. Storage requirements differ across the preparation families.

Label three different stages

An ingredient jar, a finished-preparation jar and a diluted spray container should never carry an ambiguous label such as “nettle” or “500 mix”. Include identity, stage, source, batch, date and, for diluted material, the dilution and time mixed. A plant infusion or a proprietary combined product should be named as such.

Keep a small inventory so the amount on hand matches planned work. Obtain only what you can store correctly, and follow the actual maker’s expiry and handling directions. Classical storage traditions vary; the practical aim is to preserve identity and condition without contamination, desiccation, excess water or dangerous pressure.

Different materials need different conditions

  • 500 and the five mature solids 502–506: separate labelled jars, cool and dark, retaining their intended moisture without sitting in water. Follow the maker’s breathable-lid and insulated-box arrangement; keep insulation outside the preparation.
  • 501: store the mature mineral dry in its specified glass container in a light location. Do not place it in the moist dark storage used for 500. Avoid releasing dry quartz dust.
  • 507: mature valerian liquid belongs in its own cool, light-protected bottle. Vent active fermentation safely; close as directed once stable. Reassess renewed gas production or a changed batch rather than storing pressure in an airtight container.
  • 508 ingredient: dry identified herb is separate from brewed or fermented liquid. Keep the herb dry, labelled and protected from contamination. Brew fresh material for the planned work; follow the specific maker’s storage directions for any fermented concentrate.
  • Diluted 500 and 501: mix for the application session and use promptly. A bottle left overnight is not a reserve of the original mature preparation.

Inspect condition without claiming a safety test

Inspect the label, moisture, appearance, pests, container damage and unexpected change before taking a portion. Use clean dedicated tools and do not return a contaminated scoop to the reserve. Keep an uncertain batch separate and ask the maker to assess it. Do not restore an unknown spoiled material merely by adding water or another preparation.

Traditional maturity signs such as earthy texture are useful descriptive observations. They do not prove absence of pathogens or establish a food-crop harvest interval. Animal-derived materials and compost must meet the applicable hygiene requirements for their intended use. Keep them away from kitchen utensils, food, drinking water and animal feed.

A making record that can be checked later

  • Preparation number and name; plant identification, harvest date and material supplier.
  • Animal-sheath source, preparation method, ingredient amounts and any named departure from the classical recipe.
  • Site plan, depth or container arrangement, burial/exposure date, expected retrieval season and number of units.
  • Recovery date, quantity recovered, observations, assessor and any rejected or incomplete portion.
  • Storage location, condition checks, batches supplied to which field or compost pile, and remaining inventory.
