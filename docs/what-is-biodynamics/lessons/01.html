Farm individuality, rhythms and preparations

By the end of this lesson: Connect farm individuality, timing and the distinct preparation families.

A farm works through connections

A farm individuality is built through connections. Imagine crops providing animal feed, animals contributing manure, compost returning organic material to soil, and hedges affecting shelter and habitats. Each connection changes what another part can do. Courtney places this whole-farm view within a stronger idea: Earth itself is a living being within a living cosmos. The practical connection and the spiritual premise belong together in his account.

Apply the idea to your own scale

The first question is therefore: what can this particular place sustain, and how can its parts support one another? A garden without livestock can still study its crop residues, soil, water, surroundings and sources of organic material. The concept guides attention to relationships; it does not demand that a beginner acquire animals or pretend that purchased inputs have no origin outside the garden.

Why timing needs an explanation

The second feature is timing. Day and night, seasons and weather already matter to growth. Courtney additionally discusses proposed relationships with Moon, planets and zodiac. Different calendars use different reference systems: seasonal signs, equal divisions against the stars, or unequal astronomical constellations. A date in one system is not automatically the same indication in another. Courtney acknowledges that reported calendar effects have not always been reproduced.

Three preparation families

The third feature is the preparations: materials given distinct intended roles after particular treatments. Learn their families before their numbers. Horn manure, later called 500, addresses the soil side; horn silica, 501, the plant side. Six compost preparations, 502–507, are small additions intended to influence manure or compost. They are differentiated supports within farming, not six bulk fertilizers supplying the crop’s nutrient requirements.

Where horsetail fits

Horsetail, often numbered 508, belongs to a different discussion about excessive water-mediated vitality and fungal growth. Courtney argues strongly for including it with the others; he also reports disagreement about its status. The drawing below gives his nine-preparation grouping. Its bracket marks the six compost preparations. Counting nine entries should not lead you to treat every material as interchangeable or prepared in the same way.

Bring the three features together

Put the three features into one explanation: organize the living relationships of the place, consider timing, and understand the intended role of the preparation being used. Ordinary soil care, appropriate organic-material handling and suitable crop management still matter. A small amount of a preparation is not presented in the agricultural discussion as a replacement for ordinary manuring. Its proposed effect and a measured crop response are separate questions.

Worked example: choosing the question first

  1. A learner says: “My soil needs organic matter, so should I use a compost preparation instead?”
  2. First identify the need: the supply and condition of organic material belong to ordinary soil management.
  3. Then identify the proposed addition: the compost preparations are intended to influence that material’s activity. Explaining their role does not make them a substitute for the material itself.

Two questions — optional

Why is a preparation not simply another name for manure?

See an explanation

Manure is a material used in soil fertility. A preparation is a specially treated material assigned a particular role, often used in a very small amount to influence processes. The source retains ordinary manuring.

Why should a calendar name its reference system?

See an explanation

Seasonal signs, equal stellar divisions and visible constellation boundaries organize the sky differently. Without the reference system, an instruction can be misunderstood. Courtney also retains uncertainty about reproducibility.

Course explanation

Understand the idea

Calling a farm an individuality asks us to look at the relationships among soil, crops, animals and human work. For Courtney this begins from a stronger spiritual premise: the Earth itself is a living being within a living cosmos. The whole-farm perspective and that premise belong together in his explanation, even though they ask different kinds of questions.

His second feature relates agricultural work to the Sun, Moon, planets and their rhythms. He distinguishes seasonal tropical signs, equal-sector sidereal divisions and astronomical constellation boundaries. He also says that some practitioners have not reproduced reported calendar results and that much research remains. The course therefore teaches the distinction without supplying a universal planting schedule.

His third and most decisive feature is the preparations. His Figure 1 groups horn manure and horn silica, numbers 500 and 501, as contrasting preparations; yarrow, chamomile, nettle, oak bark, dandelion and valerian, 502–507, as the six compost preparations; and horsetail, 508, as an additional preparation. He acknowledges disagreement about whether horsetail should carry that status and argues strongly for including it.

Courtney regards these preparations as a way of working with living forces rather than simply adding nutrient quantities. He describes materials and reports practical experiences, but this chapter is his introductory argument. Its reports and proposed spiritual explanation must be identified separately from controlled evidence of agricultural outcomes.

Key concept

In Courtney’s account, individuality, celestial relationships and preparations form one approach; the preparations give that approach its distinctive practical expression.

Course example

Draw a garden map showing compost, soil, crops, animals and the gardener. Add a second column for Courtney’s spiritual premises and a third for the preparations he names. The map helps explain his proposal without confusing a relationship diagram with an experiment.

Keep this distinction

Organic and biodynamic are not interchangeable terms in this source. Nor does every biodynamic practitioner necessarily accept Courtney’s emphasis on all nine preparations or his interpretation of their effects.
