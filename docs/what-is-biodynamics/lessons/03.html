Learning to perceive nature: the first spiritual beings

This first lecture gives the spiritual background that the collection places before the agricultural lectures. Its question is how a person’s relation to nature changes when spiritual science becomes a schooling of attention and feeling. Course explanation

Understand the idea

Steiner includes sensory experience, thoughts about it and outward actions in the physical world. An action can carry a moral spiritual impulse and still occur in that physical field. He therefore does not describe two sealed-off worlds. He says spiritual science changes interests, feelings and habits of attention; wholehearted study already begins this self-education, even before formal occult development. Mere sentimental declarations of love are not enough in his account.

He then describes a movement from a vivid sensory impression to what he calls moral perception. Blue sky gives way to devotion to infinity; green vegetation to an inward sense of creative thinking; white snow to a feeling for matter. A tone and its octave are connected with the concord of wish and reason. Here “moral” includes these inward qualities. These are the experiences Steiner proposes under stated conditions, rather than results established by the course.

The proposed human etheric or life body has a connected, unitary form. Nature’s etheric life is instead a differentiated world of many beings. This difference matters: a spiritually living Earth is not simply one enlarged human life body. The first class presents definite outlines and is encountered, he says, behind earthly substance and especially metals, after warmth and other underground impressions have been allowed to affect the observer.

The second class continually transforms. Steiner associates it with mist, spray, cloud and rain, and then with the first green shoots. He claims that these beings descend from the surrounding world and draw plants out of the ground. This is more than saying that rain helps growth. The editor calls the first two groups gnomes and undines in her synopsis; the lecture passages here chiefly characterize their forms, elements and work.

Steiner calls his initial approach empirical because he presents what he regards as facts of occult vision before gathering them into a theory. He distinguishes that reporting from beginning with fairy tales. Understanding this sequence does not establish that the reported beings are externally observable or that the lecture’s historical description of physical science represents present botany. Its actual spiritual causation claims should remain visible and attributed.

Key concept

The lecture moves from the self-education of attention and feeling to an asserted plurality of nature beings behind sensory appearances.

Course example

A learner watches a young shoot and writes two separate notes: “its leaves unfolded over several days” and “the green colour prompted an idea about renewal.” These are an observation and a personal response. A third note can identify Steiner’s claim about beings drawing plants upward. Keeping the three statements separate makes the argument easier to examine; the example does not verify that claim.

Keep this distinction

A sense impression, an inward response and a claimed supersensible being are different kinds of statement. The lecture’s method is not interchangeable with a controlled botanical investigation, and learning its concepts does not require reproducing its meditative experiences.
