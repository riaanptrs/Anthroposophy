What this passage means

Steiner connects temperament with the individual person encountered in ordinary life. The surrounding passage describes an underlying colouring; the later mixture qualification prevents a pure-type reading.

The focus of this lesson

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

The reading’s key idea

Steiner begins with each individual as a riddle. Temperament is an underlying colouring of personality that manifests in everyday conduct, but his explanation of its origin is spiritual: inherited constitution meets an individuality developing through repeated earthly lives. Later he says pure types never occur; a basic temperament is mingled with the others. Understanding this colouring should support a response to the particular person.

Read a person’s temperament within the whole lecture

The individual riddle

The opening moves from humanity’s general mystery to the particular person we meet. Steiner calls temperament an underlying colouring that enters everyday encounters. Even people described by the same basic type differ enough to pose a separate riddle. The reader’s first task is therefore to notice how a disposition comes to expression in a person’s conduct, rather than assuming that a category exhausts the person. This opening gives the practical subject of the lecture: understanding another human being in the details of life.

Native capture 6, columns 1–2.

Why spiritual science enters the explanation

Steiner first acknowledges that temperament manifests outwardly, then argues that ordinary observation cannot explain its origin. His proposed explanation joins inherited characteristics with an enduring individuality bringing the fruits of previous earthly lives. Temperament mediates these contributions and shapes the relationship of four human members. This spiritual premise belongs to the argument itself. Reading the lecture accurately requires understanding that premise even when we do not accept its account of reincarnation. The observed conduct and the proposed supersensible cause remain different kinds of statement.

Native captures 6, columns 2–3; 7, column 3; 8, columns 1–2.

Types, mixtures and direction

Later Steiner explicitly says that the four fundamental types never occur in pure form: one basic temperament is mingled with degrees of the others. He also values their diversity, arguing that education should direct them rather than eliminate them. The portraits therefore supply pictures of tendencies and possible one-sidedness within his framework. They do not divide humanity into four complete, exclusive kinds. His physiological, bodily appearance and illness claims must be recognized as parts of this historical account; the course does not turn them into personality tests.

Native captures 10, column 3; 11, columns 1–2.

From understanding to a response

The closing returns to each human encounter. Steiner proposes that understanding should become a receptive ability to offer the quality another person needs, with love as the social fruit of spiritual science. The course’s fictional colleague and garden example is a later application of this concern with particular circumstances. Describing conduct, considering an interpretation and trying a revisable response makes that application concrete. See the idea in an example

A usually quiet colleague becomes energetic when helping with a garden. Another, lively at lunch, works patiently on a repair. One label does not explain either person completely.

The example encourages attention to situations and mixtures. It does not establish that either colleague has one particular spiritual constitution.
