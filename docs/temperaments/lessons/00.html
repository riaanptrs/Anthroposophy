GA 57 · Lesson 00 · 1909-03-04

Meet the person before the type

Use the four temperaments as a subject of study without reducing people to labels.

Start with a situation

A usually quiet colleague becomes energetic when helping with a garden. Another, lively at lunch, works patiently on a repair. One label does not explain either person completely.

The example encourages attention to situations and mixtures. It does not establish that either colleague has one particular spiritual constitution.

The reading’s key idea

Steiner opens with the individuality of every person. A temperament is an underlying colouring of personality in his account, expressed through responses to the world. The four names describe basic patterns, but the lecture explicitly says that pure types do not occur in life. Its aim is practical understanding of encounters and development.

This short book contains one lecture, given in Berlin on 4 March 1909. Our lessons divide its argument into study sections; these are not chapters written by Steiner. We distinguish his spiritual explanation, the behaviour he describes and our own teaching examples. The source’s physiological and illness claims are historical claims, not a diagnostic system used by this course.

From observation to response

  1. Describe the situation and behaviour.
  2. Distinguish the interpretation and look for a counterexample.
  3. Offer a concrete response and check its fit.
Keep this idea

A type may organize a question; the person remains the subject of understanding.

Reading: Capture pages 6–7; 19–20.

Source: The Four Temperaments, Berlin lecture, 4 March 1909 (GA 57). Capture of the 2012 English edition, B. Kelly translation revised by Matthew Barton. Pages and lines are missing; the parallel 1987 edition was consulted. Lessons and examples are original teaching divisions.

Words for the reading

Temperament: underlying disposition in this lecture. Individuality: the particular person, whose life exceeds a general category.

Work with the idea

Describe a fictional person in two different situations without using a temperament name. Separate what the person does from what you infer. Keep this description for the final lesson.

Check your understanding

Is the course organized around several original lectures?

Show a suggested answer

No. It divides a single lecture into original teaching sections.

Does recognizing a pattern explain the whole person?

Show a suggested answer

No. The lecture itself describes mixtures and individual differences.

What is a useful first notebook entry?

Show a suggested answer

“She spoke little during introductions but asked detailed questions about the plants” describes behaviour. “She is incapable of interest” adds an unsupported judgment.

How to assess your response

Check four things: correct attribution, a clear distinction, a reason supported by the reading and recognition of the example’s limits. Revisit anything vague. Reasoned disagreement can demonstrate understanding; agreement and reports of spiritual experiences are not required.
