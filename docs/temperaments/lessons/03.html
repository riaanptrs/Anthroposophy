GA 57 · Lesson 03 · 1909-03-04

Choleric: purpose meeting resistance

Understand the movement from forceful insistence toward capable action.

What is central to Steiner’s choleric portrait?

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

The reading’s key idea

In Steiner’s choleric portrait, predominance of the I-bearer appears as forceful will, insistence on one’s own way and the wish to overcome obstacles. His proposed bodily correlate is blood circulation. The lesser danger he names is uncontrolled temper; the greater is a foolishly blinkered pursuit of one aim. Education begins with respect for an educator’s worth and competence and gives will real resistance to encounter.

The source says the child should trust the teacher’s grasp of what is happening and encounter challenging situations rather than an entirely easy life. These are actual proposals in the lecture. The course’s bridge construction is a later adaptation: a manageable task, demonstrable skill and a clear boundary. Honest correction of an error is our account of competence. Body size, eye colour and gait in the portrait remain attributed physiognomic claims, not ways to classify a learner.

Trace choleric will from constitution to education

Purposeful assertion in the source

The choleric portrait follows directly from predominance of the I-bearer. Steiner associates the I with blood circulation and says that its predominance appears as forceful will, aggressiveness and insistence on having one’s own way. He later describes an impulse to overcome obstacles and make one’s presence felt. This is his spiritual and physiological explanation of the tendency, not simply another name for anger. Its central movement is purposeful assertion encountering resistance; anger becomes one possible expression when that movement cannot adjust.

Native captures 8, column 3; 9, column 3.

Appearance, mixture and one-sidedness

Fichte and Napoleon illustrate Steiner’s claim that a predominant I holds back the growth of other members. He extends the portrait to facial form, a firm step and dark eyes, while qualifying that relative proportion matters rather than a universal shortness rule. These are attributed physiognomic claims, not course identification criteria. Napoleon later also illustrates mixture with the phlegmatic tendency. In the account of dangers, uncontrolled temper is the lesser problem; the greater is a foolishly blinkered pursuit of one aim. This links one-sidedness with narrowed purpose as well as anger.

Native captures 9, column 3; 10, columns 1 and 3; 11, column 1.

Competence and actual difficulty

For education Steiner proposes respect and esteem for natural authority. The choleric child should trust the educator’s grasp of what is happening; apparent incompetence dissolves that authority. Worth and competence are therefore the relational starting point. Outwardly the child should encounter challenging situations, resistance and difficulty rather than an entirely easy life. Both parts matter: the adult demonstrates a grasp of the task while the task gives will something real to work on. This is the source’s proposal, not an established outcome guaranteed by assigning a child a type.

Native captures 11, column 3; 12, column 1.

The course’s construction application

The bridge-building example adapts this proposal into a manageable challenge and a visible demonstration of skill. A clear boundary keeps the materials in use; testing a joint lets effort meet the demands of construction. The teacher may correct an error honestly, which is our practical account of competence rather than the source’s insistence on unfailing confidence. For adult self-education, Steiner separately proposes finding situations where rage is useless and leads into a cul-de-sac. The common movement is to arrange an encounter in which insistence must learn from what the situation actually requires.

Native capture 13, column 1; construction scenario is an original course application.

Connect the parts of the reading

Why does the choleric educational proposal need both a competent educator and resistance in the task?

An explained answer: Competence gives the child someone whose grasp of things can be respected. Real difficulty gives forceful will something to work through rather than a life shaped only by insistence. The course’s construction example turns these two source elements into a manageable task and visible guidance.

Childs adds the question of feedback

In Chapter 3 of Understand Your Temperament!, Gilbert Childs distinguishes appreciation from flattery while describing determination and pride; Chapter 7 adds that initiative may need experienced advice and correction. Extend the construction example by naming a real contribution, a specific defect and a joint check. That joint check is an original adaptation: useful authority includes the ability to receive correction.

Words for the reading

Purposefulness: pursuing an aim. Resistance: a task’s genuine difficulty. Competence: ability shown in practice, including correction of mistakes.

See the idea in an example

During a model-building activity, a learner insists the bridge must stand immediately. The educator calmly demonstrates a stable joint and offers a manageable construction problem.

Effort now meets a task that responds to skill rather than shouting. This illustrates redirection of energy, not a diagnosis of the learner.
