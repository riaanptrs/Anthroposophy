Self-education through an indirect route

Compare the four and clarify the birth-date question →

By the end of this lesson: Explain the indirect role of reason and distinguish the historical proposals from a voluntary course adaptation.

Begin with the experience

“I must stop interrupting” is an intention, but it does not yet change the moment when you rush to respond. A small arrangement can make another response easier to practise.

Understand the concept

Indirect self-education gives reason a different job from simply issuing commands. Telling yourself “never lose interest again” asks a resolution to defeat a recurring habit at every moment. Arranging a manageable situation changes what you can actually practise. Reason selects the conditions; the existing tendency supplies energy for the activity.

Follow the relationship

The historical text proposes particular detours for each temperament, including experiences of limited interest, resistance, suffering and boredom. The general structure is to work through an existing disposition rather than replace it by an order. Our voluntary course exercises adapt that structure into small, honest and useful tasks; they do not reproduce every historical proposal.

Develop the explanation

For seven days, optionally record one ordinary event: what happened, what you noticed or wanted, what you did, how quickly the reaction began and how long it lasted. Include context such as fatigue or pressure, one strength and one difficulty. This is a course reflection, not a temperament score.

Examine the response

Choose one experiment: hear two suggestions before taking charge, finish one small step before changing projects, take one specific role instead of waiting, or connect a lingering concern with one manageable act. Select the experiment by the situation, not by a permanent type.

Try a considerate adjustment

At the end, compare a similar event and an exception. Did the change help, and what else may explain the result? Steiner's indirect self-education works with existing tendencies, but the historical prescriptions below are not identical to these modest voluntary practices. No change is also useful information.

A worked example, step by step

1. The situation

Someone who begins many activities repeatedly resolves to concentrate for an entire afternoon and repeatedly fails.

2. What helps explain it

The resolution has not changed the size or arrangement of the task. A ten-minute task with one visible result provides a different condition for practising completion.

3. A possible response

Choose one short task, set aside competing materials and finish its agreed result. Then review what helped. The plan changes the practice situation while retaining curiosity and the ability to begin.

Original course example for understanding the idea; not a book quotation or a classification test.

Two questions — optional

What role does reason have in an indirect practice?

See an explanation

It chooses and arranges conditions in which an existing capacity can be used differently. It does more than repeat a command against a habit.

Does an indirect route mean that reasoning has no role?

See an explanation

No. Reason helps choose and arrange conditions. The distinction is between directing a habit by a command alone and creating a situation in which a different use of an existing capacity can be practised.

The same principle does not make the arrangements identical

In the child-education passage, Steiner proposes that the phlegmatic child encounter companions with varied interests. In the adult self-education passage, he instead proposes deliberate boredom. Both are presented as indirect ways of working with a tendency already present, but the person, relationship and arrangement differ.

Our course’s short comparison task is a separate learning adaptation. Naming the shared principle does not make it a verbatim prescription or allow one historical proposal to replace the other.

Change the case: A reader takes the adult passage about boredom and concludes that the preceding child-education passage recommends depriving a child of interesting companions.

Which distinction corrects this reading?

Answer before opening the explanation.

See an explained answer

Keep the passages separate. The child proposal works through companions’ varied interests; the adult proposal concerns a person arranging their own circumstances. A shared indirect principle does not erase that difference. The conclusion about depriving a child does not follow from the child passage.

The Four Temperaments, GA 57, Berlin, 4 March 1909; supplied captures 12–13. Child education and adult self-education are distinct passages.

Full reading, sources and further practice — optional

Reading the historical claims

The retained readings contain historical claims about bodily appearance, physiology, illness and ways of educating or handling people. These are attributed claims, not established diagnostic criteria or instructions to follow. Do not infer temperament from body shape, eye colour, ethnicity, sex, disability or birthday. Melancholic does not mean depression, and sanguine does not mean ADHD.

Constitutional charts are source- and age-specific: the 1909 pairings must not be applied universally to children. GA 295 explicitly gives different child pairings. Historical proposals involving fabricated distress, deliberate frustration, deprivation or coercion are not adopted as course practices. Use honest, respectful and revisable responses.

What useful role does Steiner give reason in self-education?

1 · Read the passage

Read the supporting excerpt

Rudolf Steiner · The Four Temperaments · GA 57 · Berlin, 4 March 1909 · Lecture, PDF capture 13, column 1; printed folio not visible.

Our logical minds can accomplish little when pitted against stronger forces such as the temperaments, but working indirectly, on the other hand, can achieve a great deal.

Sophia Books / Rudolf Steiner Press, published 2012; translated by B. Kelly (first English publication 1987), revised by Matthew Barton, translation revision copyright 2008. Selected wording from the supplied PDF; line breaks normalized.

What this passage means

Steiner assigns reason an indirect arranging role rather than excluding it. The four adult examples specify the conditions to be arranged; they are distinct from the course’s limited fictional comparison task. Exact wording visually checked in the left column’s opening paragraph.

The focus of this lesson

Explain the role of reason in arranging experiences, and distinguish the adult proposals from the child-education routes.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

The reading’s key idea

Steiner argues that direct reproach and exhortation accomplish little against temperament. Reason instead creates experiences suited to what is already present. He proposes brief suitable situations for sanguine attention, situations where choleric rage leads nowhere, seeking life’s pain through compassion for melancholics, and thorough boredom through uninteresting occupations for phlegmatics. These are his concrete adult examples, followed by a return to the principle of reckoning with what one is.

The whole passage is present in capture 13. The adult phlegmatic example deliberately differs from the varied interests of child playmates. Our five-minute comparison task is a chosen course adaptation of indirect arrangement. It does not reproduce the choleric, melancholic or phlegmatic prescriptions or turn the source’s language of cure into medical guidance.

Reason arranges an indirect route

From the educator to the adult self

At the end of the child-education passage, Steiner says that the principles can also be used for self-education. The following examples concern adults arranging experiences for themselves. He opens with the sanguine person who gains little by reproaching himself. Direct logical argument, he says, accomplishes little against stronger forces such as the temperaments. This does not remove reason from the task. Reason and intelligence receive an indirect role: selecting or creating circumstances in which an existing tendency can work in a suitable way.

PDF capture 12, right bottom; 13, left.

Give brevity a fitting place; let rage find its limit

The sanguine example asks the person to accept the tendency rather than keep issuing useless exhortations. Even small experiences can be designed in which short attention is appropriate. The choleric example asks reason to find situations where rage is of no use and leads into a self-prolonging dead end. Both arrangements make a tendency encounter particular conditions, but they do so differently. One gives brief responsiveness a fitting task; the other confronts forceful anger with its inability to achieve anything in the selected situation.

PDF capture 13, left.

Compassion and tedium are the other examples

The melancholic adult is told to face and seek life’s pain, then redirect suffering outward through compassion. This echoes the child passage while assigning the arrangement to the person himself. The phlegmatic adult is told to occupy himself with uninteresting things and surround himself with many sources of tedium until thoroughly bored. This differs sharply from giving the child playmates with varied interests. Steiner claims that boredom will break the habit of ‘phlegma’. His language of cure belongs to this historical proposal and supplies no medical treatment instruction here.

PDF capture 13, left bottom → middle.

Keep source proposal and course exercise legible

The course’s five-minute comparison task illustrates one limited version of indirect arrangement: variety has a useful place, followed by a written conclusion. The time limit, chosen materials and review question are original course decisions. They should not conceal the stronger details of Steiner’s other examples, nor be presented as his actual wording. The common principle is to reckon with what one is rather than what one is not. Read each arrangement by asking what tendency it assumes, what reason changes, and what result Steiner intends.

PDF capture 13, left/middle.

Connect the parts of the reading

How does the indirect role of reason differ from a command to change one’s temperament?

An explained answer: A command confronts the tendency directly through reproach or exhortation. The indirect role uses reason to find and create suitable conditions: a fitting place for brief attention, a limit to rage, outward compassion, or the source’s proposed tedium. The arrangement, rather than the command alone, is meant to bring change.

Keep this idea

In this argument, reason works indirectly by arranging conditions rather than merely issuing commands to the self.

Words for the reading

Indirect route: changing arrangements so that an existing tendency works in a suitable situation. Exhortation: a command or appeal to change directly. Self-education: the adult application distinguished from educating a child.

Read → understand → explain → apply. The chapter explanation is open from the start.

See the idea in an example

A learner repeatedly says “I must stop changing subjects.” Instead, they choose a five-minute comparison task in which looking at three examples is useful, followed by one written conclusion.

The arrangement gives variety a place and still asks for completion. Whether it helps must be assessed from the result, not assumed from a temperament label.
