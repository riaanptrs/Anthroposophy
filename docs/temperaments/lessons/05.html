GA 57 · Lesson 05 · 1909-03-04

Phlegmatic: finding an invitation to participate

Explain how inner well-being and reflected interest connect in Steiner’s phlegmatic account.

1 · Read the passage

Begin with the book

Rudolf Steiner · The Four Temperaments · GA 57 · Berlin, 4 March 1909 · Lecture, PDF capture 12, column 3; printed folio not visible.

Phlegmatic children learn by sharing in these interests, the more the better.

Sophia Books / Rudolf Steiner Press, published 2012; translated by B. Kelly (first English publication 1987), revised by Matthew Barton, translation revision copyright 2008. Selected wording from the supplied PDF; line breaks normalized.

What this passage means

Steiner’s child-education proposal makes companions’ varied interests the route into participation. The following paragraph also gives impassivity fitting objects; the adult tedium proposal is separate. Exact wording visually checked in the right column’s first paragraph.

The focus of this lesson

Explain how inner well-being and reflected interest connect in Steiner’s phlegmatic account.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

The reading’s key idea

Steiner associates this temperament with predominance of the etheric or life body. He describes inward well-being and attention to internal processes, leaving external events to take their course. His educational proposal uses companions with varied interests: the child becomes interested through seeing those interests reflected in others. He also proposes giving impassivity objects and events toward which it is fitting.

The description is present in capture 9; the child-education passage is complete in 12. All four members still belong to the person. The associations with metabolism, glands, bodily build and gait belong to Steiner’s spiritual physiology. Our seed-planting example is an ordinary fictional adaptation; it does not reproduce the whole proposal or establish a person’s constitution.

Inner well-being and the mediation of companions

Read the inward description first

The phlegmatic picture begins before the educational advice. Steiner assigns predominance to the etheric or life body, whose activity he connects with growth and metabolism. In his description, the person feels inward well-being and is absorbed in internal processes while external events take their course. This is a proposed relation among four members belonging to the same person. It is not an absence of astral responsiveness or of the I, and the source’s physiological correspondences remain part of its spiritual account.

PDF captures 8, middle/right; 9, middle.

Why another person’s interest matters

The educational passage proposes that the phlegmatic child should grow up with playmates who have many different interests. Steiner explicitly acknowledges that all children need playmates, then gives companionship a particular function here. The child may not be moved readily by things themselves, yet can encounter those things through an enthusiastic companion. Interest is first reflected in another person and then reflected in the child’s own soul. The arrangement changes the route into the world rather than simply demanding more outward activity.

PDF capture 12, middle bottom → right.

An additional arrangement often overlooked

Companionship is not the whole child-education proposal. Steiner also asks the educator to introduce objects and events toward which a phlegmatic attitude is fitting. His intention is to give impassivity an appropriate object, as part of the general principle of working with what is already present. He does not offer a concrete list of such objects in this passage. The adult advice later takes a different form: deliberately surrounding oneself with uninteresting occupations and tedium. Keeping the child and adult passages separate preserves the argument.

PDF capture 12, right; 13, middle.

Read the course example as an adaptation

The course’s fictional seed-planting example gives the learner a companion’s enthusiasm and a small possible contribution. It illustrates mediated interest. Its choice of manageable roles and room to decline belongs to the course, while it leaves the fitting-object proposal unexplored. Use that distinction to compare source and application accurately. Quiet observation alone does not establish the fourfold explanation or show that a learner needs correction. The reading task is to explain the proposed mechanism and purpose before deciding what an ordinary example actually demonstrates.

PDF captures 9, middle; 12, right.

Connect the parts of the reading

Why can companions’ interests work differently from simply placing an interesting object before the phlegmatic child?

An explained answer: Steiner says the child is not readily affected by things directly, but can encounter an interest reflected in another person and then take it into his own soul. The companion mediates the relation; he also adds fitting objects of impassivity as a separate arrangement.

An invitation can include preparation time

Childs’s Chapter 5 describes privacy and time to prepare a response; Chapter 7 gives the example of a useful suggestion withheld because nobody asked. Extend the shared-activity invitation with advance notice and a choice of speaking or writing. These are possibilities to check with the person, not requirements inferred from a label.

Words for the reading

Etheric or life body: the formative life member in Steiner’s account. Reflected interest: interest encountered through another person. Impassivity: the stillness he also proposes directing toward suitable objects.

Read → understand → explain → apply. The chapter explanation is open from the start.

See the idea in an example

A learner watches a seed-planting activity without joining. A companion explains a favourite plant and offers the learner a choice between filling a pot and making its label.

The invitation connects an activity with a relationship and a manageable contribution. It does not require competition, public pressure or assigning a permanent temperament.
