Explore the chapter’s core ideas

The reading’s key idea

Steiner begins with waking, dreaming and sleeping, understood through the relation of ego and astral body to physical and etheric bodies. In sleep, he says, the ego and astral body belong to the outer world; its colours and sounds are experienced free of the attachment to heavy objects characteristic of waking perception. This spiritual account introduces qualitative counterparts to weight, measure and number: expansion into cosmic space instead of downward gravity; red measuring yellow through its own expansion and change; and numbers whose members require one another. Such relations lead, in his account, to the beings expressing themselves through qualities. His point is broader than a small patch appearing visually heavy: he proposes another way of knowing reality.

The lecture assigns truth to sleep, beauty to dream and goodness to waking. Sleep supplies affinity with outer things; dream forces become memory and artistic weaving; waking permits a distinction between self and world and the choice to obey or refuse. Beauty arises by forming chaos into cosmos. Steiner then traces an artistic change from the weightless gold of icons and Cimabue through Giotto and Raphael to the Madonna's earthly weight and later naturalism. Renewal cannot simply copy the old icon: it must consciously recover free colour. He acknowledges quantitative science's achievements while arguing that reduction to quantities loses the qualities from which art works. These spiritual and historical interpretations organize the lecture's call to paint out of colour.

From consciousness to a conscious art of colour

Why consciousness comes first

The lecture begins with three human states rather than a painting exercise. Steiner attributes the distinction between inner and outer world to the ego and astral body's departure from the physical and etheric bodies during sleep. They then share the existence of external things; on waking they retain an affinity with them. His animal comparison belongs to this spiritual account. Notice the qualification about insomnia: the proposed condition is an organism adapted for sleep, not success in sleeping every night. The account therefore makes the capacity for sleep foundational to truth as a faithful inner image of something outside.

Captures 123–124 and 126–127.

Qualities become relations

Earthly objects are counted, measured and weighed, while colour and tone seem to adhere to them. Steiner asks the reader to imagine these qualities moving freely. Their weight becomes an expansive anti-gravity; red measures a larger yellow formation through its own expansion and change; number expresses a need for particular companions rather than an indifferent sum. These are claimed spiritual counterparts of quantities, leading to beings active within qualities. The drawings show bound forms below and free activity above, with hatching identified as purple, yellow and red. Their monochrome appearance and fragmented OCR make the accompanying text indispensable.

Captures 125–126, including their diagrams.

Truth, beauty and goodness have different conditions

Dream forces become memory when one returns into the body. In art, the same weaving of sympathy and antipathy becomes beauty, with a small transition into bodily participation. Chaos names a poised state in which heavy boundaries loosen without complete cosmic dispersal; beauty forms this chaos into cosmos. The source table assigns sleep to truth, dream to beauty and waking to goodness. Goodness requires separation of inner and outer so that one may obey or refuse. Steiner distinguishes that choice from the predetermined wisdom of spiritual beings, while retaining a spiritual moral order. The table does not assert that every dream is true or good.

Captures 127–129; note 49, capture 151.

Recover colour consciously rather than repeat an old culture

The historical argument follows these distinctions. Steiner describes icons and Cimabue as painting from the macrocosm through gold; Giotto turns toward the physical human being, and Raphael perfects beauty illuminated by an older experience. The Madonna has earthly weight; later naturalism leaves colour applied to heavy surfaces. Copying an icon cannot recover the consciousness behind it. His answer is conscious painting out of free colour. Painted Goetheanum performance programmes are his modest example, clarified by editorial note 54. Reconstruct this progression before making a study: the activity follows a theory of knowledge and artistic history, not merely a preference for softer outlines.

Captures 130–135, especially 132–134; notes 52–55, captures 151–152.

Connect the parts of the reading

How does the lecture move from its account of sleep to its demand for a renewed painting?

An explained answer: Sleep supplies a spiritual relation with outer things; free qualities disclose qualitative measure, number and weight and, in Steiner's account, spiritual beings. Dream connects those qualities to beauty through formed chaos, while waking adds conscious choice. The icon–Giotto–Raphael history explains why old weightless art cannot simply be repeated. Renewal must consciously experience colour as an active element from which painting develops.

Words for the reading

Qualitative anti-gravity: the expansive tendency attributed to free spiritual qualities. Chaos: free weaving poised between earthly boundaries and cosmic dispersal. Cosmos: chaos given beautiful form. Microcosm: humanity understood as an expression of the larger cosmos. Icon and Madonna: contrasting artistic stages in Steiner's account, rather than a scale of people's value.

Why does Steiner connect sleep with truth, dream with beauty and waking with goodness?

Show a suggested answer

Sleep lets ego and astral body share the outer world's existence and grounds truth as a faithful inner image. Dream forces become memory and the weaving of beauty; beauty forms chaos. Waking separates inner and outer and permits a choice to obey or refuse, which makes goodness possible.

Why does the icon–Giotto–Raphael sequence lead to painting out of colour?

Show a suggested answer

Icons express the macrocosm through weightless gold; Giotto turns toward the physical human being, and Raphael perfects a beauty still illuminated by older experience. Later naturalism leaves colour attached to heavy objects. Steiner therefore calls for conscious experience of free colour, rather than mechanical repetition of an experience he says has disappeared.
