Explore the chapter’s core ideas

The reading’s key idea

Lecture 2 distinguishes image formation through a shadow-thrower and an illuminant from lustre, in which an inner reality shines outward. Yellow is the lustre of spirit, blue of soul, and red of the living. Yellow radiates from a full centre; blue gathers at the circumference toward a lighter centre; red maintains a balanced, enduring surface. These movements provide the artist with a way to organize colour from its own felt character.

Steiner imagines green arising when yellow and blue act on placid white, and peach-blossom when red irradiates black and white interweaving in motion. Image colours are compared with fixed zodiac pictures; lustres with planetary activity. The expanded spiritual spectrum joins its ends through peach-blossom and includes black and white. Gold grounds explain an exception to radiating yellow: yellow acquires weight and becomes mineralized so that spirit can have an earthly dwelling in the painting. He also acknowledges that actual artistic creation brings modifying factors; the processes are his account, not universal rules for every paint or coloured light mixture.

From shadow images to the colour cosmos

Two sides of an image

Lecture 2 makes the image relationship more explicit by distinguishing a shadow-thrower from an illuminant. Spirit and lifelessness give black; lifelessness and life give green; life and soul give peach-blossom; soul and spirit give white. The table keeps the order of these inputs visible. Shadow and image are used in a broad sense: even white belongs to this group. Steiner’s concern is a reality appearing in another level, not simply the dark area that an opaque object casts on a wall.

Capture 24, including the table in column 3.

Rest and movement in colour formation

He next imagines yellow and blue acting from opposite sides upon placid white to yield green. Peach-blossom requires a different process: black and white continually interweave while red shines through them. He says this movement cannot be directly painted and that a portrait only approximates the living complexion. The contrast matters because an image can arise through movement without acquiring the inner activity he will call lustre. Green accepts a boundary; peach-blossom disperses as a mood. These processes belong to his felt and spiritual account, rather than a general formula for mixing coloured light or every pigment.

Captures 25–26.

Three movements that shine

Yellow demands a full centre fading outward, while blue demands a stronger circumference around a lighter interior. Red balances those directions in an enduring, even surface. Its stillness is movement at rest, different from peach-blossom’s tendency to dissolve. The three hatch sketches record these distributions without reproducing their colours. Steiner says that actual artistic creation brings factors that modify the situation. His language nevertheless makes a strong claim: colours have inherent tendencies that the artist should enter and express, rather than imposing any boundary or distribution indiscriminately.

Captures 26–29; sketches at 27, column 3.

Lustre, image and an expanded spectrum

Lustre names an inner reality declaring itself outwardly: yellow is the lustre of spirit, blue of soul, red of the living. Green, in contrast, images the living. Steiner interprets a green after-image following red through that relation. Images are compared with fixed zodiac pictures, lustres with planetary activity; he explicitly calls this a comparison. The straight physical spectrum is then bent into a spiritual arrangement, joining through peach-blossom above and green below. Black and white participate in the imagined weaving. This diagram is an expanded colour cosmos, distinct from both the ordinary spectrum and Lecture 1’s four-image circle.

Captures 29–31; diagram at 30, column 3.

Why gold can give yellow a boundary

The artistic consequence is not an unchanging rule against even yellow. Early gold grounds provide an exception: yellow receives inner weight and is mineralized. Its original radiating tendency changes so that the spiritual can have an earthly dwelling and figures can emerge from it. Steiner thus moves from mobile colour to colour fixed in matter, preparing the next lecture. The closing claim runs in both directions: colour descends into physical surfaces and can raise the human being toward spiritual experience.

Captures 32–33; notes 4–6 at 147–148.

Connect the parts of the reading

If an image colour can move and red can be still, why is movement alone insufficient to distinguish image from lustre?

An explained answer: Steiner distinguishes kinds of activity. Black and white can weave as images, while peach-blossom disperses; neither movement is the direct expression of inner reality that defines lustre. Red’s stillness is balanced activity at rest, so it remains a lustre. Yellow’s outward and blue’s inward radiance make the same principle visible in different directions. The distinction concerns how a colour expresses or images reality, not simply whether a painted patch appears stationary.

Words for the reading

Lustre: an inner quality shining outward. Shadow-thrower and illuminant: the two terms in image formation. Quiescent: inwardly at rest. Mineralized colour: colour fixed with weight in material appearance. Lustre is not simply the gloss of a surface.

Show a suggested answer

Why are yellow, blue and red called lustres?

Show a suggested answer

Their inner nature declares itself outwardly through radiance: yellow outward, blue inward and red evenly balanced. Image colours instead present a reality in another level as a picture or shadow.

Why can the gold ground be even when yellow ordinarily radiates?

Show a suggested answer

Steiner says yellow has been given inner weight and mineralized into gold, changing its original tendency. He interprets the early gold ground as giving spirit a dwelling on earth.
