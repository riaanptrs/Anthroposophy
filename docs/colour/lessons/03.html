What this passage means

The subject is the painter. This conclusion follows the claim that the ‘I’ and astral body live within colour, not merely outside it as receivers of an external cause. The following sentence calls for a conversation with colours about their place on the picture’s surface. Retained wording visually verified; read capture 045 for the final Goetheanism paragraph.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

The reading’s key idea

Steiner answers the riddle of colour fixed in matter through his spiritual account of cosmic evolution. Plant green is linked with the Moon’s separation from Earth; flower colours with Sun influences; mineral colour with forces of planets formerly united with Earth. The artistic sequence is mineral as lustre, plant as lustre-image, animal as image-lustre and human being as image. Painting out of colour means learning how the character of a colour must be transformed for the subject.

Plants are painted darker and covered with yellowish-white radiance; animals lighter, with a pale blue shimmer; minerals must seem to shine from within; human painting converts lustres to transparent image. Liquid colour moving from pot to surface lets the painter participate imaginatively in the transition from mobile to fixed colour. Steiner rejects symbolism in which colour stands for something other than itself, and culminates in the claim that the ‘I’ and astral body live within colour and bear it into physical and etheric bodies. These are his cosmological and spiritual explanations; the existing studio examples explore artistic processes without establishing those explanations.

How colour becomes material and how painting transforms it

The riddle and its cosmic answer

The third lecture asks how colour, understood as mobile image or lustre, becomes fixed in matter. Steiner regards selective absorption and reflection as an inadequate explanation and says Goethe honestly left the problem unresolved. His answer draws on Occult Science: plants arose during fluid Old Moon conditions, then incorporated the mineral element during Earth evolution. The Moon’s separation from Earth accounts for green’s fixed image character. Sun influences transform green into the lustres of flowers and fruit. These are stages and forces in his spiritual cosmology, not a modern account of pigment chemistry.

Captures 34–36.

Plants need image and radiance

The cosmic distinction becomes a painting direction. Plants must be painted darker than their ordinary appearance, then enveloped in yellowish-white radiance. Image alone would be a lifeless copy; the luminous atmosphere should make life appear. Steiner connects this with an interpretation of art history: earlier painters treated lustres as images and so could not truly paint vegetation in landscapes. Landscape’s later emergence requires an altered relation to colour. The claim links technique with consciousness and history; it is more specific than simply advising a pleasing yellow wash.

Captures 37–38.

Minerals shine from within

Mineral colour has another origin in this account. Other planets once belonged to Earth, separated from it, and now act externally upon forces retained within solids. Sunlight makes mineral colours visible but is not their intrinsic source. Painting inorganic objects therefore requires transforming image colours into lustre. The surface should suggest inner light shining from behind it. This is meant to express the object’s reality rather than merely copy its outward form. The named planets illustrate the proposed cosmic connections, without supplying a tested mineral-colour classification.

Captures 39–41; note 9 at 148.

Animals and human beings complete the sequence

Animals should be painted lighter and given a pale blue shimmer, blended with the yellowish plant atmosphere where they meet. This is image-lustre; the plant’s darker image with luminous veil is lustre-image. The order of the compound terms matters. Human beings require pure image: even yellow may lose its ordinary radiating edge because lustre is transformed into image. Steiner associates this with thought, saying that a painter expresses thoughts about the person through colour. The table therefore gives a graded transformation: mineral/lustre, plant/lustre-image, animal/image-lustre, human/image.

Captures 41–42; table at 42, column 1.

Living in colour rather than assigning symbols

Liquid paint kept in pots becomes fixed only on the surface; Steiner wants the painter to experience this transition and relate it to mobile cosmic colour becoming earthly. He criticizes the palette and explicitly rejects symbolism that makes colour mean something other than itself. Joyful yellow, dignified red and soft, tearful blue should be lived qualities. The conclusion supplies the deeper premise: the ‘I’ and astral body live within colour outside the physical body, then bring colour into physical and etheric embodiment. Dialogue with colour is grounded in that proposed unity. The final paragraph calls for a living continuation of Goetheanism through spiritual science.

Captures 43–45; note 10 at 148.

Connect the parts of the reading

Why does painting out of colour require different transformations for a plant and a human being?

An explained answer: In Steiner’s sequence, a plant’s image needs a luminous veil to express life, producing lustre-image. A human being belongs to the spiritual level and should be painted as pure image, expressing thought in transparent colour. Consequently a lustre such as yellow can be altered rather than simply obeying its usual radiating tendency. The practice follows his account of the subject’s nature; the same wash arrangement cannot supply every case.

Words for the reading

Lustre-image: a darker plant image with a luminous veil. Image-lustre: a lighter animal treatment with modified radiance. Inner luminosity: the painted mineral surface conceived as shining from behind. Old Moon: a prior metamorphosis of Earth in Steiner’s account. Astral body: the soul-bearing member in that account.

See the idea in an example

In one study you draw a leaf and fill it green. In another, you let two green washes meet and discover a leaf-like form in the boundary.

The second process allows an unexpected edge or interval to influence the image. It need not produce a more accurate botanical illustration; its aim is to make the colour relationship participate in forming the work.

Which distinction does Steiner propose between fixed plant green and mineral colour?

Reveal the explanation

Original open question and answer

What is Steiner’s proposed difference between plant green and mineral colour becoming fixed?

Show a suggested answer

He links fixed plant green to the Moon’s separation from Earth and mineral colours to forces of other planets that separated from Earth and now act from outside. Both explanations belong to his spiritual cosmic evolution.

What are the four artistic treatments in the lecture’s table?

Show a suggested answer

Mineral: inwardly shining lustre. Plant: lustre-image, darker colour with yellowish-white radiance. Animal: image-lustre, lighter colour with pale blue shimmer. Human being: pure image, including lustres transformed into image.

Why does Steiner reject both palette daubing and a symbolic colour dictionary?

Show a suggested answer

He wants the painter to live within colour’s mobile qualities before fixing it to a surface. He explicitly says colour should not signify something other than itself; its own experienced character should guide the work.
