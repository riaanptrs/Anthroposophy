Explore the chapter’s core ideas

The reading’s key idea

Steiner first compares red, peach-blossom and blue figures in a green meadow: red intensifies and animates the green, peach-blossom remains neutral, and blue dissipates its greenness. He then seeks objective colour relationships in the mineral, plant, animal and human realms. Green is the lifeless image of the living; peach-blossom, the living image of the soul; white or light, the soul’s image of spirit; black, the spiritual image of the lifeless.

An image shows a reality in another mode without being that reality. Green belongs to the plant’s physical/mineral appearance, while Steiner places its life in the etheric body. Human complexion is presented as the soul appearing in living form; light as enabling the spiritual ‘I’ to experience itself in the soul; black as a field penetrable by spirit after life and soul withdraw. The circle orders these relations so that each image’s adjective comes from the preceding level. His complexion discussion contains a documented transcript gap in note 2 and should not become a diagnosis or a ranking of people.

How colour experience becomes a circle of images

Objectivity within the world of colour

Steiner challenges the division that leaves colour’s subjective impression to psychology and its objective nature to physics. An explanation in ether vibrations, he argues, abandons the colour we experience. He proposes remaining within colour and bringing insight into feeling. This is more than permission to report personal associations. He wants feeling and imagination to reveal relationships belonging to colour itself, and therefore to claim objective significance. The artistic viewpoint is central because art must work with that experienced nature rather than merely with a description of something behind it.

Capture 15.

What the green meadow comparison establishes

The opening comparison keeps a green meadow and changes the figures within it. Red makes the greenness more active, so the red figures demand movement. Peach-blossom leaves the meadow undisturbed and allows the figures to remain at rest. Blue is said to weaken its greenness, imaginatively carrying it away into an abyss. These are deliberate comparisons rather than finished pictures. Steiner defends imaginative language by observing that even physical descriptions rely on analogies. The exercise prepares a relational approach: the same surrounding green participates differently according to the colour introduced into it.

Captures 16–17.

Why green images life

The argument then changes from colour comparison to the constitution of beings. In Steiner’s account, a plant has a physical body and an etheric body; its life belongs to the latter, but its green appearance to the physical, mineral element. Green therefore does not contain the life itself. He compares this with an ancestral portrait, which presents a person without containing that person. Since life is the characteristic level of the plant, green becomes the lifeless image of the living. The result depends on his physical/etheric distinction, not simply on the fact that many plants look green.

Capture 18 and the opening of 19.

Complexion, light and blackness

Peach-blossom is related to human complexion, which Steiner acknowledges varies between people. He interprets ruddy complexion and greenish pallor through the soul’s presence in living form, deriving the living image of the soul. Note 2 identifies an incomplete transcript at this passage; its separate 1923 repeat must not be silently inserted. White is reached through light and waking self-awareness: the spiritual ‘I’ experiences itself in the soul, giving the soul’s image of spirit. Black is approached through carbonization and darkness. Life and soul are said to withdraw, while spirit can penetrate the lifeless, giving its spiritual image. These are substantive spiritual inferences, not diagnoses or standards for judging people.

Captures 19–22; note 2 at 147.

The circle and the claim about nature

The circle orders lifeless, living, soul and spirit, with black, green, peach-blossom and white as their respective images. Each adjective comes from the preceding level: spirit can image the lifeless; the lifeless images life; life images soul; soul images spirit. Thus the fourth pairing closes the cycle instead of ending a ladder. Steiner concludes that nature’s coloured appearance is image, distinct from its underlying reality. He calls the relation objective because life’s appearance as green, in his account, holds whether a particular spectator looks at it or not.

Capture 22, columns 2–3, and 23, including the circle in column 1.

Connect the parts of the reading

How does the portrait comparison lead into the circle, and what supports Steiner’s claim that the result is objective?

An explained answer: The portrait separates an image from the reality it presents. The circle extends this to four levels and shows where each reality is imaged. Steiner calls the relation objective because it belongs to nature whether anyone looks. The argument requires his physical/etheric and soul/spirit premises; the meadow comparison alone cannot establish those premises.

Words for the reading

Image colour: black, green, peach-blossom and white. Lifeless: without life, distinct from living activity. Etheric body: the life-bearing member in Steiner’s account. Soul: the realm of inward experience. ‘I’: the spiritual self in this account. Peach-blossom: Steiner’s term for the colour he relates to variable human complexion.

Check your understanding

Why does Steiner call green an image of life?

Reveal the explanation

He locates plant life in the etheric body but green in the physical/mineral appearance. Like a portrait rather than its subject, green presents life without being the living activity it presents.

Original open question and answer

Why does Steiner call green an image of life rather than life itself?

Show a suggested answer

He locates plant life in the etheric body but green in the physical/mineral appearance. Like a portrait rather than its subject, green presents life without being the living activity it presents.

What does each central colour do to the green meadow in the opening comparison?

Show a suggested answer

Red enlivens and intensifies it and calls for moving figures; peach-blossom is neutral and allows repose; blue is said to dissipate greenness and imaginatively carry the meadow away.

How does the circle generate the four image formulas?

Show a suggested answer

Its levels run lifeless, living, soul and spirit. Each level is imaged in the preceding one: living in lifeless green, soul in living peach-blossom, spirit in soul’s white, and lifeless in spiritual black.
