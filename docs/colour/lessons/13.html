Your colour portfolio: observe, compose, explain

Bring the twelve lectures together in a small original work.

Try → explore → read → revise. You can open the explanation whenever you need it.

2 · Work through the explanation

A worked example

A learner makes two versions of an abstract landscape. In the second, a denser blue boundary and a smaller warm centre change the felt depth and balance.

The explanation refers to the blue arrangement in Lecture 2 and colour perspective in Lecture 9, describes the actual revision, and records one response that differed from the learner’s expectation.

The reading’s key idea

Return to the three questions from the orientation: what is arranged, what is experienced, and what interpretation is proposed? The core lectures gave you image and lustre and a way to let form emerge from colour. The supplementary talks added movement, feeling, perspective, composition, visual weight and a spiritual account of nature.

Your final work should show a considered use of two of these artistic ideas, with a short explanation of their source. You can understand and use an artistic proposal without accepting every cosmological claim attached to it. Equally, naming a spiritual meaning does not replace looking carefully at the actual work. Let the portfolio show both close attention and independent judgment.

Keep this idea

A strong portfolio connects an artistic choice, its visible result and a reasoned explanation.

Reading: Revisit Lectures 1–12, especially the two used in your portfolio.

Source: Colour, translated by John Salter (Lectures 1–3) and Pauline Wehrle (4–12), Rudolf Steiner Press. Page assignments refer to the supplied capture markers, not another edition’s pagination. Explanations and activities are original.

Words for the reading

Portfolio: a small collection showing process and judgment. Revision: a change made for an articulated reason. Source attribution: naming the lecture that informed a choice.

3 · Return to the source

Find the connection in the reading

Use the reading assignment and source link in the explanation. Locate one passage relevant to your first attempt. Paraphrase its claim, give the page or lecture reference, and say whether it supports or challenges your interpretation. Distinguish the author’s claim from what the example establishes.

Work with the idea

Submit an initial study, a revision and a 150–250-word commentary, or an equivalent spoken explanation. Name two lecture ideas, describe one observable change, explain one felt effect and distinguish one spiritual claim from the study’s evidence. Written plans may replace paintings. Compare the result with your first notebook entry.

Show a suggested answer

A strong submission shows the initial and revised arrangement, names two lecture ideas and explains a concrete change. It distinguishes the felt result from a wider spiritual interpretation.

How to assess your response

Check four things: correct attribution, a clear distinction, a reason supported by the reading and recognition of the example’s limits. Revisit anything vague. Reasoned disagreement can demonstrate understanding; agreement and reports of spiritual experiences are not required.
