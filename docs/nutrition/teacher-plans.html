This teaching unit explains how food supports the body, where it comes from, how digestion works, and how learners can make practical food choices. It contains six 50 minute lessons with explanations, activities, assessment questions and answer guidance.
**Planning assumption:** Learners are aged 10 to 14. The age range has not been confirmed. Use pictures, oral responses and simpler vocabulary for younger learners; extend label calculations and evidence evaluation for older learners.
**Unit outcomes:** Learners will explain nutrient functions, trace food from its source to the body, distinguish digestion from absorption, interpret basic food labels, use food safety practices and justify a varied meal.
**Teaching approach:** Observation, discussion, drawing, sorting and practical problem solving. Assess understanding rather than learners’ bodies or family diets. Make tasting voluntary and check allergies before bringing foods into class.

Unit sequence

| Lesson | Focus | Learning product |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Why the body needs food | Food observation and explanation |
| 2 | Food sources and variety | Plant part sorting and food journey |
| 3 | Nutrients and their functions | Food and nutrient map |
| 4 | Digestion and absorption | Annotated digestive system |
| 5 | Preparation safety and labels | Food comparison and label worksheet |
| 6 | Practical meal planning | Meal plan with nutritional explanation |
Each lesson includes an opening question, explicit teaching, an activity, application and a short assessment. Collect the learning products to track progress across the unit.

Preparation for the unit

Prepare food photographs or clean packaging, blank paper, coloured pencils, a plant diagram, a digestive system diagram and food cards. Suggested cards are rice, oats, bread, potatoes, beans, lentils, eggs, tofu, fish, yoghurt, fortified soy drink, carrots, broccoli, tomatoes, apples, nuts and vegetable oil. Pictures can replace all food samples.
For Lesson 5, obtain labels from two comparable products that show nutrition information per the same quantity, or use the fictional labels supplied below. If comparing actual products, check the label conventions used locally.
Before any food activity, check allergies, dietary restrictions and school procedures. Wash hands and utensils; avoid shared tasting containers. Do not require learners to disclose what they eat at home. Use fictional meals and scenarios where privacy or food access could affect participation.

Lesson 1 Why the body needs food

**Duration:** 50 minutes.
**Learning objectives:** Explain three reasons the body needs food; distinguish food from nutrients; describe one cultural or social role of food.
**Success criteria:** The learner can explain energy, growth and repair, and normal body functions, and give an example of a food and a nutrient.
**Vocabulary:** Food, nutrient, energy, growth, repair.
**Materials:** Food pictures or optional samples, paper and pencils.

Teacher explanation

Food supplies energy and materials the body needs. Energy is needed for moving, thinking, breathing, pumping blood and maintaining body temperature. The body continues using energy during sleep.
Materials from food support growth and repair. The body replaces and repairs tissues throughout life, so this need does not end when someone stops growing. Nutrients also support normal functions, including nerves, bones and the immune system.
A food is something we eat, such as beans. A nutrient is a substance in food that the body uses, such as protein. Most foods contain several nutrients. Food also has social meaning: people share meals, celebrate, follow traditions and enjoy flavours. Choices depend on culture, cost, availability, preference and dietary needs.

Teaching sequence

1. **Opening question — 5 minutes:** Ask, “Does your body use energy while you sleep?” Learners explain their predictions. Return to their ideas during teaching.
2. **Explicit teaching — 10 minutes:** Explain the three purposes of food. Ask learners to connect each purpose with an example: running, healing a cut, or keeping nerves working.
3. **Observation — 15 minutes:** Learners observe and draw two foods. Prompt them to record colour, shape and texture. Smelling is optional and tasting is unnecessary.
4. **Application — 15 minutes:** Groups discuss a pictured meal. Ask, “What might this meal provide? Why might people choose it?” Distinguish an observation from a guess about nutritional content.
5. **Exit assessment — 5 minutes:** Complete: “We need food even when resting because…” and “A food is… whereas a nutrient is…”

Questions and answer guidance

- **Does food support only movement?** No. It also supports functions such as breathing and growth and repair.
- **Are beans and protein the same thing?** No. Beans are a food containing protein and other nutrients.
- **Why can two families choose different meals?** Culture, preference, access, cost and dietary needs may differ.
**Common misconception:** Food matters only when we exercise. Correct it using examples of continuous body functions.
**Adaptation:** Offer picture prompts and sentence starters. For an extension, ask learners to explain why adults still need nutrients for tissue repair.

Lesson 2 Food sources and dietary variety

**Duration:** 50 minutes.
**Learning objectives:** Identify food sources and edible plant parts; trace a food journey; explain why variety helps meet nutrient needs.
**Success criteria:** Correctly sort at least four plant foods and explain that different foods contribute different nutrients.
**Vocabulary:** Root, stem, leaf, flower, fruit, seed, variety.
**Materials:** Plant diagram, food cards and paper.

Teacher explanation

Foods may come from plants, animals or fungi. Meals often combine several sources. For plants, we eat different parts.
| Plant part | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Roots | Carrot and beetroot |
| Stems | Asparagus and potato stem tubers |
| Leaves | Lettuce and cabbage |
| Flowers | Broccoli and cauliflower |
| Fruits | Tomato, pumpkin and apple |
| Seeds | Rice, beans and peas |
Explain that growing underground does not automatically make something a root: a potato is a stem tuber. Botanical categories and everyday cooking categories differ. A tomato is a botanical fruit but is commonly treated as a vegetable in cooking.
Different foods provide different combinations of nutrients. Variety over meals and days helps meet our needs. Colour can encourage variety among fruits and vegetables, but colour alone does not establish nutritional value. Familiar, affordable local foods can provide variety.

Teaching sequence

1. **Retrieval — 5 minutes:** Ask learners to name the three purposes of food from Lesson 1.
2. **Explicit teaching — 10 minutes:** Use the plant diagram to show edible parts. Introduce the potato and tomato examples.
3. **Sorting — 15 minutes:** Groups sort cards by plant part; place animal foods and fungi in separate source groups. Ask learners to explain uncertain choices.
4. **Food journey — 15 minutes:** Each learner illustrates one food’s journey through growing or rearing, harvesting, transport, preparation and eating. Discuss who contributes along the way.
5. **Exit assessment — 5 minutes:** Ask, “Why is a variety of foods useful?” and “Is every underground food a root?”

Questions and answer guidance

- **Why is variety useful?** Different foods contribute different nutrients.
- **Is a potato a root?** No. It is an underground stem tuber.
- **Must variety involve expensive imported food?** No. Local grains, pulses, vegetables and other available foods can support variety.
**Common misconception:** A single food can reliably provide everything the body needs. Explain that mixed eating patterns help meet different needs.
**Adaptation:** Reduce the sorting set for younger learners. For an extension, trace how storage or transport changes a food journey.

Lesson 3 Nutrients and their functions

**Duration:** 50 minutes.
**Learning objectives:** Describe the main nutrient groups and fibre; identify food sources; explain why foods can belong in several categories.
**Success criteria:** Match at least four nutrients with a function and a source, and explain the multiple contributions of one food.
**Vocabulary:** Carbohydrate, protein, amino acid, fat, vitamin, mineral, hydration, fibre.
**Materials:** Food cards, nutrient cards and the reference table below.

Teacher explanation

| Nutrient or component | Main functions | Example sources |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Carbohydrates | Provide energy; include starches and sugars | Rice, oats, bread, potatoes and fruit |
| Protein | Supplies amino acids for growth, repair, enzymes and other structures | Beans, lentils, tofu, eggs, fish and meat |
| Fats | Provide energy, form parts of cells and help absorb vitamins A, D, E and K | Oils, nuts, seeds and avocado |
| Vitamins | Support many processes; each has particular roles | A range of fruits, vegetables and other foods |
| Minerals | Support functions such as bone formation, oxygen transport and nerve activity | Dairy or suitable fortified alternatives, beans, meat and vegetables |
| Water | Helps transport substances, regulate temperature and maintain body fluids | Drinking water and water containing foods |
| Fibre | Supports bowel function; some types feed beneficial gut microbes | Whole grains, beans, fruits and vegetables |
Fibre is a type of carbohydrate our digestive enzymes do not fully break down. Vitamins and minerals are needed in smaller quantities than carbohydrates, proteins and fats, but are still essential.
Give concrete examples: calcium supports bones and teeth; iron is needed for haemoglobin, which carries oxygen in blood; vitamin C supports normal tissue functions and helps absorption of iron from plant foods.
Foods contribute more than one nutrient. Beans supply protein, carbohydrates, fibre and minerals. Nuts provide fats, protein and fibre. Water needs vary with activity, weather and individual circumstances; provide access to water rather than prescribing one intake for every learner.

Teaching sequence

1. **Opening — 5 minutes:** Ask, “Can beans be both a protein food and a carbohydrate food?”
2. **Explicit teaching — 15 minutes:** Introduce the table and examples. Clarify that carbohydrates and fats have useful functions, and vitamins do not themselves provide food energy.
3. **Matching — 15 minutes:** Groups connect food cards to all relevant nutrient cards. Use multiple connections rather than forcing each food into one category.
4. **Application — 10 minutes:** Explain the contributions of rice, beans, vegetables and oil. Learners make a nutrient map for another pictured meal.
5. **Exit assessment — 5 minutes:** Name a nutrient, its function and a source. Then explain two contributions of beans.

Questions and answer guidance

- **Is protein only for building muscles?** No. It also supports other tissues, enzymes and many body processes.
- **Do vitamins provide energy?** No. They support processes that keep the body functioning.
- **Are carbohydrates inherently harmful?** No. They are a useful energy source; food composition and the overall eating pattern matter.
- **Does oil supply protein?** Ordinary cooking oil supplies fat rather than meaningful amounts of protein.
**Adaptation:** Teach carbohydrates, protein, fats and water first, then introduce vitamins, minerals and fibre. For an extension, investigate a specific nutrient deficiency using a reliable health source.

Lesson 4 Digestion and absorption

**Duration:** 50 minutes.
**Learning objectives:** Trace food through the digestive tract; describe each main organ’s role; distinguish digestion from absorption.
**Success criteria:** Label the route in order and explain that most nutrient absorption takes place in the small intestine.
**Vocabulary:** Digestion, enzyme, oesophagus, stomach, small intestine, absorption, large intestine.
**Materials:** Digestive system diagram, paper chains or connecting beads and pencils.

Teacher explanation

Digestion breaks food down into smaller substances. Absorption moves digested nutrients across the intestinal wall into circulation.
| Part | Main role |
| --- | --- |
| Mouth | Teeth break food into smaller pieces; saliva moistens food and an enzyme begins starch digestion |
| Oesophagus | Muscular movements push swallowed food toward the stomach |
| Stomach | Churns food; acid and enzymes help digest protein |
| Small intestine | Completes much digestion and absorbs most nutrients |
| Large intestine | Absorbs more water; microbes act on some remaining material; stool forms |
| Rectum and anus | Store and release stool |
The pancreas supplies digestive enzymes to the small intestine. The liver makes bile, and the gallbladder stores it. Bile helps disperse fats so enzymes can act on them; bile is not an enzyme.
Digestible carbohydrates become simple sugars; proteins become amino acids and small peptides; fats become smaller components that can be absorbed. Much fibre remains undigested by our enzymes, although gut microbes can act on some types.

Teaching sequence

1. **Prediction — 5 minutes:** Learners predict the route taken by a bite of food.
2. **Explicit teaching — 15 minutes:** Walk through the diagram in order. Identify digestion and absorption separately.
3. **Protein model — 10 minutes:** A paper chain represents protein. Separate its links to represent amino acids. Explain that absorbed amino acids can be used to build the body’s proteins. State that this is a simplified model.
4. **Diagram task — 15 minutes:** Learners draw arrows, label organs and add a short function for each. Include the pancreas and liver as helping organs.
5. **Exit assessment — 5 minutes:** Ask where most nutrient absorption happens and how digestion differs from absorption.

Questions and answer guidance

- **Does the stomach do all digestion?** No. Digestion also occurs in the mouth and small intestine.
- **What is absorption?** Movement of nutrients across the intestinal wall into circulation.
- **Where does most absorption happen?** The small intestine.
- **Does food pass through the liver?** No. The liver assists digestion by producing bile; it is not part of the food’s route through the tract.
**Adaptation:** Give a partly labelled diagram. For an extension, investigate how the small intestine’s folds and villi increase its absorbing surface.

Lesson 5 Food preparation safety and labels

**Duration:** 50 minutes.
**Learning objectives:** Explain how preparation changes food; describe safe handling; compare nutrition labels using equivalent quantities.
**Success criteria:** State two preparation effects, two safety practices and an accurate label comparison.
**Vocabulary:** Processing, ingredient, serving, fibre, sugars, sodium, contamination.
**Materials:** Raw and cooked vegetable pictures or samples; comparable labels or the fictional practice table below.

Teacher explanation

Preparation can change texture, flavour, digestibility and safety. Cooking can soften food and reduce harmful microbes. It can decrease some nutrients while making others more accessible. Raw and cooked foods can both contribute to a varied diet.
Processing includes washing, freezing, cooking, drying, fermenting and canning. Processing alone does not establish nutritional value. Frozen vegetables and canned beans can be useful foods.
Basic safety practices are washing hands and utensils, washing produce with safe water, separating raw animal foods from ready to eat foods, cooking appropriately and refrigerating perishable foods promptly. Follow local food safety guidance for specific temperatures and storage times.
Ingredient lists are generally ordered from greatest to least weight. Nutrition information may be shown per serving or per 100 g or 100 mL. A fair comparison uses the same quantity. Total sugars can include naturally occurring and added sugars; labels do not always report added sugars separately.

Teaching sequence

1. **Opening — 5 minutes:** Ask why people cook or preserve foods.
2. **Observation — 10 minutes:** Compare raw and cooked vegetable texture and appearance. Ask what was observed and what would require measurement.
3. **Explicit teaching — 10 minutes:** Explain preparation, processing, safety and label conventions.
4. **Label task — 20 minutes:** Compare two similar products using equal quantities. Learners identify fibre, sugars and sodium and explain what cannot be concluded from a label alone.
5. **Exit assessment — 5 minutes:** State a safety practice and explain why equal quantities matter.

Practice labels

These values are invented for classroom practice and do not describe real products.
| Per 100 g | Cereal A | Cereal B |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fibre | 8 g | 3 g |
| Total sugars | 10 g | 22 g |
| Sodium | 120 mg | 250 mg |
**Student questions:** Which has more fibre? Which has less total sugar and sodium? How much fibre is in 50 g of Cereal A? Can these figures prove that everyone should choose Cereal A?
**Answer guidance:** A has more fibre and less total sugar and sodium per 100 g. A 50 g portion has 4 g of fibre. These figures support specific comparisons but do not establish a universal choice; ingredients, allergies, cost, preferences and the wider diet also matter.
**Common misconception:** Every processed food is nutritionally poor. Use frozen vegetables or canned beans as counterexamples.
**Adaptation:** Focus on one label measure at a time. For an extension, calculate values for different serving sizes.

Lesson 6 Planning a practical nourishing meal

**Duration:** 50 minutes.
**Learning objectives:** Plan a varied meal; explain nutritional contributions; adapt the plan to a realistic constraint.
**Success criteria:** Explain at least three nutrient contributions, address the assigned constraint and identify a relevant safety practice.
**Vocabulary:** Variety, meal pattern, substitution, affordability, dietary need.
**Materials:** Food cards, meal planning worksheet and optional local prices.

Teacher explanation

A nourishing eating pattern combines variety, enough food, enjoyment and practicality. One meal does not need to provide every nutrient perfectly. Patterns across meals and days matter, and individual needs vary.
A flexible meal starting point includes vegetables or fruit, a grain or starchy food, a protein source, some fat from ingredients or preparation, and water as an everyday drink. Include calcium rich foods such as dairy or suitable fortified alternatives across the day.
A meal of rice, beans, vegetables and a little oil gives a concrete example: rice mainly contributes carbohydrates; beans supply protein, fibre and other nutrients; vegetables add fibre, vitamins and minerals; oil supplies fat. A mixed dish can include these components without placing them in separate sections.

Teaching sequence

1. **Retrieval — 5 minutes:** Review nutrient functions and the main site of absorption.
2. **Worked example — 10 minutes:** Explain the example meal. Model a substitution, such as replacing rice with another available grain or starchy food.
3. **Meal design — 20 minutes:** Groups plan a meal and complete the worksheet below. Assign one constraint: a limited budget, vegetarian eating, a specified food allergy, or limited cooking equipment.
4. **Sharing — 10 minutes:** Each group explains its food choices, constraint and safety practice.
5. **Reflection — 5 minutes:** Learners explain one thing they understand now that they did not understand before.

Student worksheet

- What is your meal and who is it for?
- Which ingredients provide carbohydrates, protein, fats and fibre?
- What contributes vitamins or minerals?
- What drink will accompany it?
- What constraint were you given, and how did you address it?
- What preparation or food safety practice matters?
- What could you substitute if an ingredient were unavailable?
For allergy scenarios, specify the allergen and require checking ingredient information and avoiding cross contact. Do not ask learners to determine another person’s medical needs.

Assessment rubric

Award 0, 1 or 2 points in each area: 0 means missing or inaccurate; 1 means partly demonstrated; 2 means clearly demonstrated.
| Area | Evidence for 2 points |
| --- | --- |
| Nutrition explanation | Correctly explains at least three nutrient contributions |
| Variety | Includes several foods with different nutritional contributions |
| Practical constraint | Provides a workable response to the assigned constraint |
| Preparation and safety | Identifies a relevant practice and explains its purpose |
**Maximum:** 8 points. Use the rubric to guide feedback and revision, not to rate learners’ personal diets.
**Adaptation:** Offer a small set of food cards and oral presentation. For an extension, compare two meal plans using local prices and discuss tradeoffs.

Final assessment and answer guidance

Use these questions as an oral review or short written assessment.
1. **Give three reasons the body needs food.** Energy, growth and repair, and normal body functions.
2. **How is a food different from a nutrient?** A food is something eaten; a nutrient is a substance in food used by the body.
3. **Why does dietary variety help?** Different foods provide different combinations of nutrients.
4. **Name two contributions of beans.** Accept protein, carbohydrates, fibre or minerals.
5. **How do digestion and absorption differ?** Digestion breaks substances down; absorption moves them across the intestinal wall into circulation.
6. **Where are most nutrients absorbed?** The small intestine.
7. **Does processed automatically mean nutritionally poor?** No. Composition and the overall eating pattern matter.
8. **Why compare labels per the same quantity?** Different quantities can make direct numerical comparisons misleading.
9. **Name a food safety practice and explain its purpose.** For example, separating raw animal foods from ready to eat foods reduces cross contamination.
10. **Explain a practical nourishing meal.** Accept accurate explanations of varied foods, nutrient contributions, practicality and safe preparation.
Revisit errors through examples and explanations. Allow drawing or oral responses when writing ability would conceal nutrition understanding.

Use of the attached reference

The supplied Nutrition.md is an OCR transcription of the supplied Nutrition.pdf, which contains Rudolf Steiner’s Nutrition Food Health and Spiritual Development, compiled and edited by Christian von Arnim. The transcription includes OCR uncertainty, so check the PDF before quoting exact wording.
The unit draws thematic connections with Chapter 4 on nutrition and the body, Chapter 5 on food quality, Chapter 6 on digestion, and Chapter 9 on nutrients. The source is not treated as a current scientific authority. Its spiritual interpretations and unsupported biological claims are the author’s views.
In particular, teach that dietary proteins are digested into amino acids and small peptides. Do not teach the book’s claim that humans obtain the nitrogen needed for their proteins by breathing air. The body uses nitrogen supplied through dietary compounds, especially amino acids.
For older learners, an optional extension is to compare one short source claim with a current biology or public health reference. Ask what evidence supports the claim, whether it can be tested and whether it is a spiritual interpretation or a biological assertion. This extension is additional to the six lesson schedule.
**Further teacher references:** Current national food based dietary guidelines; WHO Healthy diet guidance; NIDDK Your Digestive System and How It Works; and local food safety guidance. Use age appropriate recommendations and local label conventions.
