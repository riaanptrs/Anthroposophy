Understand the concepts

Preference is not a universal command

Captures 75–77 continue the distinction between receiving animal-prepared nourishment and performing one’s own formative work. Steiner presents his own vegetarian experience as supporting greater stamina. Yet the quoted qualification says that an individual’s capacity must first be considered. Within this argument, a general preference cannot replace judgement about constitution. Personal experience illustrates his position but does not establish identical results for everyone.

Protein and continuous renewal

Captures 77–79 link digestive capacity to the ability to transform plant protein and discuss problems of excess. The body is pictured as continuously renewing itself. That dynamic picture connects to the anthology’s recurring concern with activity. The universal replacement of the entire body in seven or eight years is an inaccurate simplification; different tissues and cells have very different renewal rates. Keep the useful question about renewal distinct from the asserted timetable.

Read the ox example and the headnote separately

Capture 80 imagines feeding meat to an ox. Steiner claims unused forces form urates that reach the brain and produce madness. The editor’s headnote at capture 67 connects this to the much later BSE epidemic. These are two voices and two dates: a historical lecture assertion and a retrospective editorial comparison. Modern BSE is explained through infectious prions and contaminated feed, not the urate mechanism described here. A resemblance in feeding context is not verification of that mechanism.

Why access closes the discussion

Captures 81–82 include unsupported racial generalizations and then discuss food markets serving wealthy spa patients. The chapter thus leaves individual stomachs and asks how social arrangements determine what food people can obtain. This expands freedom: choices depend on circumstances as well as intentions. An account of nourishment that ignores access is incomplete even if it gives detailed instructions to individual eaters.

Historical interpretation and present knowledge

CDC describes BSE as a prion disease, with strong evidence of spread through contaminated feed. The headnote’s prediction claim does not validate Steiner’s urate theory. Racial/intestine-length generalizations are unsupported and should not be used to infer anyone’s digestive ability.

Who makes the retrospective BSE comparison at the chapter opening?

Answer and explanation

Answer: The editorial headnote.

The headnote is later editorial framing; the lecture proposes a different mechanism involving urates.

Why does the food-market discussion matter?

Answer and explanation

Answer: It shows that access and social arrangements condition dietary choice.

The ending extends the analysis from bodily capacity to the conditions in which people choose.
