Lesson 5 Food preparation safety and labels

Printable full unitDownload the lesson plans

Duration: 50 minutes.

Learning objectives: Explain how preparation changes food; describe safe handling; compare nutrition labels using equivalent quantities.

Success criteria: State two preparation effects, two safety practices and an accurate label comparison.

Vocabulary: Processing, ingredient, serving, fibre, sugars, sodium, contamination.

Materials: Raw and cooked vegetable pictures or samples; comparable labels or the fictional practice table below.

Teacher explanation

Preparation can change texture, flavour, digestibility and safety. Cooking can soften food and reduce harmful microbes. It can decrease some nutrients while making others more accessible. Raw and cooked foods can both contribute to a varied diet.

Processing includes washing, freezing, cooking, drying, fermenting and canning. Processing alone does not establish nutritional value. Frozen vegetables and canned beans can be useful foods.

Basic safety practices are washing hands and utensils, washing produce with safe water, separating raw animal foods from ready to eat foods, cooking appropriately and refrigerating perishable foods promptly. Follow local food safety guidance for specific temperatures and storage times.

Ingredient lists are generally ordered from greatest to least weight. Nutrition information may be shown per serving or per 100 g or 100 mL. A fair comparison uses the same quantity. Total sugars can include naturally occurring and added sugars; labels do not always report added sugars separately.

Teaching sequence

  1. Opening — 5 minutes: Ask why people cook or preserve foods.
  2. Observation — 10 minutes: Compare raw and cooked vegetable texture and appearance. Ask what was observed and what would require measurement.
  3. Explicit teaching — 10 minutes: Explain preparation, processing, safety and label conventions.
  4. Label task — 20 minutes: Compare two similar products using equal quantities. Learners identify fibre, sugars and sodium and explain what cannot be concluded from a label alone.
  5. Exit assessment — 5 minutes: State a safety practice and explain why equal quantities matter.

Practice labels

These values are invented for classroom practice and do not describe real products.

Per 100 gCereal ACereal B
Fibre8 g3 g
Total sugars10 g22 g
Sodium120 mg250 mg

Student questions: Which has more fibre? Which has less total sugar and sodium? How much fibre is in 50 g of Cereal A? Can these figures prove that everyone should choose Cereal A?

Answer guidance: A has more fibre and less total sugar and sodium per 100 g. A 50 g portion has 4 g of fibre. These figures support specific comparisons but do not establish a universal choice; ingredients, allergies, cost, preferences and the wider diet also matter.

Common misconception: Every processed food is nutritionally poor. Use frozen vegetables or canned beans as counterexamples.

Adaptation: Focus on one label measure at a time. For an extension, calculate values for different serving sizes.
