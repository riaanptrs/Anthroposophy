Lesson 12 · The three worlds

Spiritland: living archetypes and seven regions

Distinguish physical form, life, soul, coordination, and creative impulses in the sevenfold spiritual account.

Why can a plant appear in the first and second descriptions of Spiritland?

1 · Read the passage

Begin with the book

Rudolf Steiner · Theosophy · GA 9 · Monges / Church, 1971 · Printed p. 104 · PDF p. 132

They are the master builders of all that comes into being in the physical and soul worlds.

Anthroposophic Press, 1971; Henry B. Monges, translator; Gilbert Church, reviser. English excerpt checked against the supplied PDF image; Brazilian Portuguese is a new course study translation. Line breaks and line-end hyphenation normalized.

Open the source or edition index →

What this passage means

“They” refers to archetypes, which the previous sentence calls creative beings. The following sentences describe changing forms and cooperation. Connect this activity to the regions’ roles: physical form, life, soul, coordination, and higher creative impulses. A static blueprint leaves out the living activity the author claims.

The focus of this lesson

Distinguish physical form, life, soul, coordination, and creative impulses in the sevenfold spiritual account.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

Understand the idea

Before tracing the spirit’s further life, Steiner describes Spiritland itself. Ordinary thought, he says, is a shadow-picture of living spiritual thought-beings. The thought “lion” differs from the spiritual being corresponding to it, just as a shadow differs from the object casting it. An archetype is therefore neither an abstraction collected from many objects nor a picture invented by a human mind. The painter imagining a painting is an analogy for the creative relation (pp. 101–103).

Archetypes are living creators: they assume many forms and cooperate in producing things in the physical and soul worlds. Spiritual hearing expresses their relationships as sounding or “music of the spheres”; this is explicitly not physical sound. Steiner intends a spiritual reality communicated through analogy, rather than a decorative allegory. Shining and sounding are presented as simultaneous aspects of perception (pp. 104–105).

Region 1 contains archetypes of physical forms considered apart from life. It includes the physical forms of minerals, plants, animals, and humans, not minerals alone. “Continents” and hollow spaces where physical bodies would be are comparisons for this basic structure. Region 2 contains archetypes of life as one flowing unity, compared more closely to circulating blood than to separate rivers (pp. 106–108).

Region 3 contains the spiritual counterparts of soul formations: feelings, sensations, wishes, and passions. Atmosphere, gentle winds, and storms express their qualities and relationships. The author’s storm of battle passions is a spiritual comparison, not weather caused by a war. Region 4 coordinates the archetypes of the first three, ordering and grouping their cooperation (pp. 108–109).

Regions 5, 6, and 7 supply the creative impulses of the archetypes themselves. The text initially describes these regions together through germ-points and underlying purposes. Spiritual music is said to become Spiritual Word, through which beings express their nature. This introductory passage does not provide three separate detailed mechanisms; the next section distinguishes the human spirit’s participation in the higher regions.

The germ-beings have a thought-world sheath around a life-kernel originating in higher worlds. This reconnects the description with Chapter I’s life spirit and spirit man, rather than making the seven regions a complete limit on the author’s cosmos (p. 110). The regions interpenetrate: a diagram should show different kinds of creative activity without assigning them physical altitude.

Read Addenda 9–11 alongside these pages. Addendum 8, an optional forward reference to Lesson 18, points elsewhere for fuller training. No. 9 says spiritual rest can coexist with active mobility, so creative activity does not mean ceaseless agitation. No. 10 qualifies “purposes,” warning against merely projecting limited human planning into world evolution. No. 11 refers to further treatment of Spiritual Word. These qualifications are part of an accurate understanding of the main account.

Keep this idea

“Archetype” here means a living creative spiritual reality, not a frozen model.

See the idea in an example

A building plan shows how rooms relate before the building is finished. The drawing and the finished house are not the same thing.

What this helps explain

This comparison introduces the difference between an organizing relationship and a formed result. Steiner’s archetypes go further: he describes living creative spiritual activity, not paper plans made by a human designer.

Reveal the explanation

The first considers its physical form apart from life; the second concerns living formation and the unity of life. The regions distinguish aspects of creative activity and interpenetrate. They are not exclusive geographical containers for different species.

How does interpenetration change the way the regions should be pictured?

See a way to approach it

The regions distinguish aspects of creative activity rather than separate geographical territories. A being can therefore be considered under more than one regional aspect without contradicting the account.
