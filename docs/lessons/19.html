Lesson 19 · The path of knowledge

Feeling as an instrument of understanding

Explain the transformation of sympathy, antipathy, pleasure, and pain without confusing it with emotional numbness.

How does Steiner propose transforming pleasure and pain?

1 · Read the passage

Begin with the book

Rudolf Steiner · Theosophy · GA 9 · Monges / Church, 1971 · Printed p. 162 · PDF p. 190

On the contrary, the more a man develops the capacity to refrain from allowing each feeling of sympathy and antipathy to be followed immediately by a judgment, an action, the finer will his sensitivity become.

Anthroposophic Press, 1971; Henry B. Monges, translator; Gilbert Church, reviser. English excerpt checked against the supplied PDF image; Brazilian Portuguese is a new course study translation. Line breaks and line-end hyphenation normalized.

Open the source or edition index →

What this passage means

The contrast refers to the immediately preceding rejection of eradicating sympathy and antipathy. Notice the relation between withholding an automatic verdict and refining sensitivity. Continue through pp. 164–165: Steiner’s stronger claim is that transformed feeling develops organs of soul perception, rather than merely producing a calmer reaction.

The focus of this lesson

Explain the transformation of sympathy, antipathy, pleasure, and pain without confusing it with emotional numbness.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

Understand the idea

At the end of p. 161, receptivity is joined to correct valuation. On p. 162, Steiner argues that making pleasure, pain, or personal sympathy the immediate basis of judgment places one’s personality in front of the thing being encountered. “I like this” initially tells us about our relationship to something. His aim is to learn what the thing or person is in its own character.

The passage on p. 162 expressly says that sympathy and antipathy are not to be eradicated. Their roles must change: a feeling should not automatically become a verdict or an action. Steiner expects a finer sensitivity to result, because an initially unattractive thing can then disclose qualities that personal aversion concealed. Equanimity means remaining able to attend and understand, not becoming indifferent.

On pp. 163–164, pleasure and pain become opportunities for learning. The seeker asks what joy or suffering reveals rather than remaining wholly occupied with being pleased or hurt. A feeling is thus an intimation of a quality to investigate, not the entire account of that quality. For example, enjoying a poem can lead to attention to its rhythm and images; enjoyment alone does not explain those features or settle every judgment about the poem.

Steiner gives this transformation a stronger spiritual meaning. On pp. 164–165, pleasure and pain become organs through which the nature of things and other souls is disclosed. The eye comparison separates reception from action: an eye conveys an impression, while the hand acts. Likewise, the transformed feeling is to transmit an impression rather than simply dictate conduct. He claims that actual soul organs of perception develop through this process.

This connects the path with Chapter III: sympathy and antipathy described the relationships of the soul world; here the learner’s own feeling life is to become capable of receiving that world. The ordinary reading exercise below illustrates a change in attention. It does not establish the existence of the spiritual organs Steiner describes.

Clear thinking need not mean coldness. Feeling gives an encounter personal significance, and Steiner’s fuller discussion connects living thinking with feeling, will and love. If a brief message hurts me, that hurt belongs to my experience; it does not yet establish what the sender intended. I can acknowledge the feeling while examining my interpretation. Two students can understand the same explanation while one feels relief and the other frustration. In Chapter 6, Steiner distinguishes our particular encounters from the feelings we associate with them. Understanding and the cultivation of feeling belong together; emotional intensity alone does not establish truth or measure individuality.

Chapter 1 of GA 4 helps explain this connection: how I understand a situation can awaken a feeling and shape a response. A classmate closes their book. I imagine that they are discouraged and feel concern; asking them may reveal that they have simply finished. Thought can support care, while the picture I form of another person still needs checking.

Additional connection: The Philosophy of Freedom (GA 4), markers 11, 56–57, 74, 113 in the 147-page Basis transcription. Locate by chapter in another edition. Find the book.

Attention can become care
Connection with the later lectures

Attention can become care

Cultivating feeling also has a positive direction. In the later lectures, love is important to the path of knowledge and to opening toward the world. A useful classroom example is patient listening: you give another person enough attention for their meaning to become clearer, even when their first words irritate you.

Consider a fictional friend who seems abrupt. Before replying, ask what they meant and listen to the answer. You may still disagree or set a boundary. What has changed is the quality of attention. This illustrates how steadiness can support care and understanding, rather than merely holding a reaction back.

Companion source: Rudolf Steiner, What Is Anthroposophy? Three Perspectives on Self-Knowledge (2002), page markers 139, 160–162. Numbers refer to the first study transcription (191 pages), not the pages of Theosophy.

Calmness still allows a response
Connection with Course 2 · GA 10

Calmness still allows a response

In GA 10, Steiner explains that an insult does not itself determine a person’s worth, while allowing that an affront may require a response. Imagine being unfairly blamed for a missed deadline. You can notice anger, check the facts and answer firmly. Calmness concerns how the response is formed; it need not remove the feeling or the need to act.

Source: How to Know Higher Worlds, PDF pages 43–44 in the 108-page capture (Dead Authors Society, 2018).

See the idea in an example

You feel irritated when someone corrects the date in your notes. You check the calendar before deciding whether the correction is right.

What this helps explain

The irritation is real as an experience, but does not decide the date. Equanimity means enough steadiness to examine the matter while still feeling what you feel. It does not require pretending to be unaffected.

Check your understanding

How does Steiner propose transforming pleasure and pain?

Reveal the explanation

Steiner explicitly rejects eradicating feeling. The proposed transformation aims to let feeling disclose something about its object rather than merely repeat the person’s immediate reaction.

What separates an ordinary illustration of refined feeling from the further spiritual claim?

Pause and answer in one sentence, aloud or in your notes.

See a way to approach it

An ordinary example can show a less self-centred response. Steiner further claims that the transformation develops organs of soul perception; the example illustrates the distinction without proving that claim.

Original open question and answer

Why is “become insensitive to pleasure and pain” a mistaken summary of this section?

Show a suggested answer

Steiner explicitly rejects blunting or eradicating feeling. He proposes making feeling more delicate and less dominated by immediate self-centered reaction, so that it can reveal something about its object. He further claims that this transformation develops organs of soul perception; the ordinary example illustrates the distinction without proving that claim.
