Lesson 07 · Re-embodiment and destiny

Memory, learning, and lasting effects

Explain how Steiner connects perception, recollection, acquired capacity, and the continuing effects of action.

How does a recollection differ from a fruit of experience?

1 · Read the passage

Begin with the book

Rudolf Steiner · Theosophy · GA 9 · Monges / Church, 1971 · Printed p. 43 · PDF p. 71

I am quite a different person in my relationship to the world after having made an impression on my surroundings.

Anthroposophic Press, 1971; Henry B. Monges, translator; Gilbert Church, reviser. English excerpt checked against the supplied PDF image; Brazilian Portuguese is a new course study translation. Line breaks and line-end hyphenation normalized.

Open the source or edition index →

What this passage means

The words “relationship to the world” concern what a deed leaves outside the person, not only a memory within. On the preceding page, cutting a branch changes what can happen next. Relate this to the chapter’s other direction: memories become new present representations, while their fruits can remain as skills. Explain the external effect and the inward capacity before following the later extension to destiny.

The focus of this lesson

Explain how Steiner connects perception, recollection, acquired capacity, and the continuing effects of action.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

Understand the idea

Begin with Chapter I’s body, soul, and spirit. Chapter II asks what passes away with an encounter and what continues. The rose is present to the eye only while it can be seen. Steiner contrasts this passing perception with a truth understood about the rose, whose validity he regards as independent of this particular encounter. Soul mediates between the passing impression and the enduring spiritual content.

Memory and action give this mediation two directions. Remembering brings yesterday into present soul life. Acting puts something of the person into the world and prepares tomorrow. Steiner’s cut-twig example makes the outward direction concrete: after the twig is cut, the tree’s subsequent course differs from what it would have been. Remembering the cut and the continuing effect on the tree are distinct consequences.

The deed also changes the actor’s relation to the world. Someone who helps a neighbour enters a relationship created by that help, whether or not the scene is vividly remembered. On pp. 43–44 Steiner asks whether an effect bearing the I’s imprint might later approach that I from outside, as memory approaches from within. At this stage he deliberately presents the return of consequences as a question; the later karma argument develops it.

On pp. 44–45 Steiner rejects the picture of a perception stored unchanged and then taken out again. The original perception passes; the encounter produces changes through which a new mental image arises later. In his example of recognizing someone met yesterday, today’s perception and a presently formed recollection are united. This is his account of soul activity, rather than a lesson about the modern mechanisms of memory.

The body participates in this account. On p. 46 Steiner says the soul imprints a process on the body as though by a sign, and later perceives what it has imprinted. Calling soul the preserver of memory therefore does not omit the bodily sign. Trace body’s impression, soul’s preservation, and spirit’s appropriation of what experience yields.

Steiner distinguishes two possessions of spirit: the true and good that it understands, and the riches brought from its own history. Experience enlarges the particular spirit’s horizon. He does not equate truth with whatever someone remembers: a remembered episode and the validity of a truth have different roles.

The p. 47 passage supplies the decisive learning example. We need not remember each attempt at reading or writing to retain the resulting ability. Steiner calls these abilities the fruits of experience: spirit transforms what soul has gathered into an increased power to meet later situations. A storeroom is an inadequate image because the result is a changed capacity, rather than unchanged pictures.

Keep two sequences clear: encounter → present recollection → assimilated fruit, and deed → continuing effect in the world. So far the chapter has considered life between birth and death. At the bottom of p. 47 Steiner explicitly changes that scope. The next lesson studies his argument for extending spiritual history beyond this lifetime.

Seeing a bicycle now, understanding what a bicycle is, remembering a particular ride, and being able to ride are different. In GA 4, a percept is the content encountered through observation, not the act of observing. After an encounter, a mental picture can remain; Chapter 6 calls it an individualized concept: a concept retaining its reference to a particular encounter. Individualized here does not mean an arbitrary private opinion. Representation is another translation of this term and need not mean a visual image. When you attend to that memory, it becomes an inward percept: you are observing a remembered experience, rather than seeing the bicycle again. Your memory of where you left the bicycle does not establish where it is now. These distinctions clarify memory and capacity without proving reincarnation.

Additional connection: The Philosophy of Freedom (GA 4), markers 31–35, 51, 54–57 in the 147-page Basis transcription. Locate by chapter in another edition. Find the book.

The same kind, or the same object?

  1. Concept

    You understand what a bicycle is. This concept can apply to many bicycles.

  2. Particular encounter

    You encounter a bicycle with a blue basket by the gate. Your representation retains a reference to that encounter.

  3. Recognition

    Later, you see another bicycle: you recognize the same kind of object. To claim it is that very bicycle, you need to relate the new encounter to the earlier one. A similar basket alone may not be enough.

Explain what supports each recognition. Remembering a feature and checking the identity of the object are related but distinct tasks. You can describe the memory in words without forming a visual image.

Why memory matters to inner life
Connection with the later lectures

Why memory matters to inner life

Remembering a conversation can change how you understand someone today. Memory gives past experience a place in present inner life. In the later lectures, Steiner connects memory with the image-forming activity he also discusses through dreams, and relates it to the astral organization working through the physical and etheric bodies.

In the discussion of dreams, Steiner makes a useful distinction: whether an image is true is one question; how the soul forms an image is another. A dream of a familiar room shows image-forming activity even if the room appears differently from reality. Steiner then develops his spiritual account of the relationship between this activity and memory. GA 9 asks how remembered events differ from acquired capacities; the later lecture asks about the activity underlying recollection. Remembering, imagining, and acquiring a skill remain different.

See the idea in an example

You can write your name, but cannot remember every practice session in which you learned the letters.

What this helps explain

Remembering a lesson and retaining a skill are different. Steiner uses this distinction to prepare his discussion of how experience changes a person. Nothing in this example tells us that the skill came from another life.

Check your understanding

How does a recollection differ from a fruit of experience?

Reveal the explanation

A recollection presently represents an earlier encounter; it is not the original perception kept unchanged. A fruit is the capacity spirit extracts through assimilating experience, such as the ability to write. The capacity can remain when particular episodes fade. Steiner first explains this within one earthly life before arguing for a wider spiritual continuity.
