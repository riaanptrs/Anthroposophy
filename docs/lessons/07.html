Memory and the fruits of experience

You recognize someone you met yesterday. Today's perception and yesterday's encounter meet in memory. Later you may forget individual reading lessons while still being able to read.

These illustrate two continuities. Memory makes an earlier experience available now; a capacity is something experience has enabled you to do. Steiner calls enduring gains of this kind the fruits of experience.

Action gives continuity another direction. Pruning a branch changes the tree whether you remember doing it or not. The past can remain effective in the person and in the shared world.

The chapter first establishes these distinctions within one life. Its later extension beyond birth and death is a further spiritual argument, not something already proved by remembering or learning.

Two questions — optional

Is a capacity the same as a vivid memory?

See an explanation

No. You can retain a skill without recalling each learning episode. Their connection does not make them identical.

Do consequences depend on remembering the action?

See an explanation

No. The pruned tree remains changed even if the act is forgotten. Outward effects and inward memory have different histories.

Selected passage: capture 34

The human spirit grows through assimilated experiences.

English translation: Elizabeth Douglas Shields, 1910. Digital reissue: Delhi Open Books; publication year unstated.

What Steiner is saying

Steiner begins with a difference in time. Seeing the rose requires both its presence and an open eye; the immediate perception passes when that encounter ends. A truth understood about the rose, he argues, has a validity independent of this particular encounter. Soul mediates between the passing bodily impression and the spiritual content.

Remembrance supplies a further relationship. When someone recognizes a person met yesterday, today’s perception is joined with the earlier encounter. Steiner calls soul the preserver that brings the past into present life. A conception can therefore belong to inner life after the external object is absent; this is different from receiving the original sensation again.

Spirit receives both the good and true that it understands and the experience that soul brings from its own history. These experiences need not remain unchanged. A person can forget the separate attempts through which reading was learned and still retain the ability. The fruits of experience are the increased capacities that Steiner says spirit assimilates from soul’s history.

Action gives endurance another direction. Cutting a branch changes what subsequently happens to the tree, whether the actor remembers the cut or not. Remembrance brings yesterday into the person; action prepares something in the world for tomorrow. The chapter first establishes these two directions within an earthly life before extending its question beyond birth and death.

Key concept

Fruits of experience

Steiner uses this expression for the capacities acquired through assimilating experience, which can remain when memories of individual episodes fade.

How the idea works

  • A bodily encounter supplies an impression
  • Soul relates the past encounter to present life
  • Spirit assimilates its fruits as developed capacities

Course example

Someone practises tying a knot and later forgets the particular practice session while retaining the skill. This course example distinguishes a remembered event from an acquired ability.

An important distinction

Remembering an episode and retaining a capacity are different results. Knowing how to read does not require recalling every attempt through which it was learned.

Check your understanding

Which result is a fruit of experience in Steiner’s learning example?

Reveal the explanation

The source distinguishes the retained reading ability from the separate experiences through which it was acquired.

Why are remembrance and action discussed together?

Reveal the explanation

Remembrance brings an earlier encounter into present soul life; action leaves continuing effects in the world.

Why this matters

The chapter needs this distinction before asking what an individuality might carry forward. Its account of continuity concerns assimilated development rather than only a collection of remembered scenes.

Connection

The next paragraph changes the scope of inquiry. Steiner asks whether spiritual form, like bodily form, requires a history preceding this earthly life.
