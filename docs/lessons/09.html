Chapter 2. Re-Embodiment Of The Spirit And Destiny

Read PDF captures 39–41. Read all of captures 39–41, beginning with the transition from inward fruits to deeds and finishing at the end of Chapter 2.

Selected passage: capture 40

For only the one part of my deed is in the outer world; the other is in myself.

English translation: Elizabeth Douglas Shields, 1910. Digital reissue: Delhi Open Books; publication year unstated.

What Steiner is saying

A deed makes something enduring in two places. It changes the outer world, while the actor gains capacities and becomes related to what has been done. Steiner calls the effects a second, independent life of the soul. His argument concerns the continuing relation between these outer results and the individuality that acted.

Sleep supplies the first comparison. Night interrupts conscious activity, but the next day begins in circumstances partly shaped by yesterday’s acts. A house previously built does not disappear when its occupant sleeps. Steiner extends this pattern to the human spirit entering a new earthly life: interruption alone need not mean creation from nothing or release from earlier effects.

He then considers changed surroundings. A mechanic and a bank clerk moving from Europe to America arrive in a new place, yet their developed capacities help determine the work and surroundings they meet. Steiner uses this comparison to explain how an individuality could remain related to former deeds without returning to an identical physical setting. In his account, spirit-self finds a field related to its earlier activity.

The final distinction separates three relationships. Body is subject to heredity; soul lives within self-created fate, which the text names karma; spirit is subject to repeated embodiment. Karma concerns the relation between actions and a later field of soul-life. Reincarnation concerns the spirit that continues through embodiments. The soul joins inherited embodiment and continuing spirit during an earthly life.

The source makes a strong claim that joys, sorrows and possibilities of experience depend on former actions. It concludes that unprejudiced thinking can reach conviction concerning karma and reincarnation, while spiritual seeing can encounter past lives. These are Steiner’s claimed conclusion and ways of knowing. Present action remains part of the account because it continues to create capacities and consequences.

Key concept

Karma

Steiner names the self-created fate relating the soul’s present field of life to the spiritual individuality’s previous actions karma.

How the idea works

  • Body: heredity of form
  • Soul: self-created fate, or karma
  • Spirit: repeated embodiment, or reincarnation

Course example

Someone repairs a fence. The fence remains changed, and the person may become more skilled at the repair. This course example illustrates the outward and inward sides of one deed; the extension across earthly lives belongs to Steiner’s argument.

An important distinction

Karma and reincarnation are related but distinct: one concerns the effects of action, the other the spirit’s repeated embodiment. The source’s account does not identify a particular person’s earlier deeds.

Which set preserves the chapter’s final distinctions?

Reveal the explanation

The source distinguishes inherited embodiment, the soul’s self-created fate and the spirit’s repeated embodiment.

Why this matters

This distinction reconnects the chapter’s two directions of endurance: experience becomes capacity within spirit, while action creates effects in the world. Soul mediates both movements.

Connection

The chapter synthesis brings these relations together. Chapter III then investigates the physical, soul and spiritual worlds presupposed by this account of continuity.

Optional deeper study

The cave-animal comparison

Steiner also refers to animals entering Kentucky caves and losing sight. Its role is to illustrate a connection between activity, capacities and subsequent conditions. This historical biological explanation can be followed as part of the source’s analogy without replacing a contemporary account of evolution.
