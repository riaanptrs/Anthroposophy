The I: inward centre and spiritual openness

How does Steiner distinguish the I from the experiences it gathers?

Rudolf Steiner — Theosophy

4. BODY, SOUL, AND SPIRIT

Read PDF captures 24–26. Begin at the childhood self-awareness transition at the end of capture 24. Read Jean Paul, self-reference and the I’s relation to body, soul and spirit; stop in capture 26’s right column before the definition of spirit-self.

English translation: Elizabeth Douglas Shields, 1910. Digital reissue: Delhi Open Books; publication year unstated.

What Steiner is saying

Jean Paul recalls an event in which he became aware of himself as an independent being. Steiner uses the recollection to introduce self-consciousness, not to prescribe an exact age for every child. The relevant change is experiencing oneself as the centre of one’s life, rather than only referring to oneself as if to another person.

The naming discussion makes the inward standpoint clearer. A shared object-name can refer to the same table when different speakers use it. I refers to each speaker from within. Steiner treats this distinctive self-reference as an avenue to understanding the inward centre, rather than reducing the person to the spoken word.

The I gathers the experiences of body and soul and acts through them. Sensation, feeling and willing belong to its lived experience without exhausting what the chapter means by this centre. The text also distinguishes the I itself from the manifestations the seer claims to perceive.

The account then gives the I two directions. Bodily and soul life supply experiences from one side; spiritual content can be received from the other. The I acts in body and soul, but opens itself toward spirit. This prepares the next distinction between being oriented toward truth and making spiritual content part of an individual life.

Key concept

I

Steiner describes the I as the inward centre that gathers bodily and soul experiences, acts through them and can receive spiritual content.

How the idea works

  • Experiences of body and soul are gathered
  • The person refers to themself from within
  • The I acts through body and soul
  • The I opens toward spiritual content

Course example

Two people can say that the same table is heavy, but each uses I to refer to their own standpoint in carrying it. This course example clarifies self-reference; it does not establish every further spiritual claim Steiner makes about the I.

An important distinction

I does not simply mean selfishness. Steiner’s account includes receiving truth and goodness beyond a private preference.
