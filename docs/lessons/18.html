Lesson 18 · The path of knowledge

Thinking as the beginning of knowledge

Explain why Steiner begins with living thought, and distinguish receptivity from blind faith.

What does becoming an “empty vessel” mean in this reading?

1 · Read the passage

Begin with the book

Rudolf Steiner · Theosophy · GA 9 · Monges / Church, 1971 · Printed p. 154 · PDF p. 182

Man is a thought being, and he can find his path to knowledge only when he makes thinking his starting-point.

Anthroposophic Press, 1971; Henry B. Monges, translator; Gilbert Church, reviser. English excerpt checked against the supplied PDF image; Brazilian Portuguese is a new course study translation. Line breaks and line-end hyphenation normalized.

Open the source or edition index →

What this passage means

The emphasis is on thinking as the starting point. The surrounding paragraph treats a communicated account as a living formative force before the learner has personal perception. Read pp. 155–159 to distinguish understanding an investigator’s report, judging its truth, and claiming the perception oneself; the footnotes on pp. 160–161 preserve independent judgment.

The focus of this lesson

Explain why Steiner begins with living thought, and distinguish receptivity from blind faith.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

Understand the idea

Chapter IV changes the question from what the human being and the worlds are to how they can be known. On pp. 154–155, Steiner says that a thoughtful account of the higher worlds is itself a first step toward personal perception. He regards thought as a living force: assimilating another investigator’s description can awaken capacities in the learner. This is a claim about the formative power of thinking, not simply advice to memorize information.

The dialogue on p. 155 answers the reader who wants to see independently and fears being asked for blind faith. Steiner proposes beginning with an unprejudiced assimilation of what another researcher communicates. The researcher reports experience rather than demanding belief. On p. 156, he argues that healthy human understanding can grasp such an account before the reader has developed direct spiritual perception. Keep three acts distinct: understanding the account, judging its truth, and claiming to perceive its subject oneself.

Steiner then insists that genuine thinking requires work. On pp. 157–158, he distinguishes vigorous, living thought from idle abstraction and from the agreeable flickering of inner pictures. Thinking should give firmness and order to the soul; vivid images or intense feelings alone do not constitute the knowledge he means. His assertions about the health effects of spiritual development belong to this account and are not medical assurances supplied by the course.

On p. 159, the teacher gives a stimulus, while recognition is meant to arise within the learner. Steiner’s expectation is stronger than merely allowing a reader to consider an idea: he expects sustained thinking to lead to recognition and eventually personal perception. To explain him accurately, state that expectation. To assess it, keep the learner’s own reasons and questions visible rather than substituting the teacher’s authority for judgment.

The second movement is quiet receptivity to the world. The image of an empty vessel on p. 159 and the example of learning from a child on pp. 159–160 ask the reader to suspend habitual verdicts long enough for something new to be encountered. The footnotes on pp. 160–161 explicitly reject shutting out one’s judgment or giving oneself to blind faith. Their distinction is between a living impression and a premature, blind judgment, not between thinking and obedience.

Steiner connects this receptivity with selflessness, perseverance, and a valuation that does not make personal likes the measure of everything. In this course, a close-reading exercise gives that distinction a manageable form: first reconstruct what a passage actually says, then examine it. Understanding the author fairly and retaining an unresolved objection can coexist.

First work through a short argument; afterwards look back at the connections you made. Steiner distinguishes attending to a problem from making your earlier thinking the object of fresh thinking. This does not mean you were unaware while reasoning. Nor is a remembered answer the same as reconstructing how you reached it. The 1918 addition to Chapter 3 distinguishes passing thought-images from actively developing a thought. Chapter 8’s addition warns against judging thinking only by its abstract recollection. Work through the relations again; you need not reproduce the original excitement. A remembered concept can be understood afresh. A successful reconstruction shows comprehension, not necessarily agreement or a correct application to the world.

The result is 5. Your activity was connecting the operations. Looking back, explain what you carried forward from the first step and why you used it in the next. The review is a new act of thinking about the earlier one. The aim is clarity, not speed. Understanding these operations does not by itself prove freedom or independence from the brain.

Connection with the later lectures

Notice the activity of understanding

Try explaining why you revised an earlier answer. Notice the activity involved: you compare meanings, follow a relationship, and form a new judgment. In the later lecture, Steiner asks readers to attend to thinking as an inner activity, and connects this with his understanding of freedom.

The lecture makes this connection more precise. Steiner refers explicitly to The Philosophy of Freedom and relates thinking as pure activity, beyond sensory images, to the apprehension of morality and the consciousness of freedom. His meaning goes beyond making an efficient decision or having a strong preference. Our reading exercise is a starting point for that question, not the full experience he describes. You will return to freedom in Lesson 21.

Another passage asks anthroposophists themselves to understand what other people value and widen their horizons, rather than relying on blind faith or polemics. Steiner still presents anthroposophy as the more inclusive view; this is not a claim that every position is equally true. As a study practice, explain one reason a thoughtful reader might disagree with a passage before writing your response.

See the idea in an example

A friend describes an unfamiliar game. You first learn its rules well enough to explain them, then decide whether you want to play.

What this helps explain

Understanding a proposal and accepting it are different steps. Reading Steiner fairly similarly means learning what he is saying before judging it. The game example illustrates the reading attitude, not the truth of his spiritual claims.

Check your understanding

What does becoming an “empty vessel” mean in this reading?

Reveal the explanation

The child example and footnotes distinguish receptive attention from blind faith. Steiner expects thoughtful assimilation to develop recognition from within, while retaining the learner’s judgment.

Why do receptive attention and subsequent judgment belong together?

Pause and answer in one sentence, aloud or in your notes.

See a way to approach it

A learner first gives an account room to be understood rather than dismissing it from habit. Thoughtful examination remains necessary; receptivity is not a requirement to agree or report spiritual perception.

Original open question and answer

Does Steiner’s request to make oneself an “empty vessel” mean surrendering independent judgment to a teacher?

Show a suggested answer

No. The child example and the footnotes distinguish receptive attention from blind faith and from shutting out one’s judgment. Steiner wants a learner to assimilate an account thoughtfully and expects this to develop recognition from within. Accurately describing that expectation does not oblige a student to report agreement or spiritual perception.

Before moving on, explain the main distinction without looking at the text. Identify a passage in the reading that supports your explanation and record one open question.

How to assess your response

Check whether your explanation states the meaning clearly, keeps the distinctions, points to support in the source, and identifies the kind of claim: observation, argument, analogy or supersensible report. Return to any part you cannot yet explain. A well-supported disagreement can show understanding.

This course presents Steiner’s account as a subject of study. Understanding his claims does not require accepting them as established facts.
