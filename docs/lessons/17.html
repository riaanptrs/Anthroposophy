Lesson 17 · The three worlds

Thought forms, colour language, and the threefold aura

Explain the claimed forms and three aura aspects while distinguishing spiritual colour experience from physical seeing.

Why does the aura account require more than a colour name?

1 · Read the passage

Begin with the book

Rudolf Steiner · Theosophy · GA 9 · Monges / Church, 1971 · Printed p. 146 · PDF p. 174

Generally speaking, all that implies spiritual activity takes more the form of rays spreading out from within, while everything that arises from the animal nature has the form of irregular clouds surging through the aura.

What this passage means

Notice the contrast of form and movement, not just colour. Read it under pp. 143–144’s distinction between physical seeing and spiritual colour experience. Later, p. 152 includes naturally selfless impulses in the first aura, so “animal nature” cannot simply mean evil. Explain the three interpenetrating aspects before applying any colour-name.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

Understand the idea

The final subsection brings the three-world account back to human thought and soul life. Steiner says feelings, impulses, and thoughts become perceptible to developed spiritual organs. A thought’s content lives in the thinker, while its effects are said to stream into the spiritual world and toward another person. Thought-forms express both character and definiteness: clear thought has definite outlines; confused thought has a wavering form (pp. 140–142).

The aura is the proposed soul and spiritual outpouring around the physical person. The description distinguishes changing moods from more lasting dispositions. Examples connect sensory impulse, knowledge, love, devotion, and active thought with different forms and colour qualities. Similar intellectual activity can serve selfish pleasure or an objective, selfless interest, so motive as well as ability matters in the account (pp. 142–147).

The essential qualification on pp. 143–144 is how colour language works. The author says that seeing an ordinary blue surface includes a soul experience as well as a sensory impression. A spiritual experience can justify calling a quality “blue” by comparison, without presenting physical blue before an eye. He explicitly rejects an inner imitation of physical colour as the intended meaning. These descriptions are meant to communicate claimed spiritual perception, not any arbitrary symbol a learner invents.

Three species of aura colour are then distinguished: mist-like and comparatively opaque; illuminating, as though the space itself becomes light; and sparkling or raying, continually producing active life. They interpenetrate rather than form three separate painted shells. The author relates attending to each aspect separately to attending to sound rather than sight when listening to music (pp. 148–149).

The first aura reflects the body’s influence on the soul. The second expresses the soul’s own life beyond immediate sensory influence, before full orientation toward the eternal. The third expresses the eternal spirit’s mastery and activity through the person. First-aura impulses are not all called bad: p. 152 includes naturally selfless impulses, especially mother-love. The distinctions therefore require their explanatory roles, rather than a moral ranking of three colours (pp. 149–152).

The higher-aura examples relate comprehensive thought, love of beings, and selfless sacrifice to yellow, green, and blue qualities. Yet the same colour-name occurs in different contexts and aspects, with different shades and characters. The closing paragraph rejects character or thought reading from a hallucinatory aura. Read it alongside the many descriptions of soul qualities: the author’s intention is spiritual understanding, not a shortcut for diagnosing a person from a colour (pp. 152–153).

Addendum 13 is essential. Steiner acknowledges the scientific logic of comparing seers’ reports, but says spiritual disclosure cannot be forced on demand; he proposes training and conditions of perception instead. He also distinguishes his spiritual aura from the physically investigated phenomena discussed by Moritz Benedikt. This is a methodological defence of the account, not a reported experiment demonstrating it. Learners may examine both the explanation and the unresolved question of how such claims could be independently assessed.

A second source for colour-language

GA 10 reinforces the distinction explained above: sensory colour-words are used by comparison for the experience Steiner describes. Ask: does “not ordinary visible colour” mean “only an invented metaphor”? His answer is no: he regards the described reality as spiritual. Understanding his intended meaning and verifying that claim are separate questions.

Source: How to Know Higher Worlds, PDF pages 56; 106–108 in the 108-page capture (Dead Authors Society, 2018).

Colour language does not make aura a painted outline

Colour, Lecture 6 (5 December 1920), uses light in an expanded spiritual sense and relates it to thought. This helps explain why the colour vocabulary in Theosophy should be read in its stated supersensible context. Painting a blue halo can illustrate an idea, but its visible pigment does not verify the spiritual experience described. Avoid using a colour chart to assign someone a fixed personality or moral rank.

Colour language in the aura account is not a claim about ordinary coloured light.

See the idea in an example

When someone calls a voice “warm,” they are not reporting its temperature. A word from one kind of experience helps communicate another.

What this helps explain

This illustrates how language can work across kinds of experience. Steiner similarly distinguishes aura colours from colours seen by the eyes. Unlike a casual metaphor, however, he intends to report an actual supersensible perception. The voice comparison does not verify that perception.

Check your understanding

Why does the aura account require more than a colour name?

Reveal the explanation

The text distinguishes three interpenetrating aspects, changing and enduring qualities, shades, forms, and motives; a colour-name alone loses those contexts. Its colours refer to spiritual experiences by comparison with sensory ones, and the conclusion rejects hallucinatory character reading. A simple colour or photograph therefore does not establish the person’s spiritual qualities.
