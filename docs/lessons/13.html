Lesson 13 · The three worlds

From experience to capacity

Connect the account of spiritland with learning on earth.

1 · Read the passage

Begin with the book

Rudolf Steiner · Theosophy · GA 9 · Monges / Church, 1971 · Printed p. 112 · PDF p. 140

Just as the architect, without working with brick and mortar, works out the plan of the house in his drafting room in accordance with architectural and other laws, so too does the architect of human creation, the spirit or higher self, develop its capacities and aims in spiritland in accordance with the laws of that land, in order to bring them over into the earthly world.

Selected excerpt from the supplied English edition; Brazilian Portuguese is a new course translation. Line breaks normalized.

Open the source or edition index →

What this passage means

The comparison links earthly experience with the preparation of new capacities. The core relation is between forming an ability and later expressing it in material conditions.

The focus of this lesson

Connect the account of spiritland with learning on earth.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

Understand the idea

After describing spiritland, Steiner asks what the spirit does there between earthly lives. His answer emphasizes the transformation of earthly experience into capacities for a future life. This develops Chapter II’s distinction between retaining a memory and becoming changed by what one has experienced.

The architect comparison is useful: someone can learn through making a building, then bring an enriched capacity to a later project. The second building need not reproduce the first. In the book’s account, spiritual assimilation is likewise not simply replaying every scene of a previous life. The analogy makes a relationship comprehensible without proving that this process occurs between lives.

Earthly relationships and work matter within this picture. Spiritual development is not presented merely as the accumulation of private extraordinary experiences. What is learned through living with others contributes to further activity. Distinguish this proposed continuity from a measurable account of how a particular present talent originated.

Keep this idea

What is learned can become a new ability rather than a replay of the past.

See the idea in an example

After building a first bookshelf, a carpenter plans a second one more skillfully. It need not look like the first bookshelf.

What this helps explain

The earlier work has become increased capacity, rather than a requirement to repeat the same object. Steiner uses a related distinction in his account of development between lives. The practical example helps us understand transformation, but does not establish its occurrence after death.

Check your understanding

In this account, would a future capacity have to appear as a detailed memory of the earlier experience?

Show a suggested answer

No. The distinction between memory and capacity allows experience to be transformed rather than replayed. The claim that this happens between earthly lives still depends on Steiner’s broader spiritual account.
