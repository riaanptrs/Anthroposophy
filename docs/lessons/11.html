After death: desire, bodily organs and release

You still want to hear a favorite recording when the player is unavailable. The desire and the means of satisfying it can come apart. This is a limited analogy, not an observation of life after death.

Steiner says that bodily interests can remain after bodily death because losing an instrument does not immediately remove the soul's attachment to using it. Kamaloca names the condition of such longing and release.

Purification concerns what depends on the completed bodily life. Wanting a familiar taste depends on bodily tasting; developing care through sharing a meal is a different fruit of experience. Steiner says release loosens dependence on the former bodily means while fruits that can accompany the spirit remain. The distinction does not make every relationship or achievement worthless, and the meal example does not establish what persists after death.

Karma concerns another relation, involving consequences that can be worked out in further embodiment. Release from attachment to one bodily instrument does not cancel that relation. Neither distinction tells us the present condition of a particular deceased person.

See an explanation

Release concerns dependence on the completed body for enjoyment. Fruits concern capacities developed through experience that the account says can accompany spirit. Karma concerns continued connections through deeds and later embodiment. Releasing one attachment is not the same as losing a capacity or cancelling a consequence; the distinction explains the teaching without verifying an afterlife.

2. THE SOUL IN THE SOUL WORLD AFTER DEATH

Read PDF captures 50–53. Begin subsection 2 on capture 50. Read through capture 53, including the food-desire example and the account of purification; stop before the individual regional descriptions on capture 54.

English translation: Elizabeth Douglas Shields, 1910. Digital reissue: Delhi Open Books; publication year unstated.

What Steiner is saying

During embodied life, the soul makes bodily impressions inwardly significant and communicates them to the spirit. In the other direction, a thought can become a wish and then a bodily deed. The soul’s inclinations can therefore orient the spirit toward physical satisfaction or spiritual purposes.

Bodily death ends the body’s functioning as the instrument of this mediation. Steiner’s account then follows soul and spirit through the proposed higher worlds: the spirit remains joined to a soul that has acquired bodily interests. Releasing the physical instrument does not immediately release those interests.

The food example identifies the difficulty. Pleasure and the desire for it belong to the soul, but the palate and other bodily organs provide satisfaction. The desire can remain when its instrument is gone. Kamaloca names this condition of bodily-dependent longing after death; it is a region of desires in the account, rather than a geographical location.

Purification transforms that orientation. What can only be experienced through the departed body dissolves; fruits that can live with the spirit remain. This also differs from karma: bonds that can be adjusted in a further embodiment are distinguished from attachments to the bodily life just completed. Releasing the latter does not cancel the former.

Key concept

Kamaloca

In this section, Steiner uses Kamaloca for the soul’s postmortem condition of desires still connected with bodily existence.

How the idea works

  • Body ceases to function as instrument
  • Bodily-dependent inclinations remain in the soul
  • Purification releases those attachments
  • Spirit retains assimilable fruits

Course example

A person can want to hear a familiar recording after the player has broken. Desire and its means of satisfaction are distinguishable. This course example clarifies that relation, without establishing the source’s account of life after death.

An important distinction

Purification concerns bodily attachment; karma concerns relationships that can be worked out in another embodiment. Their roles are not interchangeable.
