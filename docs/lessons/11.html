Lesson 11 · The three worlds

After death: what is released, what remains

Trace purification through the seven soul regions and distinguish it from karma and spiritual assimilation.

1 · Read the passage

Begin with the book

Rudolf Steiner · Theosophy · GA 9 · Monges / Church, 1971 · Printed p. 92 · PDF p. 120

After death the soul has not immediately lost such a desire, but it no longer possesses the bodily organ that provides the means for satisfying it.

Anthroposophic Press, 1971; Henry B. Monges, translator; Gilbert Church, reviser. English excerpt checked against the supplied PDF image; Brazilian Portuguese is a new course study translation. Line breaks and line-end hyphenation normalized.

Open the source or edition index →

What this passage means

“Such a desire” refers to the preceding food example. Pleasure and desire belong to the soul; the palate supplies a bodily means of satisfaction. Explain why losing the organ and relinquishing the attachment are different events in Steiner’s account, then use p. 91 to distinguish this purification from karma.

The focus of this lesson

Trace purification through the seven soul regions and distinguish it from karma and spiritual assimilation.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

Understand the idea

In this section, Steiner first restates the soul’s role between body and spirit. Bodily impressions become inward experience, while a thought can become a wish and then a bodily deed. In his after-death account, the body releases soul and spirit, but the spirit initially remains joined to a soul still oriented toward bodily life. The soul eventually releases the spirit by relinquishing what depends on the completed embodiment while retaining fruits that can live on spiritually (pp. 87–91).

The author’s food example makes the proposed difficulty precise: the pleasure and desire belong to the soul, but the palate and other bodily organs supply their means of satisfaction. He says the desire can remain after those means are lost. Purification is the transformation of this orientation: antipathy is overcome and sympathy expands until the soul gives itself to its own world. It is not the destruction of the spirit or everything learned on earth (pp. 92–94).

In region 1, Burning Desire, unsatisfied selfish sensory longings are said to exhaust themselves. Affinity matters: the text says a soul without the corresponding longings can pass without feeling these effects. Region 2, Mobile Sensitivity, concerns dependence on the glitter and succession of external impressions. Without the physical objects sustaining this dependence, it dies away. Region 3, Wishes, loosens the remaining atmosphere of wishes that cannot find bodily satisfaction (pp. 94–96).

Region 4, Liking and Disliking, addresses identification of the self with the body. Steiner describes an initial feeling of emptiness and its transformation into recognition that bodily identity is not the whole human being. Release from this attachment allows sympathy to flow outward. His further claim on p. 97 about suicide is a historical spiritual assertion, not knowledge this course can apply to a deceased person or advice about bereavement.

Region 5, Soul Light, purifies even pleasure in beautiful nature, civilized comfort, or religious reward when it remains tied to sensory satisfaction. The author distinguishes this from perceiving spiritual significance in nature, which contributes to enduring development. Region 6, Active Soul Force, concerns activity pursued for the pleasure of acting: apparently generous artistic or scientific work can still have that motive (pp. 97–99).

Region 7, Soul Life, releases the conviction that all activity must be devoted exclusively to the sensory physical world. In the account, the soul returns to its own element and the spirit becomes free for Spiritland. Duration differs according to the attachments formed during life; the regions are not a uniform timetable or separate physical places (pp. 99–101).

Return to p. 91 for a necessary distinction. Purification relinquishes bonds to the incarnation just completed. Karma concerns relationships that can be adjusted through another incarnation. Spiritual assimilation, taught next, concerns the transformation of earthly fruits into capacities. Releasing an appetite does not cancel every consequence of a deed; these processes have different roles in the author’s wider account.

Keep this idea

Purification means release from attachment in this account, not cancellation of every consequence of action.

See the idea in an example

You move your favourite mug to a new cupboard, but still reach toward its old place.

What this helps explain

An impulse can remain after the situation supporting it changes. This modest example helps introduce attachment. Steiner’s description of desires after death goes much further: the mug example illustrates a relationship, not postmortem experience.

Check your understanding

What is relinquished during purification in the account?

Reveal the explanation

The attachments that bind the soul to the bodily life just completed are relinquished. Spiritually assimilable fruits can remain, while karmic relationships have another role across incarnations. The text therefore distinguishes release of bodily dependence from erasure of capacities, consequences, or spiritual individuality.

Why does releasing bodily dependence not mean erasing the whole past?

See a way to approach it

The account distinguishes attachments from spiritually assimilable fruits and karmic relationships. Capacities and consequences can have continuing roles even while dependence on the bodily life is relinquished.
