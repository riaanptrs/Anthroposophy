Lesson 11 · The three worlds

Attachment and release

Explain purification in the soul world without confusing it with karma.

Reading: Rudolf Steiner, Theosophy (GA 9), Chapter III, pp. 87–101.

Page numbers refer to the 1971 English edition (Monges, revised by Church); use the chapter to locate the passage in other editions. Explanations are original course material.

Understand the idea

The next section applies the soul-world account to life after death. Steiner describes desires that depended on bodily satisfaction as continuing when their bodily means are no longer available. Purification names the loosening of these attachments so that the spirit can continue its development.

An ordinary habit helps illustrate dependence: someone may keep reaching for an object that is no longer present. The illustration clarifies the idea of an impulse outlasting its means of satisfaction. It does not establish the book’s account of postmortem experience. Steiner’s description is a supersensible claim, not a conclusion supplied by this everyday example.

Keep purification distinct from karma. One concerns release from attachments in the soul world; the other concerns deeds and destiny across a wider account of development. The regions should not become a fixed timetable for an individual. The passage is a subject for textual study, not a basis for telling a bereaved person what someone is now experiencing.

Try it

Work with the idea

Make a two-column comparison of purification and karma. Add a third note explaining what the habit analogy illuminates and what it cannot establish. You may use a completely fictional example.

Use a notebook. This is a study exercise, not a promise of spiritual experience.

Check your understanding

Does release from a bodily attachment mean that every consequence of a person’s actions has been cancelled?

Show a suggested answer

No. In this account, purification and karmic consequences have different roles. Loosening attachment is not the same as erasing the relation of deeds to destiny.

Before moving on, explain the main distinction without looking at the text. Identify a passage in the reading that supports your explanation and record one open question.

How to assess your response

Give yourself 0–2 points for each: accurate meaning, clear distinctions, support from the text, and awareness of the kind of claim (observation, argument, analogy, or supersensible report). 0 = missing; 1 = partial; 2 = clear and supported. Revisit anything partial or missing. Well-supported disagreement can receive full marks.

This course presents Steiner’s account as a subject of study. Understanding his claims does not require accepting them as established facts.
