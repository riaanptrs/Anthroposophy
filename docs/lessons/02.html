Lesson 02 · The essential nature of the human being

Embodiment and inner experience

Trace the relationships among bodily organization, sensation, feeling, willing, and thinking in Steiner’s account.

Why does Steiner include willing in soul life?

1 · Read the passage

Begin with the book

Rudolf Steiner · Theosophy · GA 9 · Monges / Church, 1971 · Printed p. 8 · PDF p. 36

Thus it will be seen without further elaboration that even simple sensations belong to the inner world.

Anthroposophic Press, 1971; Henry B. Monges, translator; Gilbert Church, reviser. English excerpt checked against the supplied PDF image; Brazilian Portuguese is a new course study translation. Line breaks and line-end hyphenation normalized.

What this passage means

The word even matters: soul life begins with sensation itself, before pleasure, displeasure or an impulse to act is added. In the surrounding red-table example, sharing the object does not mean directly perceiving another person’s experience of its color. Explain why Steiner then includes feeling and willing alongside sensation.

The focus of this lesson

Trace the relationships among bodily organization, sensation, feeling, willing, and thinking in Steiner’s account.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

Understand the idea

The first three subsections develop the opening distinction. Steiner places human embodiment alongside mineral, plant, and animal existence: material composition, growth and reproduction, and perception with inner experience. He then describes specifically human organization in relation to thinking. This prepares the question of how one human being belongs to several orders of existence.

The soul subsection begins with sensation itself. Two people can look at the same red table, yet one does not directly perceive the other’s experience of red. Feeling adds pleasure or displeasure to sensation. Willing brings inner life back into the outer world through action: a person approaches, avoids, builds, or changes something. Soul therefore includes sensing, feeling, and willing; reducing it to emotion loses two movements of the text.

Thinking can examine both perceptions and actions. In knowing, it seeks the relationships of things; in acting, it brings reasonable coherence to life. On page 10 Steiner distinguishes the natural necessity of bodily processes from the laws of thought that the person voluntarily acknowledges. His point is that a desire does not by itself decide what is true or what action is justified.

The body remains the basis through which this occurs. At the next subsection’s opening on page 11, Steiner explicitly calls the brain the bodily instrument of thinking. The course follows this account of embodiment without adopting the chapter’s historical anatomical comparisons or the demeaning disability language in its Carus quotation as standards for human worth or present-day biology.

Use the bell as an original application of the text. Vibration and the hearing organ concern embodiment; hearing the sound, feeling irritated, and wanting to silence it belong to inner experience; examining the pattern or a considerate response introduces reflection. All can occur in one encounter. Steiner’s next step is to distinguish the physical, living, and sentient organizations that he believes make such an encounter possible.

Distinguishing body and inner experience does not yet tell us whether they are ultimately separate realities. In GA 4 Chapter 2, Steiner asks how self and world belong together. He challenges both an absolute separation with no explained connection and a declaration of unity that leaves the experienced differences unexplained. Use the distinctions to form the question; naming two aspects is not itself a proof of dualism, and unity need not mean that all aspects are identical. Chapter 10 calls Steiner’s account monism: percept and idea belong to one reality, with neither reduced to the other. Here monism does not mean that everything is matter. The first 1918 addition distinguishes shared conceptual understanding from the individual ethical action through which a person brings an idea into life.

Additional connection: The Philosophy of Freedom (GA 4), markers 13–16, 92–95 in the 147-page Basis transcription. Locate by chapter in another edition. Find the book.

A sound and what it expresses
Connection with Course 2 · GA 10

A sound and what it expresses

In How to Know Higher Worlds, Steiner contrasts a bell’s sound with an animal’s cry. Imagine a dog whining at a door. Separate what you hear, how you feel, and what you infer about the dog. The interpretation may change if you learn that it is waiting for someone. This helps distinguish bodily perception, inward response and an attempt to understand another being; it does not give direct access to the animal’s experience.

See the idea in an example

A bell rings. You hear its tone and feel annoyed because you were concentrating. Another listener enjoys the same sound.

What this helps explain

The moving bell and the bodily processes of hearing are the physical side. The experienced tone and your annoyance belong to inner experience. Soul includes the sensation itself, not only the emotion that follows.

Check your understanding

Why does Steiner include willing in soul life?

Reveal the explanation

Willing expresses inner life in an outward deed, so soul life both receives impressions and responds to the world. Thinking examines perceptions to gain knowledge and examines actions to bring coherence and direction. The person can acknowledge reasons instead of simply following each impulse. This connects body, soul, and spirit while preserving their distinct roles.
