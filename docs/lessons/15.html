Lesson 15 · The three worlds

Nature, formative beings, and human thinking

Connect the three worlds with form, life, sensation, and thought, including the three elementary kingdoms.

How do elementary kingdoms differ from visible nature kingdoms?

1 · Read the passage

Begin with the book

Rudolf Steiner · Theosophy · GA 9 · Monges / Church, 1971 · Printed p. 129 · PDF p. 157

They are only another form of the spirit beings, and when a man forms thoughts about things, his inner nature is merely directed away from the sensible form and out towards the spiritual archetypes of these things.

Anthroposophic Press, 1971; Henry B. Monges, translator; Gilbert Church, reviser. English excerpt checked against the supplied PDF image; Brazilian Portuguese is a new course study translation. Line breaks and line-end hyphenation normalized.

Open the source or edition index →

What this passage means

“They” refers to objects of the sensory world. “They” refers to sense objects. Steiner connects a thing’s sensory form and the thought that understands it through a spiritual archetype. Relate this to the ice-and-water comparison, then distinguish the visible nature kingdoms from the formative elementary kingdoms. Observing a form and accepting its proposed spiritual origin are different steps.

The focus of this lesson

Connect the three worlds with form, life, sensation, and thought, including the three elementary kingdoms.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

Understand the idea

Steiner now returns to physical existence: humans participate in the three worlds simultaneously. Sensory perception and bodily action relate to the physical; sympathy, antipathy, wishes, and feelings to the soul world; thinking to Spiritland. The ice-and-water comparison explains how perceptible things can stand out from a larger reality while remaining manifestations of it. The author explicitly calls their “condensation” metaphorical (pp. 127–128).

This also supplies his explanation of why thoughts can understand physical things. A thought about a stone and the stone’s perceptible form are said to relate to the same spiritual archetype. Thinking turns toward that archetype instead of merely duplicating the sensory object. The Kepler quotation on p. 129 illustrates the consonance between human understanding and natural order. The spiritual origin of that consonance remains Steiner’s explanatory claim.

Mineral forms exhibit external lawful interactions. Plants add growth and reproduction: unlike a finished crystal, a plant retains a shape-producing capacity. Animals add a changing inner life of sensation and impulse within an enduring living form. Human beings additionally experience thought as thought and can use it to guide impulses. Follow the sequence of added activities rather than treating the kingdom names as a list to memorize (pp. 130–135).

The first elementary kingdom consists of archetypal beings before shape is assumed. The second consists of shape-creating beings, described as invisible craftsmen of plant growth. The third consists of soul beings, whose activity underlies animal sensations and impulses. Elementary kingdoms name the formative relations in this spiritual account; they are not additional biological species hidden among known organisms (pp. 132–135).

The complete sevenfold list on pp. 135–136 joins those three elementary kingdoms to four visible realms: mineral, with created forms; plant, with forms and active formation; animal, with formation and soul activity; human, with these activities and spirit appearing as thought in the sensory world. The same plant therefore belongs to a visible nature kingdom while being understood through another realm’s formative activity. The lists answer different questions.

The last pages reconnect this account with Chapter I. Physical body, ether or life body, and sentient soul body are related to archetypal form, life, and soul; intellectual soul is where the human being becomes a conscious worker on itself. The body also links humans with mineral, plant, and animal activity. Read Addendum 4: Steiner does not say that animals contain no thought. He attributes their thought to a group-ego acting from the soul world, contrasting it with independent human thought (pp. 136–137; Addendum 4).

A useful diagram must preserve both observable examples and the spiritual interpretation assigned to them. Seeing a lily grow supplies the observation; a supersensible formative being is the author’s further explanation. Melting ice helps express manifestation, but it does not itself establish a spiritual source of matter. The lesson’s task is to reconstruct how the author connects the worlds without turning his account into contemporary laboratory classification.

See the idea in an example

Think of ice and liquid water. Their relationship helps us consider how a visible form can belong to a wider process.

What this helps explain

Steiner uses this comparison when relating the physical world to spiritual reality. It does not mean that spirit is literally liquid, or that heating matter reveals spirit. A comparison carries a particular relationship across, not every property of the example.

Check your understanding

How do elementary kingdoms differ from visible nature kingdoms?

Reveal the explanation

The three elementary kingdoms name archetypal and formative beings/activities in Steiner’s account, while the visible kingdoms describe formed beings in which those activities operate. They are different levels of description, not seven parallel biological species. The human realm also adds spirit appearing as thought.

What is added in the human realm of this account?

Pause and answer in one sentence, aloud or in your notes.

See a way to approach it

Spirit appears as thought in the human realm. The sevenfold account relates formative activity to formed beings, with this additional human relationship rather than seven equivalent biological categories.
