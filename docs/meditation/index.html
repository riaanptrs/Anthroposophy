Which practice should I choose?

Choose according to what you want to learn, what you understand and what you can sustain. These are learning recommendations from this course. Start Now! brings together instructions from different settings; it does not prescribe this choice guide or require everyone to perform every exercise.

Why give meditation a place in your life?

In Steiner’s account, ordinary attention is often carried along by impressions, habits and personal reactions. Meditation gives thinking and feeling a freely chosen, meaningful centre. Repeated practice is intended to strengthen the soul’s capacity for spiritual knowledge. Relaxation can accompany a sitting, but the book’s purpose reaches beyond feeling calmer.

The supporting work on character matters because increased concentration alone does not make judgment wise or conduct loving. Reverence, honesty, emotional balance and concern for others give the practice its direction. Ask both what you are learning inwardly and how you are meeting ordinary responsibilities. These are Steiner’s spiritual purposes; a stronger sensation is not proof that they have been achieved.

If you are beginning, start here

  1. Read Session 1 to understand the purpose, then Session 2 to learn how to work with a phrase. If terms such as soul, spirit or higher Self are unfamiliar, use the linked Foundations and Theosophy explanations alongside these sessions.
  2. Choose one phrase whose meaning you can explain. Use the short sitting in Session 2 at a quiet, suitable time. About five minutes is our starting arrangement; Steiner explicitly allows five minutes or less when responsibilities limit time.
  3. Keep the main content stable. If you also choose the supporting exercises, begin with connected thought about a simple object, following Session 3. Let the other qualities develop gradually in the source programme.
  4. After a week, review whether you understand the content and can return to it without neglecting your responsibilities. This is our check-in point, not a deadline for spiritual results. Continue patiently, simplify the arrangement or return to study according to what you find.

A daily phrase or verse

Choose this when
You want a straightforward starting practice for sustaining attention on a spiritual meaning, and the chosen wording is meaningful to you.
Why this practice
The work moves from understanding a thought to repeatedly letting it live in attentive thinking and feeling. Returning to the same content develops steadiness rather than a habit of collecting new techniques.
Preparation
Read the whole text, clarify its unfamiliar words and explain its meaning in your own words. Choose one wording for the sitting. The course’s daily verse is already provided in Session 2.
When and how often
Use a short regular period, morning or another suitable time. Five minutes or less is permitted in the 1904 instruction. Steiner also recommends keeping a meditation content for months or years if possible. The approximately fifteen-minute Wagner sequence is a separate, particular instruction.
Continue when
You understand what you are attending to and can renew the effort, even when a sitting is distracted or uneventful. Notice the ability to return; an intense experience is not required.
Reconsider or return to study when
The wording remains unintelligible, you spend the sitting arguing about its meaning, or the arrangement repeatedly conflicts with duties. Clarify the text outside the sitting, shorten the period, or choose another understood content deliberately.

The six supporting exercises

Choose this when
You are beginning or continuing meditation and want to cultivate the attention, initiative, balance, positivity and openness that accompany it.
Why this practice
They connect inner concentration with the development of the whole person. In the book they support spiritual training; in daily life you can examine whether you think more deliberately, keep intentions and judge less hastily.
Preparation
Learn each quality and its common misunderstanding: equanimity allows feeling; positivity still recognizes errors; openness still allows judgment. Begin with connected thoughts about a simple object.
When and how often
The six-month presentation starts with roughly a month of thought practice, then adds initiative, followed by the other qualities. Month six systematically alternates the five preceding exercises. Continue the earlier practice as far as possible; reading the table does not complete the months.
Continue when
You can identify an actual attempt at the quality, such as returning to a relevant thought or carrying out your own small intention. Allow uneven progress and retain the balance between qualities.
Reconsider or return to study when
You compress six months into a few days, try to practise everything at once, suppress all emotion, or use positivity to avoid addressing harm. Return to the relevant explanation and choose a manageable emphasis.

The backward review

Choose this when
You want to observe your own actions more clearly and connect practice with learning from the day.
Why this practice
Reverse order requires deliberate reconstruction instead of habitual replay. Viewing yourself within the scene, with the distance you might bring to another person, helps you distinguish what happened from your explanations of it.
Preparation
Understand the difference between reversing the order and changing the facts. Start with one small, ordinary episode; acknowledge gaps in memory.
When and how often
The source places the review in the evening. Begin with a small portion and extend it as fluency grows. The 1923 passage describes eventually reviewing the day in three or four minutes; that is not a required beginner speed.
Continue when
You can reconstruct an episode accurately enough to learn from it and then finish. A modest repair in tomorrow’s conduct can be a useful application.
Reconsider or return to study when
The review turns into repeated self-accusation or an endless replay of a painful event. Finish the exercise, choose a smaller ordinary episode another time, or return to the instructions. Exhaustive fault-finding is not the task.

The Calendar of the Soul

Choose this when
You want a continuing relationship with a weekly verse and the changing connection between inner life and the course of the year.
Why this practice
The Calendar aims at feeling self-knowledge through the soul’s inward and outward movements. It invites comparison between the verse, your experience and nature, rather than a demand to feel a prescribed seasonal mood.
Preparation
Read Session 5’s explanation of the annual pattern. Understand the chosen verse and its Christian festival and northern seasonal context. In a southern or tropical setting, make your way of relating the verses to local experience explicit.
When and how often
Return to one verse through its week. Four complete verses are provided for learning the method; use your full Calendar for the intervening weeks of the 52-week cycle. The course does not assign a universal hemisphere adjustment.
Continue when
Repeated encounters deepen your understanding, including when your actual experience differs from the verse. That difference is material for reflection.
Reconsider or return to study when
You force yourself into the stated mood, treat a date as a verdict on your condition, or cannot understand the imagery. Revisit the explanation and choose whether you are studying an example verse or following the annual sequence.

The Rose Cross

Choose this when
You want to explore a symbolic image after you can explain its preparatory thoughts and willingly engage with its spiritual meaning.
Why this practice
The symbol gathers prepared thoughts and feelings into one inward image. The intended exercise is the soul’s freely sustained activity with that meaningful image.
Preparation
Work through the plant–human contrast, human freedom and the transformation of impulses. Explain the black cross and seven red roses. Build the image with feeling before using it in meditation.
When and how often
Use it as an alternative main meditation rather than another required addition. During the sitting, let the preparatory argument rest and remain with the image and its prepared feeling. Renew the preparation outside the meditation as needed; this introduction sets no universal duration.
Continue when
The image retains the meaning you have built, and you can distinguish it from an external perception. Renewing preparation can be part of continuing.
Reconsider or return to study when
You are merely looking at a decorative picture, rehearsing an unexplained symbol, or treating a deliberately formed image as evidence of an external spiritual event. Return to the preparation. If visualization is difficult, study the meaning in words before deciding whether to choose this practice.

The Foundation Stone

Choose this when
You want to work with the whole meditation or an understood passage and can engage thoughtfully with its Christian and Anthroposophical setting.
Why this practice
Its movements connect spirit-remembering, spirit-reflection and spirit-beholding with living, feeling and thinking. The final appeal joins warmed hearts and illuminated heads with willing good deeds.
Preparation
Read the complete text in Session 7. Explain its bodily and spiritual references, the three practices and their relation to the ending. Use the earlier foundations when those terms are unfamiliar. The displayed wording is the course’s study translation of the printed German.
When and how often
Session 8 offers an introductory arrangement over four study sessions; it is not Steiner’s historical seven-day rhythm. After understanding the whole, you may return regularly to a chosen passage while retaining its relationships. References to heart and lung are not instructions to manipulate breathing.
Continue when
A chosen passage becomes more intelligible in relation to the whole, and its connection with thoughtful conduct remains clear. Keep the wording stable during the actual sitting.
Reconsider or return to study when
You select disconnected lines without understanding their relationships, continually switch translations during meditation, or find that you cannot meaningfully engage with this spiritual framework. Return to study or choose another understood practice.

How do I decide whether to continue?

Ask four concrete questions: Can I explain the chosen content? Can I return to it deliberately? Is the rhythm compatible with my duties? What can I observe in my attention, patience or conduct? Record a real example, or honestly record that no change is yet clear.

Distraction and dry periods alone are not reasons to keep changing techniques. Renew the understood practice patiently. If the difficulty is lack of understanding, return to study; if it is an overloaded schedule, simplify. Choose a different practice when its purpose and preparation fit your learning better, rather than to chase a stronger sensation.

Reading all nine sessions gives an overview; it does not require adopting all six practices. The source programme’s months and the Calendar’s weeks are practice rhythms, not deadlines for spiritual attainment.
