The seven chapters are anthology numbering. They are not seven consecutive Agriculture Course lectures. Each chapter starts with a short editorial introduction in the first two columns; the dated Steiner lecture starts in the third column. The imprint credits the editor, Marcia Merryman means, with these chapter introductions.

The 1912 selections cite Anthroposophic Press © 1992, with no translator named in the supplied imprint. The 1923 selections cite Matthew Barton’s revised translation, Rudolf Steiner Press © 2001. These credits accompany the selected passages; a single blanket translator credit would be inaccurate.

The course’s fourteen readings

  1. 00 — Reading an anthology with three voices: identify selection, authorial layers and digital locators.
  2. 01 — Farm individuality, rhythms and preparations: Courtney’s three features, preparation categories and disputed horsetail status.
  3. 02 — Courtney’s wider interpretation: plant/human correspondences, formative forces, later sources and explicitly personal synthesis.
  4. 03 — Learning to perceive nature: soul education, moral impressions, unity/plurality and the first two nature-being classes.
  5. 04 — Nature beings, rhythms and planetary spirit: the next classes, temporal activity, memory/conscience and the Earth analogy.
  6. 05 — Elemental plant activity: four ethers, upward/downward movement and Steiner’s winter-soil fertilization account.
  7. 06 — Threshold and displacement of spheres: consciousness relationships, constructive/destructive activity, nourishment and the afterdeath conclusion.
  8. 07 — Fertilizing as enlivening soil: whole/part reasoning, nourishment/forces, tree/humus comparison and etheric/astral relationships.
  9. 08 — Two preparations and discussion: seasonal/directional polarity, personal participation, machines, trials and enhancement of ordinary manuring.
  10. 09 — Soil forces and replenishment: capacity to receive nourishment, microorganisms and the lecture’s strong chemical assertions.
  11. 10 — Six compost preparations: differentiated plants/enclosures, soil responsiveness, discussion and Gardner’s qualifications.
  12. 11 — Desired growth and verification: near/distant planetary routes, seed reproduction, weed proposal and the certainty/testing tension.
  13. 12 — Pests, plant abnormalities and responsibility: organism-specific accounts, horsetail, qualified astronomy and the audience’s misuse concern.
  14. 13 — Source synthesis: connect the argument, preserve authorial differences and identify missing lectures and questions.

Each reading begins with a short verified source excerpt, then provides a labelled explanation, key concept, original example, distinction, two retrieval questions and an ungraded reflection. The first retrieval question has a choice interface with explanations and retry. Deeper material remains available in native expandable sections. The course’s orientation, parts, split readings and synthesis are teaching divisions, not extra numbered Steiner lectures.

Locator and diagram controls

The PDF is image-only. Its OCR occasionally confuses words, footnotes and handwritten diagrams, and damages highlighted text at the end of Elementals II. Source images were checked directly. Quotes normalize only ordinary line breaks and line-wrap hyphenation; no absent phrase, folio, translator or publication date has been supplied.

PDF captures count screenshots; displayed Kindle locations identify the screen shown, not exact word ranges. These are consistently distinguished from printed page numbers. Courtney’s introduction contains seven numbered figures. The agricultural lectures refer to blackboard drawings whose verbal descriptions are visible but whose drawings are not reproduced in these captures. The course must not claim to have recovered those absent diagrams.

Remaining source boundaries

This is a complete review of the supplied anthology. It gives direct local access to GA 327 lectures 4–6 and their discussions. The complete Agriculture Course, including lectures 1–3 and 7–8, remains a future source. In particular, Lecture 2’s farm-individuality argument is known here through Courtney’s introduction, not through a newly reviewed complete Lecture 2.

Courtney’s theological, historical and physical-effect interpretations are kept attributed to his introduction and its mediated sources. The lecture’s request for outward trials and its claim of inherent spiritual certainty are both retained. Studying a proposed mechanism is distinct from establishing an agricultural result.

Source claims, later commentary and original course adaptations remain separately attributed.
