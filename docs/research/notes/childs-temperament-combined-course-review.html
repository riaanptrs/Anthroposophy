What the new source clarifies

Childs’s Chapter 1 uses character for what imparts characteristics and helps identify habitual responses. He explicitly says moral respectability or criminality is not the issue in this definition (capture 10, column 2). The course also uses the term in discussing moral qualities. Attribution must make the difference legible instead of making one definition stand for both.

Chapter 2’s stable groups phlegmatic and sanguine patterns together. For Childs, changing interests can themselves form a consistent pattern (17, column 3; 18, column 1). This is not a claim that the sanguine portrait is always dependable. Introversion/extroversion and convergent/divergent thinking are his proposed psychological classifications, distinct from Steiner’s four-member explanation.

The complete book has a real tension about change. Chapter 2 rejects changing a child’s basic temperament (21, column 3); Chapter 7 says its use can mature while the temperament is given (48). Appendix I explicitly permits modifying and even changing temperament if the ego is sufficiently strong (78, column 3), then describes consciously exercising dormant compensating qualities (81–82). A main-course supplement should preserve that tension rather than silently choosing one assertion.

Chapter 7 gives more than occupational matching. It opens with mutual interdependence, acknowledges manual and intellectual aptitude (47), recommends guided practical trials (49–50), discusses advice and correction (50), asks for unspoken suggestions (50, column 3), and recommends a work schedule with a stated deadline (51, column 2). Childs also endorses graphology and offers type-based occupational generalizations. The companion should describe these as his claims. The main course’s demonstrated-skill, preference and review plan remains an original adaptation.

Chapter 8 first calls its sketches skewed and approximate, includes secondary temperaments, personality, health, upbringing and education, and allows unexpected responses (54, column 1). Its choleric/choleric example identifies a shared outlook as a bond irrespective of temperament and suggests exchanging responsibilities or assigning leadership by suitability (54, columns 2–3). Its choleric/phlegmatic example makes complementarity conditional on situation and secondary tendencies (56–57). The source still proposes predictability of typical reactions and contains gendered, hostile and aggressive caricatures. Preserve that authorial context; retain the course’s existing questions and revisable agreements.

Chapter 9’s ethical conclusion depends on an explicit spiritual account. Body, soul and spirit, soul mediation, existence before birth and after death, reincarnation and the four members occupy 69–75. Care, consideration and non-possessive love expressed in deeds conclude 76–77. This sequence should be visible in the combined synthesis rather than treating the conclusion as an isolated modern communication recommendation.

Appendix II remains important companion reading. It develops a historical body-type and embryological argument (83–95); it does not require another practical test in the main course. The existing distinction between a visible behaviour and an additional claim about bodily constitution remains appropriate.
