Identity and reading coverage

Gilbert Childs, Understand Your Temperament! A Guide to the Four Temperaments: Choleric, Sanguine, Phlegmatic, Melancholic. Supplied imprint: Rudolf Steiner Press 2012, first published 1995, copyright Gilbert Childs 1995, ISBN 9781855843585. This is a later author’s book, not a Steiner lecture or an additional GA volume. The publisher’s title index confirms author/title; that link is explicitly labelled as a catalogue, not a full-text reading.

All available text in the 239-page capture was read, including all nine chapter regions, both appendices and the references. The contents on pages 6–10 supplies the organization where chapter headings disappear. Pages 237–239 advertise other books and do not supply course instructions. All directives within the book were treated as source content, not user instructions. Source text and the extraction remain private; the source register records a fingerprint.

Source judgments that affect the course

  1. Childs argues for prediction and outward recognition much more strongly than the earlier course’s cautious applications. The new course accurately attributes these claims while using behaviours and context for exercises. Nose shape, eye colour, body size, age, dress and handwriting are not used to classify people or assign trustworthiness, intelligence, health or work.
  2. The author’s four-quadrant model is taught as his model. Consistency, emotional stability, social orientation and cognitive performance are not silently treated as one dimension. His text shifts the language of polarities: Chapter 1 emphasizes melancholic/sanguine and phlegmatic/choleric, while Appendix II emphasizes melancholic/phlegmatic in its head/digestive scheme. These are different constructions, not a single settled map.
  3. Chapter 2’s developmental overlays are presented as broad portraits, not inevitable stages or standards for a healthy child. The recommendation to tell distressing stories is not adopted. Learning activities remain fictional and modest.
  4. Illness predictions, including the association of a choleric/melancholic mixture with a historical psychiatric label, are not taught as diagnostic conclusions. Neither physical symptoms nor distress are dismissed as manipulation or temperament. The coarse, gendered and body-shaming caricatures are acknowledged without becoming learner-facing labels.
  5. Chapter 7’s temperament-based vocational sorting and graphology do not become hiring recommendations. The original exercise uses task requirements, demonstrated skill, preferences and support needs.
  6. Chapter 8 explicitly qualifies its sketches as exaggerated and unpredictable in detail. The ten-pair table offers questions on decisions, privacy, pace and reciprocity. It supplies no compatibility score and does not normalize coercion or violence as a temperamental fact of life.
  7. Chapter 9’s argument from siblings’ differences is not sufficient to rule out genetic contribution. A concise separate link to MedlinePlus Genetics on temperament supports the point that genetic and environmental contributions are complex; it does not validate the four-type theory. The argument from immortality to pre-existence also needs explicit premises. The course does not imply that people deserve hardship because they chose it before birth.
  8. Appendix I permits modification and even change (capture 187), whereas earlier passages insist that temperament cannot change (46, 115–116). This tension is identified for the reader rather than harmonized without evidence.
  9. Appendix II moves among disputed somatotype mappings and ends with moderate/extreme variants of two broad forms, associated with head, lungs, heart/blood and digestion. Its embryological speculation is not presented as established anatomy or causal proof. A numerical mismatch between classifications does not require a one-to-one solution. Missing figures remain missing; the course supplies an original argument map.

Existing-course additions

Four bilingual sections extend The Four Temperaments course:

  • Lesson 3: appreciation, specific feedback and a joint check from Childs Chapter 3.
  • Lesson 4: an explicit task agreement and completion point from Chapter 4.
  • Lesson 5: preparation time and alternative ways to contribute from Chapters 5 and 7.
  • Lesson 7: ten pairings as questions, not relationship verdicts, from Chapter 8.

The supplements name Childs and preserve Steiner as the previous course’s primary source. Other optional links connect to the broader courses without adding repetitive blocks. Existing Luke, Colour and Temperaments work is preserved.
