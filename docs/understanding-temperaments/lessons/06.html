Explore the chapter’s core ideas

Understand the concept

Steiner says the four fundamental types never occur in pure form: each person has a basic temperament mixed with varying degrees of the others. He even adds a phlegmatic element to his portrait of Napoleon as choleric. He values temperamental diversity and calls for direction rather than elimination. His outward portraits and statements about extreme illness remain specific claims within this historical lecture. Captures 9–10 include build, gait, facial expression and eye-colour claims; capture 11 lists lesser and greater dangers in historical psychiatric language. Read these with attribution, without turning them into a body-based personality test or a medical forecast. The mixed-type passage qualifies purity but does not retract the source’s spiritual or physiological claims.

What does a mixture mean?

A mixture means that more than one tendency contributes to the same person’s response. Someone may greet a project with sanguine curiosity, pursue a chosen part with choleric force and maintain a familiar task with phlegmatic regularity. The portraits distinguish movements within a life; a single label cannot describe their whole arrangement.

It helps to separate the beginning, continuation and aftermath of an event. What first attracts attention? What keeps the person engaged? What remains afterward? A person who starts quickly but repeatedly returns to an old disappointment presents a different pattern from someone who starts quickly and readily forgets it.

How context changes expression

A clear task may gather attention that scattered during an unclear one. Trust may let a forceful person listen. Familiarity may let a quiet person lead. Compare similar situations over time and ask for the person’s account. In your notes, describe the particular tendency and the conditions in which it appeared.

The claim that tendencies mix comes from the source. The three-stage observation above is an original study method for making the idea understandable. It does not assign percentages or establish Steiner’s spiritual explanation from behaviour.

A fundamental tone with other tendencies present

Steiner’s qualification is precise: the pure types do not appear in life, but he still proposes one basic temperament mingled with the others. His example is Napoleon as choleric with a substantial phlegmatic element. That is a more specific claim than simply saying everyone behaves differently in different settings. Our notes about context help examine an encounter; they do not establish the lecture’s proposed basic tone.

The appearance passages are also specific. Steiner connects the portraits with build, facial movement, gait and eyes, then qualifies the height comparison: he means relative proportion, rather than all cholerics being short and all sanguines tall. This limits that particular generalization; it does not withdraw the rest of his appearance claims.

Reading: New capture p. 9, column 3; p. 10, columns 1–3.

Direction, diversity and the language of extremes

The lecture values the diversity the temperaments bring and argues for directing them rather than making everyone alike. It distinguishes smaller dangers, such as uncontrolled anger, flightiness, apathy and attention confined to one’s own experience, from greater extremes. Its stronger warnings include blinkered pursuit of one aim and historical terms such as mania, idiocy and insanity. These are source claims, not outcomes demonstrated by the portraits. They belong to a critical reading of the book; they should not become predictions about a person in your case.

Reading: New capture p. 10, column 3; p. 11, column 1.

Age overlays and a tension about change

Childs distinguishes a predominant temperament from qualities associated with a stage of life. He describes a sanguine colouring of childhood, choleric overtones of adolescence, a more melancholic middle age and a more phlegmatic old age. In his account, the childhood colouring can coexist with another primary and secondary temperament. These broad age portraits therefore add another layer to his mixture model; they do not replace it.

The complete book also preserves a tension. Chapter 2 rejects attempts to change a child’s basic temperament, and Chapter 7 treats temperament as given while its use can mature. Appendix I nevertheless says people can modify and even change their temperaments if their ego is strong enough. Its closing practical discussion emphasizes consciously exercising dormant, compensating qualities. Keep these statements visible together rather than imposing a single fixed-or-changeable rule on the whole book.

Reading: Childs, Chapter 2, new capture p. 21, column 3; p. 24, columns 2–3; p. 25; Chapter 7, p. 48; Appendix I, p. 78, column 3 and pp. 81–82. Book companion Lessons 02 and 10.

Reveal the explanation

In The Four Temperaments, Steiner says a basic temperament is mixed with degrees of the others. That qualification concerns pure types; it does not itself establish whether an innate temperament changes. In Understand Your Temperament!, Childs’s Chapter 2 rejects changing a child’s basic temperament, and Chapter 7 allows behaviour to mature while temperament retains its essential attributes. Appendix I nevertheless explicitly permits modifying and even changing temperament through a sufficiently strong ego. Keep that tension visible. Lea’s varied actions and her account of uninterrupted repair time support a more precise description of behaviour and circumstances, without deciding either author’s inner explanation.
