The focus of this lesson

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

Understand the concept

The lecture begins with the riddle of each individual and offers temperament as the meeting of heredity and continuing individuality. All four members belong to each person; predominance and mixtures explain the typical pictures in this account. The practical argument works with existing tendencies in education and self-education. Its conclusion then asks for immediate understanding of each encounter: love when love is required, another quality when something else is required. Capture 13 supplies the full closing argument. Steiner links living wisdom with the foundations of society and calls authentic love of others spiritual science’s most beautiful goal. His claim concerns knowledge becoming responsive action, beyond abstract theorizing or moral sermons. Our final fictional case applies a reading method to an ordinary situation; it does not establish his spiritual premises.

Bring the four portraits together

You should now be able to explain each temperament without relying on its name alone. Choleric describes will gathered around an aim; sanguine describes interest awakened and redirected by impressions; phlegmatic describes inward contentment and stable rhythm; melancholic describes the inward weight and persistence of experience.

For each, connect a characteristic with its constructive expression, its possible difficulty and an educational route. For example: sanguine responsiveness can welcome new possibilities, lose continuity, and develop steadiness through a meaningful interest that is repeatedly taken up. That is an explanation of a relationship between qualities, rather than a memorized list.

What a complete explanation looks like

“Lea quickly becomes interested in new ideas and sometimes leaves an earlier task unfinished. This resembles the mobility of interest in the sanguine portrait. Her patient repairs show another capacity. Agreeing to complete one small sign connects her interest with continuity; her response will show whether the arrangement helps.”

Use the same structure for another portrait: define it, explain the relevant characteristics, give an example and a contrasting observation, then explain why a proposed response fits. Finally distinguish the behavioural portrait, Steiner’s spiritual interpretation and the practical adaptation you have chosen.

The social purpose of understanding

The closing passage gives the study a social purpose. Steiner moves from abstract concepts to an immediate understanding of the person one meets, using pictures that can address living experience. A meeting may call for love or for another quality suited to the situation. He describes anthroposophy as preparing a social foundation through which people perceive and understand one another, with love as its blossom and fruit. The conclusion asks what understanding enables between people, rather than how many labels a learner remembers.

Your final account can therefore explain how a more precise portrait changed a response. It should also identify what kind of source supports the explanation. The book records a Berlin lecture of 4 March 1909; the publisher explains that lecture reports can contain errors because stenographic records were often not revised by the lecturer. The selected English wording belongs to the revised translation, while the German reference supplies another textual witness. Neither should be silently presented as the other edition.

Reading: New capture p. 13, columns 2–3; lecture identification on p. 14; publisher’s note on p. 16.

Childs’s spiritual premise and practical conclusion

Chapter 9 makes Childs’s spiritual foundation explicit. He describes body, soul and spirit, with the soul mediating between the outer bodily world and inward spiritual life. From the premise of an eternal spirit, he argues for existence before birth as well as after death, and then reincarnation. He connects this account to the four members and their temperamental predominance. These conclusions depend on the spiritual premise of his argument.

His closing purpose is practical as well as explanatory. Greater self-knowledge should help us understand another person and act with care and consideration. He describes a non-possessive, unconditional Christian love that is expressed through deeds rather than mere sentiment. Retain both parts when comparing the sources: Childs is offering a spiritual explanation and an ethical aim. The course’s attentive encounter is an original way of practising the latter, with room to revise its understanding.

Reading: Childs, Chapter 9, new capture pp. 69–77: soul mediation p. 70; birthlessness and deathlessness p. 71; four-member account pp. 72–75; practical conclusion pp. 76–77. Book companion Lesson 09.

Course synthesis example

Worked final comparison: Lea’s garden notebook

My opening entry called Lea unreliable after she proposed ideas, left a meeting early and patiently repaired a planter. Her explanation changed the second observation: leaving fulfilled an agreement to collect supplies. Three later notes add an unfinished bird-sign project whose scope was unclear, patient repair supported by uninterrupted time, and a decision dispute in which only the materials order was urgent. Together these entries make agreements and working conditions part of the account, rather than treating every action as one enduring trait.

Mobility of interest describes attention readily awakened and redirected by a new impression. It helps me understand Lea’s welcome for new ideas and possible difficulty returning to one sign; her patient repair is a counterexample to a wholly scattered portrait. Rudolf Steiner’s The Four Temperaments explicitly qualifies a basic temperament with mixtures (PDF capture 10, column 3). Gilbert Childs’s Understand Your Temperament! likewise describes all four in unequal proportions (Chapter 1, captures 12–14). This overlap supports retaining contrasting observations; it does not establish Lea’s inner fourfold constitution.

Their self-education proposals differ in emphasis. Steiner gives reason an indirect task: arrange circumstances in which an existing tendency can work suitably; his sanguine example makes room for brief interest (The Four Temperaments, capture 13, columns 1–2). Childs asks the individual consciously to exercise a dormant, compensating quality rather than remain governed by the main tendencies (Understand Your Temperament!, Appendix I, captures 81–82). A short chosen task changes conditions; deliberately practising steadiness exercises a capacity. Our garden experiment combines these ideas as a course adaptation, rather than a prescription quoted from either book.

Childs also leaves a tension that this comparison cannot settle. Chapter 2 rejects changing a child’s basic temperament (capture 21, column 3), and Chapter 7 permits behavioural refinement while keeping its essential attributes (captures 47, column 3–48, column 1). Appendix I explicitly permits modifying and even changing temperament through a strong ego (capture 78, column 3). Improved follow-through would show behavioural development; it would not decide that stronger claim. Steiner’s qualification about pure types is compatible with mixed tendencies and does not, by itself, resolve Childs’s statements about change.

I would ask Lea to choose one bird sign, agree what counts as finished and offer a short period without interruptions. We would review completion and her account afterward, then repeat on three occasions. In a further fictional practice session, she completes the sign but misses an unclear handover; I keep the helpful quiet period and revise the agreement to name who receives the sign and when. Clarifying shared commitments still needs work. For a new project, observing, asking and reviewing transfer; its scope, resources and interruptions must be learned afresh.
