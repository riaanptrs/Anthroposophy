What this passage means

This illustrates indirect self-education: change the circumstances in which a tendency works instead of merely ordering yourself to stop having it. The small experiment below must name both the tendency and the deliberately arranged condition.

The focus of this lesson

Distinguish useful arrangement from repeated self-reproach.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

Understand the concept

In the final practical passage, Steiner argues that simply ordering oneself to have a different temperament accomplishes little. Reason can instead arrange suitable situations: short-lived interest where it has a place, a task where rage is ineffective, concern directed toward another, or, in his phlegmatic example, boredom brought to an extreme. We preserve the distinction between direct self-reproach and indirect arrangement without prescribing every historical suggestion. Our activity uses a small chosen task and a review point. It does not deliberately provoke anger, seek distress or impose prolonged boredom. The question is how to give a tendency a workable expression while retaining responsibility for action.

What self-education changes

Self-education works on how a tendency is expressed. Strong purpose can learn to pause for evidence; quick interest can learn to return; inward ease can become steady participation; serious concern can find an outward act of care. The aim is to develop a freer response while keeping the useful capacity.

Steiner’s practical distinction is between commanding yourself to be different and arranging circumstances in which a different response becomes possible. Our course turns this into small experiments that can be reviewed.

Choose an experiment that develops the capacity

For forceful purpose, ask one clarifying question before insisting on a plan. For shifting interest, choose one small completion before opening the next activity. For settled routine, make one agreed contribution alongside someone engaged in the task. For weighty concern, write the specific difficulty and take one useful step within reach.

Record what you actually did and what helped. An experiment can be useful even if it does not fit the temperament you first expected. Explain the movement you are practising: listening, returning, joining or turning concern into care.
