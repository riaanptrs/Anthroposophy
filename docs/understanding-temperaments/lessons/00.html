Explore the chapter’s core ideas

Begin with the particular person

Steiner begins with each person as a riddle. A temperament describes an underlying colouring of responses: what attracts attention, how feeling is stirred, how action begins and how long an impression remains. It gives a question to investigate when meeting someone. Understanding grows through attention to this person’s actions, purposes and circumstances.

In the continuing garden case, record what Lea does before deciding what it means. Leaving a meeting early could break an agreement or fulfil another responsibility. Ask about the circumstances and retain contrasting observations. The course uses these fictional situations to practise responsive understanding; they are our examples, rather than episodes from the books.

Four names, several kinds of difference

The traditional names are choleric, sanguine, phlegmatic and melancholic. Their portraits emphasize, respectively, purposeful will, mobile interest, inward contentment and lasting inward impressions. People can express several tendencies. Later lessons explore each portrait’s constructive possibilities, difficulties and proposed educational routes.

Temperament concerns the manner of responding. In our teaching, character concerns qualities cultivated in conduct, such as honesty or consideration; a mood is a passing state. Someone lively can act considerately, and someone usually calm can become angry. Childs also uses character for identifying habitual characteristics. Keep his particular definition distinct from the course’s ethical use of the word.

The two streams in Steiner’s explanation

Steiner’s account goes beyond a behavioural description. One stream is inherited embodiment received through generations. The other is an enduring individuality which, he says, carries the fruits of earlier earthly lives. Temperament mediates their meeting. His image of blue and yellow producing green illustrates a result shaped by both streams.

Reincarnation is a premise of this explanation. Observing habits or inherited features does not establish that spiritual continuity. Learn what the source claims, then distinguish it from what your example shows. The next lesson explains how the four members of the human being and their differing predominance develop this account.

How the three sources contribute

The two Steiner texts have closely related concerns; they are separately credited textual witnesses, not independent confirmation of a theory. Childs is a later author. Keep each source’s explanation distinct from the fictional activities created for this course.

Go deeper into the sources, terms and editions

Steiner begins with each individual as a riddle. Temperament is an underlying colouring of personality that manifests in everyday conduct, but his explanation of its origin is spiritual: inherited constitution meets an individuality developing through repeated earthly lives. Later he says pure types never occur; a basic temperament is mingled with the others. Understanding this colouring should support a response to the particular person. What is a temperament?

In this course, temperament means a characteristic way of responding: what draws attention, how readily feeling is stirred, how strongly a person acts, and how long an impression remains. It is the manner of a response, rather than the particular opinion someone holds. Two people can both care about a garden while approaching its work with very different rhythms.

The four traditional names are choleric, sanguine, phlegmatic and melancholic. In the portraits studied here, the choleric presses toward a purpose; the sanguine turns readily toward fresh impressions; the phlegmatic rests in a steady, familiar rhythm; the melancholic experiences impressions inwardly and dwells on their significance. These are starting definitions. The next lessons explain the characteristics and differences.

Temperament, character and a passing mood

Temperament describes how a response tends to unfold. Character concerns qualities developed in conduct, such as honesty or consideration. A mood is a temporary state. A lively person can act with integrity or act carelessly; someone usually calm can become angry. The temperament name alone does not decide any of those questions.

Steiner interprets temperament as a meeting of inherited embodiment and enduring individuality. His account therefore goes beyond a list of behaviours. We first learn the portraits, then examine his explanation of their spiritual background.

The two streams behind Steiner’s definition

Steiner gives temperament a more specific meaning than a habitual style of response. One stream is inherited embodiment: characteristics received through parents and earlier generations. The other is an enduring individuality which, in his account, brings the fruits of previous earthly lives. Temperament mediates their meeting. His colour image makes the relationship intelligible: blue and yellow produce green, a result shaped by both. The individual is therefore neither explained wholly by ancestry nor described wholly apart from a bodily inheritance.

He develops this premise through an analogy spanning crystals, plants, animals and human beings. Living form is transmitted through generations; he then proposes that an individual’s education and experience are preserved across reincarnations. Read the analogy as the reasoning of the lecture. Describing someone’s inherited traits or learned skills does not by itself establish this spiritual continuity. The full two-stream discussion belongs to the book companion’s Lesson 01.

Childs’s working meanings

Childs compares temperament to cards dealt at birth: basic tendencies provide material for a life, while individuality concerns how those cards are played. Personality becomes visible in actions, outlook and opinions, and includes differences of education and upbringing. His use of character needs particular care. In Chapter 1 it means what imparts characteristics and helps identify habitual responses; he explicitly sets moral respectability or criminality aside when defining that word.

The course also uses character when discussing honesty, care and responsibility. Keep those two usages distinguishable when comparing authors. Childs’s distinction asks how a shared tendency can be used differently by different people. It does not make the quality of their decisions identical. His further inference from consistent behaviour to bodily appearance is an additional claim, developed in the appendices, which the observation of one action cannot establish.

Where the names come from

Steiner did not invent the four temperaments. Their history reaches back to Greek and Roman humoral medicine associated with Hippocrates and Galen. Steiner offers a later spiritual interpretation, which must be distinguished from that older medical framework. National Library of Medicine: historical context
