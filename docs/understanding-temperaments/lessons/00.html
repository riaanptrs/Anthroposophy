Explore the chapter’s core ideas

Understand the concept

Steiner opens with the individuality of every person. A temperament is an underlying colouring of personality in his account, expressed through responses to the world. The four names describe basic patterns, but the lecture explicitly says that pure types do not occur in life. Its aim is practical understanding of encounters and development. This short book contains one lecture, given in Berlin on 4 March 1909. Our lessons divide its argument into study sections; these are not chapters written by Steiner. We distinguish his spiritual explanation, the behaviour he describes and our own teaching examples. The source’s physiological and illness claims are historical claims, not a diagnostic system used by this course.

What is a temperament?

In this course, temperament means a characteristic way of responding: what draws attention, how readily feeling is stirred, how strongly a person acts, and how long an impression remains. It is the manner of a response, rather than the particular opinion someone holds. Two people can both care about a garden while approaching its work with very different rhythms.

The four traditional names are choleric, sanguine, phlegmatic and melancholic. In the portraits studied here, the choleric presses toward a purpose; the sanguine turns readily toward fresh impressions; the phlegmatic rests in a steady, familiar rhythm; the melancholic experiences impressions inwardly and dwells on their significance. These are starting definitions. The next lessons explain the characteristics and differences.

Temperament, character and a passing mood

Temperament describes how a response tends to unfold. Character concerns qualities developed in conduct, such as honesty or consideration. A mood is a temporary state. A lively person can act with integrity or act carelessly; someone usually calm can become angry. The temperament name alone does not decide any of those questions.

Steiner interprets temperament as a meeting of inherited embodiment and enduring individuality. His account therefore goes beyond a list of behaviours. We first learn the portraits, then examine his explanation of their spiritual background.

Where the names come from

Steiner did not invent the four temperaments. Their history reaches back to Greek and Roman humoral medicine associated with Hippocrates and Galen. Steiner offers a later spiritual interpretation, which must be distinguished from that older medical framework. National Library of Medicine: historical context
