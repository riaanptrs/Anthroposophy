Explore the chapter’s core ideas

Understand the concept

Childs begins with mutual dependence and rejects workplace status games. He says temperament retains its essential attributes, while behaviour can mature and be refined; disciplined practice may bring secondary and other temperamental qualities into use. His occupational portraits connect choleric initiative with leadership, phlegmatic regularity with dependable work, sanguine sociability with fast-moving people-facing work, and melancholic empathy with continuity and caring work. He also gives aptitude, application, guided hands-on experience and changing circumstances a place. The complete chapter is supplied in captures 47–52. Childs recommends temperament-sensitive job selection and useful indications from graphology; these are his proposals. Our existing team exercise assesses demonstrated skills, experience, preferences and task requirements, with roles open to review. It illustrates coordination rather than validating handwriting assessment or assigning an occupation from a temperament label. Appendix I later permits changing temperament with a strong ego; read that formulation alongside this chapter’s distinction rather than silently treating them as one rule.

What each tendency can contribute to a group

The choleric portrait highlights the energy to decide and begin. The sanguine highlights openness to people and new possibilities. The phlegmatic highlights continuity and an even pace. The melancholic highlights careful concern for meaning, consequences and what may be overlooked. A group often needs all these functions during the same project.

Their difficulties can also meet: urgency can feel like pressure, novelty like distraction, steadiness like delay, and careful concern like pessimism. Explaining the underlying movement helps the group discuss what is needed at that stage of the work.

Turn a characteristic into a useful contribution

During planning, give new ideas a clear hearing; during a decision, identify who is responsible; during execution, establish a reliable rhythm; during review, examine concerns carefully. These are functions everyone can develop. Use demonstrated ability and willingness to choose roles.

For example, “You notice possible weaknesses; would you check the drainage?” connects concern with a defined contribution. It also leaves room for the person to say that a different role suits them better.

Interdependence, aptitude and guided experience

Childs opens the workplace chapter with mutual dependence: rank and one-upmanship can obstruct the cooperation on which everyone relies. He gives aptitude, whether manual or intellectual, a central place in deciding who should do what. His occupational portraits propose matches between temperament, skills and work, but he also recommends hands-on experience under guidance, even on a trial basis. The chapter thus includes an opportunity to learn how a person actually works, beyond the initial portrait.

Reading: Childs, Chapter 7, new capture p. 47, columns 1–3; p. 49, column 3; p. 50, column 1. Book companion Lesson 07.

Communication can reveal an unused contribution

Within his four portraits, Childs makes several concrete proposals. Initiative may need experienced advice and correction; quiet, methodical work may be disrupted by constant interruptions. A worker may have noticed an improvement and stayed silent because nobody asked. Childs recommends regular enquiries, and elsewhere a job schedule with a stated deadline. These details concern communication and support as well as task distribution. The course’s skill-and-preference plan is an original adaptation: the new source detail strengthens its invitations to ask, try and review.

Reading: Childs, new capture p. 50, columns 1–3; p. 51, columns 1–2. Developing secondary qualities: p. 48, columns 1–3.
