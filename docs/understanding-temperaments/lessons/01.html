What this passage means

The members belong to one human constitution. Predominance describes their relationship; the preceding pages explain temperament as the meeting of inherited embodiment and reincarnating individuality.

The focus of this lesson

Learn the mapping without confusing predominance with exclusive possession.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

Four members within one human being

Steiner describes the physical body as the member shared with the mineral world. The etheric or life body concerns living organization, growth and metabolism. The astral body bears sensations, desires, passions and changing contents of experience. The bearer of the I gives human self-awareness. These are concepts in his spiritual account of the human being.

All four belong to the same person. Predominance means that one gives their relationship a particular stamp; it does not mean that someone possesses only one member. The lecture associates the I with choleric temperament, the astral with sanguine, the etheric with phlegmatic and the physical with melancholic.

Four movements of response

In the choleric portrait, will gathers around a purpose and meets resistance with force. In the sanguine portrait, interest responds quickly to impressions and readily turns toward another. The phlegmatic portrait emphasizes inward ease and familiar rhythm. The melancholic portrait emphasizes the inward weight and persistence of an experience.

Imagine rain damaging the garden. One response begins organizing repairs; another notices a new possibility; another steadily restores the routine; another stays with the loss and its meaning. These are original illustrations of the four movements. A person may show several, and circumstances can change their expression. Compare the direction of a response before treating its outward energy or quietness as decisive.

Separate a portrait from its proposed mechanism

Steiner adds bodily correspondences: the I with blood circulation, the astral with nerves, the etheric with glands and the physical with its bodily foundation. These further claims help explain how he connects the members with the portraits. Observed initiative, distraction or quietness does not demonstrate the proposed constitution or physiological mechanism.

Childs later introduces psychological coordinates such as introversion and extroversion, thinking styles and stability. They are his classifications, distinct from Steiner’s four-member explanation. The comparison below keeps the portraits visible while the deeper readings explain these additional models. Go deeper into the sources, terms and editions

Steiner describes the same four members in every earthly human being: physical body, etheric or life body, astral body and bearer of the I. Their relationship is configured by the meeting of heredity and reincarnating individuality. One member stamps the combination through predominance: I-bearer/choleric, astral/sanguine, etheric/phlegmatic, physical/melancholic. Predominance is relational, not exclusive possession of a member. The physical body belongs to bodily existence; the etheric is a supersensible life sheath integrated into it; the astral bears drives, passions, desires and changing sensations and thoughts; the I-bearer enables self-awareness. The lecture then links I, astral and etheric respectively with blood circulation, nerves and glands, and extends its account to bodily appearance. These are its spiritual physiological and physiognomic claims. The course uses the map to understand the text, while its ordinary example corrects the mistake that only one type has a given member.

The four portraits in everyday language

Choleric: energy gathers around an aim. Typical characteristics in this portrait are initiative, decisiveness, forceful expression and persistence against resistance. When the tendency becomes one-sided, determination can become impatience or domination.

Sanguine: attention is readily caught by what is new and engaging. Typical characteristics are liveliness, curiosity, quick enthusiasm and a readiness to make contact. When the tendency becomes one-sided, attention moves away before an activity is completed.

Phlegmatic: a familiar rhythm and inward ease have a strong attraction. Typical characteristics are calmness, regularity, patient continuity and a slower entry into new activity. When the tendency becomes one-sided, contentment can become disengagement.

Melancholic: an experience makes a weighty inward impression. Typical characteristics are sensitivity, reflection, seriousness and sustained concern. When the tendency becomes one-sided, a difficulty can occupy attention so completely that alternatives recede.

Compare their response to the same event

Imagine that rain damages a shared garden. The choleric emphasis is “What must we do, and who will start?” The sanguine emphasis notices a new possibility and wants to explore it. The phlegmatic emphasis seeks a calm sequence for restoring the usual work. The melancholic emphasis stays with what was lost and why it matters. These original examples make the differences visible; each person may show several of them.

Steiner connects these portraits with four members of the human being. The table below gives his pairings. “Predominance” means a stronger influence within their relationship, not that a person possesses only one member. Learn the behavioural portrait and the proposed spiritual explanation as two related parts of his argument.

What the four members mean in this lecture

The physical body is the member shared with the mineral world. Steiner calls the etheric body the first supersensible sheath, integrated with the physical body until death; it concerns life, growth and metabolism. The astral body bears instincts, drives, passions, desires and the changing contents of sensation and thought. The bearer of the I gives human self-awareness. These are definitions within his account of the human being, not four visible personality traits. The editorial note also relates etheric life to plants and astral life to animals.

Reading: New capture p. 8, columns 1–2; p. 9, column 2; editorial note 4 on p. 14, column 2.

The bodily correspondences are a further claim

Steiner associates the I with blood circulation, the astral with nerves, the etheric with glands, and the physical body with its own bodily organization. His sanguine explanation adds that blood restrains nervous activity; his melancholic explanation describes bodily resistance to the other members. Distinguish these proposed mechanisms from a description such as changing interest or felt burden. Knowing the pairings explains the lecture’s reasoning; it does not turn a bodily measurement into a temperament assessment.

Reading: New capture p. 8, columns 2–3; p. 9, columns 1–3.

Childs adds psychological coordinates

Alongside the four names, Childs introduces introversion and extroversion, convergent and divergent thinking, and stability and instability. He places phlegmatic and melancholic tendencies on the more inward, convergent side, and choleric and sanguine tendencies on the more outward, divergent side. Convergent thinking is described through sustained concentration and method; divergent thinking through imagination, flexibility and variety. He explicitly states that the two groups do not show an appreciable difference in their range of intelligence.

His use of stable is also particular. He groups phlegmatic and sanguine temperaments together because a pattern can remain consistent even when it is a pattern of changing interests. Stable therefore does not mean motionless or necessarily dependable. These psychological coordinates are Childs’s proposed classifications. They supplement his portraits; they are distinct from Steiner’s explanation through the physical, etheric and astral members and the I.

Reading: Childs, Chapter 2, new capture pp. 15–18, especially p. 17, columns 1–3 and p. 18, column 1. Book companion Lesson 02.

Read the supplied terminology through its argument

The Mystery copy sometimes uses ethereal for etheric, and me or self for the I. Its four-member discussion explains the life body through growth and preservation, the astral through sensations and desires, and the I through self-consciousness. These variations in English wording do not add extra members. Its mediation passage explains how the inherited and individual streams meet before it develops predominance among the four members.

The copy also sometimes uses blood where the surrounding portrait is sanguine: a child’s changing interest, a light step or mobile astral expression. Use sanguine in the course explanation of that portrait, while keeping any selected source passage’s actual printed wording. This is distinct from literal blood circulation, which the source associates with the I and the choleric portrait. Following the surrounding argument prevents an irregular word from reversing the pairings.

Reading: Mystery PDF capture 9, column 3 through 10, column 1 (mediation); 10–11 (four members); 13–15 (pairings); 17, columns 2–3 and 21, column 3 (irregular sanguine wording). Book companion Lessons 03–05.
