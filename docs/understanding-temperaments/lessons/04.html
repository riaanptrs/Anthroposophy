What this passage means

The focus of this lesson

Explain how inner well-being and reflected interest connect in Steiner’s phlegmatic account.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

Understand the concept

Steiner associates this temperament with predominance of the etheric or life body. He describes inward well-being and attention to internal processes, leaving external events to take their course. His educational proposal uses companions with varied interests: the child becomes interested through seeing those interests reflected in others. He also proposes giving impassivity objects and events toward which it is fitting. The description is present in capture 9; the child-education passage is complete in 12. All four members still belong to the person. The associations with metabolism, glands, bodily build and gait belong to Steiner’s spiritual physiology. Our seed-planting example is an ordinary fictional adaptation; it does not reproduce the whole proposal or establish a person’s constitution.

What does phlegmatic mean?

The phlegmatic temperament is the portrait of inward contentment and a relatively even rhythm. External events do not immediately pull the person away from that rhythm. A new task may take time to attract active interest, while a familiar activity can be continued with considerable regularity.

Characteristics, strengths and difficulties

Typical characteristics are calmness, patience, comfort with routine and a less hurried response to demands. Constructively, this can bring dependable maintenance, steadiness in a tense group and an ability to keep doing ordinary work after others lose interest.

The difficulty arises when contentment closes off participation. An invitation may pass without becoming a personal concern; familiar comfort may outweigh a reason to change. The question is how steadiness becomes engaged activity. Quietness by itself does not answer that question: someone can be silently and intensely involved.

How phlegmatic differs from melancholic

Both may appear quiet. In the phlegmatic portrait, the person tends to remain at ease within a familiar rhythm. In the melancholic portrait, an experience can remain inwardly weighty and occupy thought. One person quietly waters the seedlings as usual; another quietly reflects on last year’s loss. Similar outward volume can accompany very different inner directions.

Why shared interest is an invitation

Steiner associates this temperament with the etheric or life member. His educational proposal emphasizes the interests of companions: a child may become involved in an activity through being with others who care about it. The relationship helps the activity enter the person’s circle of concern.

For example, invite someone to sort seeds beside an interested partner, with a clear contribution and time to settle into it. Once participation begins, the same steadiness can sustain the work. The invitation develops an existing strength by connecting it with the shared task.

Companionship and a fitting environment

The lecture’s phlegmatic proposal has two parts. Companions should bring varied interests: something that does not draw the child directly may become interesting through another child’s enthusiasm. Steiner then asks for objects and events toward which an impassive attitude is appropriate. This second clause gives inward ease a fitting place instead of demanding that it disappear everywhere.

He does not supply a fixed list of matching objects. A quiet stage of an agreed activity is an original example of how the course might interpret this idea. Compare that with joining an interested companion: one arrangement makes room for a settled pace, while the other offers a route outward. The question is how each condition serves participation, rather than whether the person becomes more visibly animated.
