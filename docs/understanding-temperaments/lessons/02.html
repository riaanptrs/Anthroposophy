Choleric: purpose and correction

Compare the four and clarify the birth-date question →

By the end of this lesson: Explain purposeful will, distinguish it from anger and show how initiative can accept correction.

Begin with the experience

Rain cancels the outdoor gathering. Your first response is to find an indoor venue, assign tasks and get everyone moving. In the choleric portrait, attention gathers around a purpose and the will presses toward action.

Understand the concept

The choleric portrait centres on will directed toward an aim: something should happen, and obstacles call for action. Attention can be mobilized quickly and held by a challenge. This can appear as initiative, courage and persistence. Anger may arise when a purpose is frustrated, but anger is not the whole temperament; a calm, determined action may express the same emphasis.

Follow the relationship

A strength becomes one-sided when achieving the aim becomes more important than hearing facts or considering other people. Persistence then turns into rigidity, and initiative into taking over. Development means retaining the ability to act while learning to revise the action. It does not require replacing every decisive response with hesitation.

Develop the explanation

You might recognize satisfaction in solving obstacles, deciding quickly or taking responsibility. These can become initiative, courage and persistence. Choleric does not simply mean angry: anger is one possible response when the purpose meets resistance.

Examine the response

The same energy becomes difficult when urgency turns into interruption, control or refusal to revise a plan. Others may comply without having been heard. Finishing quickly can then conceal a practical mistake or a damaged relationship.

Try a considerate adjustment

Try a small voluntary change: before assigning tasks, hear two suggestions and name one fact that could change your plan. Keep the initiative while allowing correction. Afterwards, notice whether the decision included information you would otherwise have missed.

A further example

In a fictional parent-child scene, a child insists a model bridge must stand immediately. The parent can acknowledge the wish to finish, set a calm boundary against grabbing tools and offer to test one stable joint together. The response supports effort and skill without a power struggle or a permanent label.

Keep the context in view

These are illustrations, not predictions about everyone called choleric. A deadline, fatigue or responsibility for the group may also explain urgency. The useful discovery is a response you can adjust, not proof of a fixed inner constitution.

A worked example, step by step

1. The situation

A group is building a model bridge. One person immediately takes charge, but refuses a partner’s warning that a joint is loose.

2. What helps explain it

The helpful contribution is organizing effort around completion. The difficulty is treating correction as an obstacle to authority, even though the warning serves the shared aim.

3. A possible response

The person can say, “Show me the loose joint before we continue.” Testing it keeps initiative and adds information. The bridge, rather than a contest of wills, becomes the common focus.

Reading the historical claims

The retained readings contain historical claims about bodily appearance, physiology, illness and ways of educating or handling people. These are attributed claims, not established diagnostic criteria or instructions to follow. Do not infer temperament from body shape, eye colour, ethnicity, sex, disability or birthday. Melancholic does not mean depression, and sanguine does not mean ADHD.

Constitutional charts are source- and age-specific: the 1909 pairings must not be applied universally to children. GA 295 explicitly gives different child pairings. Historical proposals involving fabricated distress, deliberate frustration, deprivation or coercion are not adopted as course practices. Use honest, respectful and revisable responses.

The focus of this lesson

Understand the movement from forceful insistence toward capable action.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

Understand the concept

In Steiner’s choleric portrait, predominance of the I-bearer appears as forceful will, insistence on one’s own way and the wish to overcome obstacles. His proposed bodily correlate is blood circulation. The lesser danger he names is uncontrolled temper; the greater is a foolishly blinkered pursuit of one aim. Education begins with respect for an educator’s worth and competence and gives will real resistance to encounter. The source says the child should trust the teacher’s grasp of what is happening and encounter challenging situations rather than an entirely easy life. These are actual proposals in the lecture. The course’s bridge construction is a later adaptation: a manageable task, demonstrable skill and a clear boundary. Honest correction of an error is our account of competence. Body size, eye colour and gait in the portrait remain attributed physiognomic claims, not ways to classify a learner.

What does choleric mean?

The choleric temperament is the portrait of concentrated will: a person forms an intention and brings energy to making it happen. The central movement is from “I want to do this” toward action, especially when something stands in the way. The word is often associated with anger, but the underlying idea here is force of purpose. Anger is one possible expression when that purpose meets resistance.

Characteristics, strengths and difficulties

Look for the connected pattern: readiness to take the lead, direct speech, quick decisions, energetic action and a wish to overcome obstacles. In a constructive expression, this can bring courage, responsibility and perseverance. The person may be willing to begin a difficult task that others hesitate to undertake.

The same force can become impatience with delay, difficulty accepting correction, or pressure on others to follow one plan. The distinction is in how the purpose meets reality: perseverance adjusts the method when evidence changes; stubbornness keeps insisting even when the method fails.

A concrete example

A group cannot assemble a planter. One member says, “Let us measure it, choose the right tool and finish the frame.” This illustrates initiative directed toward a task. If the member dismisses the measurement because it contradicts their plan, the same insistence obstructs the work. What changed was the willingness to let the task correct the intention.

Why Steiner recommends competence and real difficulty

Steiner associates this temperament with the I-bearer and emphasizes respect for someone who can actually do something. In his educational account, a meaningful challenge gives strong will something worthy to meet. The adult demonstrates skill and allows the work itself to reveal what succeeds.

In our practical adaptation, acknowledge the aim, state the concrete obstacle and invite a competent next step: “You want it finished today. This joint moves. Show me how we can make it secure.” The aim is to develop purposeful strength together with judgment.
