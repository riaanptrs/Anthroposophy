Explore the chapter’s core ideas

The reading’s key idea

The opening distinguishes the enigma of the human being in general from the enigma of each person we meet. Knowledge of general human nature does not yet explain this individual. Temperament names an intermediate level: recurring qualities shared by groups, differently mixed and expressed in each person. The text explicitly says that four groups cannot define the particular case exactly. Its further claim is that the visible exterior expresses a more complex inner being, which spiritual science seeks to understand. For Steiner, this understanding matters because social life and our feelings toward others depend on how we meet their individuality.

The source’s claim that spiritual science can reveal the inner human being is part of its explanatory position. Our ordinary comparison of the colleague in a meeting and on a walk illustrates the need to refine a description; it does not establish spiritual perception. Read the group names together with the qualification about individual mixtures.

The interval between a group and a person

Two forms of the human enigma

The opening first names the human being as the great enigma of physical life. It then turns from humanity in general to the distinct person we meet. These questions differ: one seeks the nature common to human beings; the other asks how to understand someone whose inner life is particular. The source says there is a large space between general human nature and what lives in each individual. Its social concern begins here. Our conduct toward another person depends on how we meet this particular enigma, including the feelings awakened in that meeting.

Supplied PDF capture 3.

What temperament contributes

Temperament occupies an intermediate position in this account. Its basic qualities recur across groups, while their colouring and mixture remain individual. The text names sanguine, choleric, phlegmatic and melancholic, then immediately qualifies the division: it does not define the individual exactly. One tendency may predominate, yet it is mixed with others in differing ways. Thus the same vocabulary can help compare recurring patterns while leaving room for a person’s distinctive expression. The first teaching task is to hold these two facts together, rather than treating a group name as a completed description.

Supplied PDF capture 4, columns 2–3.

Outer expression and inner being

The author asks readers to consider external appearance as a member or expression of a more complex human entity. Spiritual science is presented as the means of approaching this inner being. That is a stronger explanatory claim than the ordinary observation that a first impression can be incomplete. The colleague who is quiet in a meeting and expansive on a walk illustrates the latter relationship. Understanding the example does not establish the former claim. Read the source by identifying both what it wants to explain and the kind of knowledge it claims can explain it.

Supplied PDF capture 4, columns 1–2.

Make the description more exact

Use the two encounters to improve the original word quiet. You can describe how the person behaves in each setting, compare the differences, and ask what conditions matter to them. A recurring tendency remains useful when it directs attention toward a concrete question. It becomes less informative when it replaces the scenes with an unqualified identity. In the source’s own terms, the aim is to approach the individual enigma little by little and let knowledge enter feeling and conduct. Record both the new detail and the question it prompts in your encounter notebook.

How can the four groups coexist with individual difference?

Words for the reading
