General human nature and the particular person

By the end of this lesson: Distinguish general human nature, a shared portrait and a particular individual.

Start with a situation

A colleague speaks little in meetings but tells detailed stories during a walk. Your first description was simply “quiet.” The second encounter asks you to refine the first description. Neither scene alone supplies a complete explanation of the person.

Understand the concept

There are three levels to distinguish. General human nature concerns capacities shared by human beings. A temperament portrait groups a recurring manner of responding. An individual is the particular person whose history, relationships and choices give that response its meaning. Knowing a group description does not complete knowledge of the person.

Follow the relationship

Move between the levels carefully. “People can feel disappointment” is a general statement. “This portrait lingers over an impression” describes a pattern. “Rita is quiet because her friend moved away” concerns a particular event and needs particular knowledge. The last statement cannot be read directly from the first two.

Apply the distinction

Someone may speak little in a formal meeting and freely during a walk with a friend. Both observations belong to the same person. A temperament description becomes less useful when it erases the conditions of the encounter. Mixed tendencies and individual circumstances keep the classification subordinate to the person being understood.

A worked example, step by step

1. The situation

A colleague says little at a meeting but talks freely while walking with a friend.

2. What helps explain it

Both scenes belong to the same individual. Formality, trust and the subject of conversation may help explain the difference. A general quietness label would leave those relationships unexplained.

3. A possible response

Ask what made speaking easier during the walk. Let the answer change your understanding of the person and the conditions that support participation.

What are the three levels distinguished in this lesson?

See an explanation

General human capacities, a shared pattern described by a temperament portrait, and the particular person with their own circumstances and choices. The middle level cannot replace the last.

What is the intermediate role of a temperament portrait?

See an explanation

It offers a shared pattern between general human capacities and the unique individual. It can orient a question, but cannot replace the person’s circumstances or account of the event.

Reading the historical claims

The retained readings contain historical claims about bodily appearance, physiology, illness and ways of educating or handling people. These are attributed claims, not established diagnostic criteria or instructions to follow. Do not infer temperament from body shape, eye colour, ethnicity, sex, disability or birthday. Melancholic does not mean depression, and sanguine does not mean ADHD.

Constitutional charts are source- and age-specific: the 1909 pairings must not be applied universally to children. GA 295 explicitly gives different child pairings. Historical proposals involving fabricated distress, deliberate frustration, deprivation or coercion are not adopted as course practices. Use honest, respectful and revisable responses.

The source’s claim that spiritual science can reveal the inner human being is part of its explanatory position. Our ordinary comparison of the colleague in a meeting and on a walk illustrates the need to refine a description; it does not establish spiritual perception. Read the group names together with the qualification about individual mixtures.

The interval between a group and a person

Two forms of the human enigma

The opening first names the human being as the great enigma of physical life. It then turns from humanity in general to the distinct person we meet. These questions differ: one seeks the nature common to human beings; the other asks how to understand someone whose inner life is particular. The source says there is a large space between general human nature and what lives in each individual. Its social concern begins here. Our conduct toward another person depends on how we meet this particular enigma, including the feelings awakened in that meeting.

Supplied PDF capture 3.

What temperament contributes

Temperament occupies an intermediate position in this account. Its basic qualities recur across groups, while their colouring and mixture remain individual. The text names sanguine, choleric, phlegmatic and melancholic, then immediately qualifies the division: it does not define the individual exactly. One tendency may predominate, yet it is mixed with others in differing ways. Thus the same vocabulary can help compare recurring patterns while leaving room for a person’s distinctive expression. The first teaching task is to hold these two facts together, rather than treating a group name as a completed description.

Supplied PDF capture 4, columns 2–3.

Outer expression and inner being

The author asks readers to consider external appearance as a member or expression of a more complex human entity. Spiritual science is presented as the means of approaching this inner being. That is a stronger explanatory claim than the ordinary observation that a first impression can be incomplete. The colleague who is quiet in a meeting and expansive on a walk illustrates the latter relationship. Understanding the example does not establish the former claim. Read the source by identifying both what it wants to explain and the kind of knowledge it claims can explain it.

Supplied PDF capture 4, columns 1–2.

Make the description more exact

Use the two encounters to improve the original word quiet. You can describe how the person behaves in each setting, compare the differences, and ask what conditions matter to them. A recurring tendency remains useful when it directs attention toward a concrete question. It becomes less informative when it replaces the scenes with an unqualified identity. In the source’s own terms, the aim is to approach the individual enigma little by little and let knowledge enter feeling and conduct. Record both the new detail and the question it prompts in your encounter notebook.
