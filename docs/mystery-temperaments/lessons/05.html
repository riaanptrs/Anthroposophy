Four portraits: members, direction and response

By the end of this lesson: Compare four responses to one event and distinguish each portrait from its spiritual explanation.

Start with a situation

In a fictional workshop, one person presses toward a goal, another tries each new material, a third settles into a familiar rhythm, and a fourth dwells on a difficulty. These actions give us different directions to describe. A single scene cannot identify a permanent constitution or a cause of distress.

Understand the concept

To compare the four portraits, keep the event constant and change the focus of attention. Choleric asks what must be accomplished; sanguine notices what is new or inviting; phlegmatic preserves an established rhythm; melancholic considers the weight of what has happened. These questions are teaching summaries of the portraits, not words that every person necessarily says.

Follow the relationship

In the source’s constitutional explanation, each emphasis is related to a different predominant member. Purposeful self-assertion is linked with the I, changing sensation with the astral, inner well-being with the etheric and experienced resistance with the physical. The observable response and its proposed spiritual explanation are two parts of the account, not identical descriptions.

Apply the distinction

In a workshop, one participant asks for a goal, another explores a new possibility, another wants a dependable rhythm and another notices a difficulty. These contributions can help explain four emphases. They do not establish four permanent constitutions: the same person may make different contributions when the task changes.

The book’s detailed argument — optional

Connect with the book

Steiner relates the choleric to ego predominance and outward assertion; the sanguine to astral predominance and quickly changing impressions; the phlegmatic to etheric predominance and inward well-being; the melancholic to physical predominance and experienced resistance. These are connected explanations of how the whole person acts, not four isolated lists of traits.

A worked example, step by step

1. The situation

A bus is cancelled. One traveller seeks another route, one proposes an unexpected outing, one waits for the familiar service and one worries about a missed commitment.

2. What helps explain it

All may be responding sensibly to different priorities. Their first response becomes understandable when you name what each is attending to, not merely the facial expression you imagine.

3. A possible response

Ask each traveller what matters most and coordinate the next step. Compare the four contributions before assigning any predominant tendency to an actual person.

Original course example for understanding the idea; not a book quotation or a classification test.

Why is keeping the same event useful when comparing portraits?

See an explanation

It makes differences in attention and response easier to explain without changing every circumstance at once. It is a teaching comparison, not a controlled test of a person’s constitution.

Reading the historical claims

The retained readings contain historical claims about bodily appearance, physiology, illness and ways of educating or handling people. These are attributed claims, not established diagnostic criteria or instructions to follow. Do not infer temperament from body shape, eye colour, ethnicity, sex, disability or birthday. Melancholic does not mean depression, and sanguine does not mean ADHD.

Constitutional charts are source- and age-specific: the 1909 pairings must not be applied universally to children. GA 295 explicitly gives different child pairings. Historical proposals involving fabricated distress, deliberate frustration, deprivation or coercion are not adopted as course practices. Use honest, respectful and revisable responses.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

The reading’s key idea

Steiner relates the choleric to ego predominance and outward assertion; the sanguine to astral predominance and quickly changing impressions; the phlegmatic to etheric predominance and inward well-being; the melancholic to physical predominance and experienced resistance. These are connected explanations of how the whole person acts, not four isolated lists of traits.

The lecture also connects these members with blood, nerves, life processes and bodily resistance. Those physiological explanations are further source claims. In our fictional workshop, describe what happens before proposing its cause; the same action may have more than one explanation.

How the four portraits follow from the four members

Ego predominance: assertion meeting the outer world

The choleric portrait begins with a strong ego or I giving its particular stamp to the other members. Steiner describes feelings and actions arising with vehemence from that centre: the person wants to assert the self against outer circumstances and bring a will to bear on obstacles. He connects this spiritual predominance with vigorous blood activity and firmness of organisation. Notice the argument’s sequence: predominant member, proposed bodily expression, then a characteristic manner of acting. The workshop’s determined builder illustrates that final direction; the scene itself supplies no evidence about blood or spiritual constitution.

Uploaded PDF capture 14, column 1 and the opening of column 2.

Astral predominance: readily awakened, quickly changing interest

The sanguine portrait follows the astral body’s mobile life of sensations, images and feelings. Impressions easily awaken interest, yet another impression quickly replaces them. Steiner treats the ego as a source of order in this movement and blood as a regulator of nervous activity. His discussion of anaemia, illusions and hallucinations is a further physiological claim, not the definition of sanguine attention. The important explanatory relationship is between astral predominance and an interest that moves outward from impression to impression. Trying every workshop material can make that direction visible without establishing its proposed inner cause.

Uploaded PDF capture 14, columns 2–3, and capture 15, columns 1–2.

Etheric predominance: the inward feeling of well-being

For the phlegmatic portrait, Steiner turns from changing outer impressions to the etheric or vital body’s inward life. It regulates growth and other life processes and is associated with the feeling that things are going comfortably within the organism. When this member predominates, inward satisfaction is said to reduce the inclination to direct a strong will outward. The source alternates etheric and ethereal in this English wording; our explanation uses etheric/vital consistently. A familiar workshop rhythm can suggest inward contentment, but quiet repetition might also come from careful preparation, fatigue or the task’s requirements.

Uploaded PDF capture 15, columns 2–3.

Physical predominance: an instrument experienced as resistance

The melancholic account reverses the intended instrument relationship. Instead of serving the higher members, the physical body is said to dominate and resist them. Energy turns inward toward obstacles that cannot readily be overcome; pain, burden and persistent impressions then colour experience. Steiner therefore gives introspection an explanation within his fourfold account, rather than merely naming sadness. Read this alongside all three other portraits: assertion, mobility, contentment and resistance are distinct relations within one proposed human constitution. Our comparison asks you to explain those relations accurately and to keep another possible explanation of the workshop action available.

Connect the parts of the reading

What do all four portraits have in common as explanations?

An explained answer: Each links the relative predominance of one member with a bodily expression and a characteristic direction of experience. Explaining that relationship fairly does not require treating a workshop action as proof of the proposed constitution.
