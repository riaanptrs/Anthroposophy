Form drawing: completing, reflecting and transforming

Draw a vertical line and a curve to its left. Before drawing on the right, describe where three points of the reflected curve would lie.

The appendix proceeds from completing an unfinished figure to imagined water reflection and changing relations between inner and outer contours. Outward projections receive inward responses; closed arrangements develop into open, streaming forms. Steiner’s selected words stress inventive, flexible teaching and inward anticipation rather than a fixed sheet of copied patterns.

For strict reflection, each point lies equally far from the axis on the opposite side. A point two centimetres to the left and one centimetre above a chosen baseline becomes a point two centimetres to the right at the same height. Inner/outer response is a different task: an outward bulge may be answered by an inward contour without keeping equal distances across an axis. The first relation has a geometric rule to check; the second asks for a considered transformation. Distinguishing them prevents creative variation from being marked as a failed reflection, or an inaccurate reflection being excused as free invention.

Fold a sheet along a vertical axis and compare corresponding points of a simple reflected line. Equal distance gives a checkable rule. Then deliberately answer a bulge with an inward curve without claiming strict reflection. One task trains precision; the other trains considered variation. Neither needs a claim about bodily forces during sleep in order to be a worthwhile exercise.

Two drawings, two different rules

Reflection keeps corresponding points at equal perpendicular distances from an axis. An inner/outer response asks how one contour should change when the other changes. These tasks can both involve imagining a missing line, but their rules are different.

Reconsider the relationship between the two contours. Equal distances across an axis check the first task. Reading the appendix closely

Begin with an unfinished relation

The first figure has a vertical reference line and a dark contour with a dotted counterpart. The accompanying selection asks the teacher to begin a symmetry line and help children recognize incompleteness, imagine what is needed and finish the drawing. The essential action is anticipation from something already perceived. Steiner connects that inward urge with developing a correct picture of reality and repeatedly calls for inventive teaching. The book’s notes attribute this selection to A Modern Art of Education, Lecture IX. Our selected short sentence belongs to that passage as printed in Koepke’s translated volume, not to an independently examined original edition.

Capture 70, left and middle columns; note 1 at capture 76.

Separate reflection from later balancing

Next comes an object above a water surface and its reflected counterpart. The prose asks the child to imagine how the object is mirrored. The course’s existing original diagram makes one precise reflection rule visible: corresponding points lie equally far from the same axis on opposite sides. That rule is useful for checking the first activity, but it is not a complete description of every later source figure. The next nested three-lobed arrangement asks for a changing relation between inner and outer contours. Here attention shifts from completing a mirror partner to responding appropriately to a surrounding form.

Capture 70, middle and right columns.

Follow the transformation across both pages

The continuation explains that an outward projection of the outer contour should receive an inward movement of the inner line. A later arrangement starts from three inner forms and adds corresponding outer forms to create a harmonious whole. It then changes toward outer contours that no longer meet but stream away, with an inner response that turns inward. The playful image of a point that runs away helps children imagine that transformation. These are complementary relations, not a single mechanical mirror rule. The source calls the aim “asymmetrical symmetries.” The readable figures now allow the course to explain the progression rather than describe it as missing.

Capture 71, left and middle columns; native figures.

Credit the explanation and its additional claim

The final paragraph says these waking activities prepare the ether or formative-force body to continue vibrating during sleep and perfect the day’s work; the child then wakes with inwardly moving ether and physical bodies. Preserve this as Steiner’s spiritual explanation quoted by Koepke. It is not demonstrated by completing a drawing. The notes credit reflection and later exercises to Kingdom of Childhood, Lecture 4; the last native superscript is 3, even though OCR misreads it as 2.
