Foodwise · Lesson 35 · Foods that transform a meal

What travels with coffee, tea and cocoa?

A familiar drink carries processing, trade and social ritual.

Cook’s account · attributed paraphrase

Cook approaches coffee through stories of discovery and the development of a drink culture. The familiar roasted bean is a seed from a fruit; roasting is part of its transformation. Tea begins with leaves, and cocoa with seeds, but the final drink or confection may contain many additional ingredients. This makes a useful distinction between a plant source and a finished product. Cook also gives these foods spiritual and cultural readings. A coffee legend is not a verified discovery date, and an interpretation of concentration is not a promise of benefit for every drinker. The lesson asks what preparation and social setting add to the plant material. For Cook, that setting also extends along the supply chain: growing, processing and selling involve people whose working conditions and share of the price deserve attention.

Optional reading: From cocoa to the people who grow it

Cook’s question reaches beyond a drink’s ingredients: who grows the crop, who transforms it and who benefits from its sale? Her plantation wages and spending figures describe the book’s period, not today’s prices. The connection to follow is between the plant product and the people along its route.

Change the case: Two chocolate bars show the same cocoa percentage. A buyer concludes that growers must receive the same pay for both.

What does the cocoa percentage allow the buyer to conclude?

It tells the grower’s pay because the ingredient percentage is the same.

No. Composition and payment are different questions. A percentage describes the product, while contracts, prices and working conditions concern the relationships through which it reaches the buyer.

It gives composition information; grower conditions need separate evidence.

Yes. This separates what a label actually reports from the ethical question Cook asks. Look for traceable information and keep unanswered questions visible rather than invent a fair-payment conclusion.

It makes the growers irrelevant because both bars taste similar.

No. Similar products can travel through different social and economic relationships. Cook’s point is to include those relationships in attention, not erase them once an ingredient is identified.
