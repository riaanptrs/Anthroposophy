Foodwise · Lesson 34 · Foods that transform a meal

Which plant part flavours the meal?

Leaves, seeds, bark, flower buds and rhizomes can all be seasonings.

Cook’s account · attributed paraphrase

Cook’s herb and spice descriptions connect flavour with plant form and cultural use. Coriander leaves differ from its seeds; cinnamon uses bark; cloves are flower buds; ginger is a rhizome, an underground stem. Calling them all spices hides these distinctions. Freshness, drying and heat can change the flavour presented to the diner, so timing is part of seasoning. Cook also observes the difference between raw and cooked garlic. Her arrangement of tastes is her framework rather than a replacement for contemporary sensory science. The practical lesson is to attend to a particular ingredient and compare a small amount before assuming that more seasoning will improve a dish.

See the idea in particular foods

Rosemary

Plant part: Leaves

Cook’s account: Cook discusses rosemary among herbs with strong culinary character.

Kitchen application: Use modest amounts; remove tough woody stems before serving.

Coriander

Plant part: Leaves and seeds

Cook’s account: Cook distinguishes the culinary character of leaves and seeds.

Kitchen application: Compare fresh leaf aroma with gently crushed seed; one does not replace the other identically.

Cinnamon

Plant part: Inner bark

Cook’s account: Cook situates this aromatic bark among spices.

Kitchen application: Ground cinnamon disperses differently from a stick removed after infusion.

Clove

Plant part: Dried flower bud

Cook’s account: Cook includes cloves in her tour of seasoning plant parts.

Kitchen application: Whole cloves are potent; remove them from a dish before serving.

Ginger

Plant part: Rhizome: underground stem

Cook’s account: Cook compares fresh and dried ginger’s culinary character.

Kitchen application: Fresh grated ginger and dried powder differ; begin with small amounts and taste.
