What changes when the bodily means of experience are no longer available?

After describing the human being, Theosophy asks how soul and spirit relate to the end of bodily life. The selected food example introduces a difference between losing an organ and relinquishing a desire.

After death the soul has not immediately lost such a desire, but it no longer possesses the bodily organ that provides the means for satisfying it.

Course explanation

Understand the idea

In Steiner’s supersensible account, bodily death releases soul and spirit from physical embodiment. Spirit initially remains connected with a soul still oriented toward earthly experience. Death therefore does not immediately end every relationship formed during bodily life.

The food example identifies two contributions. Pleasure and longing belong to soul life; the palate and other bodily organs provide means of satisfaction. Steiner says that the desire may remain after those means are gone. Losing the bodily organ and releasing the longing are not the same event in his account.

Purification names the proposed release of attachments tied to the embodiment just completed. The soul gradually relinquishes bodily dependence while fruits capable of spiritual assimilation remain. The account is not a uniform timetable, and the regions described later are not separate geographical places.

Keep purification distinct from karma and spiritual assimilation. Karma concerns relationships and consequences across incarnations, meaning different bodily lives; assimilation concerns earthly experience becoming spiritual capacities. Relinquishing an appetite does not cancel every consequence of a deed or erase individuality’s entire past.

Begin with a familiar taste

To understand Steiner’s account of death, begin with enjoying a favourite food. Its taste involves the tongue and other bodily organs. The pleasure you experience and the wish to taste it again belong to your inward life. Wanting that familiar taste and actually tasting it are different things. The example distinguishes a desire from the bodily means through which it is satisfied.

Desire and its bodily means after death

Steiner claims that after death, soul and spirit continue while the organs of the physical body are no longer available for earthly enjoyment. In his food example, a desire for bodily enjoyment can initially remain without the means of satisfying it. Losing those means and releasing the desire are therefore different events in his account. This continuation is his supersensible claim; the everyday food example helps explain the distinction but does not establish what happens after death.

What purification means here

Steiner calls the gradual release from these bodily attachments purification. Here, the term concerns changing the soul’s relationship to desires that depended on bodily life. In the food example, the soul ceases to depend on enjoying that taste through bodily organs. Relinquishing an attachment does not, in this account, mean losing everything learned through life. Releasing a dependence and retaining something gained through experience are different questions.

Key concept

Steiner describes death as an end of physical embodiment while some soul desires initially persist without the bodily means of satisfying them.

Keep this distinction

The after-death account is Steiner’s claimed spiritual knowledge. An ordinary analogy neither verifies it nor permits assigning a particular fate to someone who has died.

What persists initially in the selected food example?

Reveal the explanation

The passage explicitly separates the continuing desire from the lost bodily organ. Purification concerns release from such dependence.

Purification means erasing everything learned through life.

Reveal the explanation

No. Purification concerns releasing attachments that depended on bodily enjoyment. Relinquishing such a dependence is different from erasing everything gained through experience. The fuller discussion of what becomes of that experience belongs to later study.

What distinction is Steiner making between a desire and its bodily means of satisfaction?

Pause and answer in one sentence, aloud or in your notes.

See a way to approach it

A wish to taste a favourite food is an inward desire; the tongue and other organs provide bodily means of tasting it. Steiner claims that after death a desire can initially remain when those means are no longer available. The example explains his distinction, rather than proving the continuation.
