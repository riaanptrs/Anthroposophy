What would make an action free rather than simply chosen?

Choosing between options tells us what happened, but not why an action took place. Steiner’s freedom argument asks whether an individually understood ethical idea can determine the deed. The selected maxim also includes understanding another person’s will.

To live in love towards our actions and to let live in the understanding of the other person’s will is the fundamental maxim of free human beings.

Course explanation

Understand the idea

A motive is the idea or reason directing an action; a driving force concerns what moves the person toward it. Steiner does not identify every desire with moral intuition. Moral intuition supplies an ethical content apprehended by the individual, rather than mere conformity to an external command.

Moral imagination develops a concrete possible action from that content. Moral technique concerns the knowledge and means needed to realize it under actual conditions. A good intention, a vivid plan and practical feasibility answer different questions; none should stand in for all three.

The source maxim connects love toward one’s own actions with understanding another person’s will. Free individuality therefore includes a social relationship. It does not mean demanding identical actions, ignoring another person’s reasons or treating every impulse as justified because it feels personal.

The same arrangement, different motives

Two organisers arrange an accessible meeting. One acts only to win praise; the other has thought through why everyone affected by the decision should be able to participate and acts from that ethical understanding. The visible arrangement may be the same, while the reason determining each deed differs. Steiner’s question about freedom asks what moves us to act. Having several options and choosing one does not, by itself, explain whether an action is free.

What moral intuition means here

Steiner calls the individual grasp of an ethical idea moral intuition. In this example, the organiser understands the reason for enabling participation and makes that idea the motive for acting. Simply repeating a rule or following a wish does not establish this understanding. Lesson 25 used spiritual Intuition for conscious participation in a spiritual being’s inner nature. Moral intuition here concerns an ethical idea understood through thinking; the shared word does not make the two activities identical.

From an ethical idea to a workable meeting

Moral imagination gives the ethical idea a concrete possible form: move the meeting to an accessible venue or devise another suitable way to take part. Moral technique concerns the knowledge and means needed to make that arrangement work: check entrances, transport, timing and resources. Ask the participant what they need, rather than assuming that your proposed solution suits them. The ethical aim, the possible arrangement and its practical realization answer different questions.

Key concept

Freedom concerns what determines the deed: an individually apprehended ethical idea, rather than choosing alone or following an unexamined impulse.

Everyday example

An organiser understands why everyone affected by a meeting’s decision should be able to participate, imagines an accessible arrangement, then checks entrances, transport, timing and resources with the participant. These distinguish the ethical aim, the possible action and its practical realization. The same visible arrangement could also be chosen only for praise, so its success alone does not explain the motive.

Keep this distinction

A new or successful action is not automatically free. Moral imagination is the creation of a concrete ethical form, distinct from technical Imagination as spiritual perception. Understanding another person’s will permits evaluation and disagreement.

See a way to approach it

Ask what reason actually determines each action and whether the organiser has individually understood the ethical idea they act from. Choosing the same accessible venue does not answer that question: one might seek praise, while another acts from an understood ethical reason. A successful arrangement alone does not establish freedom in Steiner’s argument.
