Around twelve: explaining causes

Distinguish an observed pattern from a causal explanation.

Understand the idea

The curriculum plan places greater emphasis on cause, law and exact relationships around Grade 6. Physics, geometric construction and business mathematics offer situations where a result must be explained, not simply described.

A causal question asks how changing one condition changes another and why. Compare relevant conditions, distinguish observations from the proposed mechanism and consider what else could affect the result.

The approximate age is an organizing interpretation, not an experimentally established threshold for every child. Younger children also reason about causes. The teaching task is to make explanations more precise and testable at an appropriate level.

A worked example

Use invented records of a toy cart: on the same surface, released from rest at a low ramp mark it travels 40 cm; from a higher mark it travels 70 cm. The changed release height is a plausible cause because it changes the starting condition; the longer travel is the result. Repeated trials and keeping the cart, surface and release method comparable would strengthen the comparison. One pair of distances does not isolate every influence or supply a complete mechanism.

Check and apply your understanding

If the second run also uses a smoother floor, can the extra distance be attributed only to height?

Pause and answer in one sentence, aloud or in your notes.

See a way to approach it

No. Height and surface changed together. Compare one relevant condition at a time where possible and explain the proposed mechanism. A causal explanation needs more than noticing that two numbers differ; reasoning about this is not restricted to one birthday.
