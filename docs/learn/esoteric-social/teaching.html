Teach the four lectures

Four 90-minute seminars, with guided lessons between meetings.

Before the course

Ask learners what makes an institution humane. Introduce the historical setting: these were talks to anthroposophical members shortly after the First World War. The book’s introduction is by Stephen E. Usher, written in 2001; it is a separate voice. Explain that students will learn an argument and evaluate it, rather than demonstrate religious agreement.

For each week, assign the four corresponding lessons beforehand. Source reading is optional and uses each learner’s copy of the book. The supplied OCR has unresolved review flags; use the PDF for any exact quotation.

Session 1: The human being and the social question

Before class: lessons 1–4; optional PDF captures 14–22.

  • 0–10 min: recall two ideas and one question from the lessons.
  • 10–25 min: explain the chapter’s key distinctions using the concept guide.
  • 25–45 min: work through the example from lesson 2; identify the assumptions before proposing an answer.
  • 45–65 min: Create a dependency map and compare a role description with a fuller human account.
  • 65–80 min: use the chapter reflection from the workbook and discuss one objection.
  • 80–90 min: exit ticket—explain one concept, apply it to a new case and name a remaining uncertainty.

Look for: Explain why Steiner connects social renewal with the way we perceive other human beings.

Teaching difficult passages

Do not skip Steiner’s theological contrasts or his claims about atheism. Lesson 10 names their assumptions and limitations. Distinguish his account of Judaism from Jewish self-understanding and his spiritual judgments from medical knowledge. Invite students to reconstruct and challenge the argument. Do not ask them to diagnose anyone’s karma, spiritual rank or religious health.

Feedback and assessment

Use the six criteria in the final project. Ask learners to explain how their proposal changed after hearing another perspective. Reward accurate reconstruction, good reasons and thoughtful revision, including well-supported disagreement. A provisional proposal can show strong understanding when it identifies its limits and explains what would change its conclusion.
