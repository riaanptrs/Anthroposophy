The organizational phase: strength and limitation

Explain the opening discussion of consolidation without supplying the missing continuation.

Reading locator: supplied PDF captures 52–53. See the source map

From exploration to organization

At the end of the twenties, Lievegoed describes a movement toward taking stock, making plans and consolidating responsibility. Surveying a field, researching conditions and making reasoned decisions become prominent. He associates this with Steiner's intellectual soul and Moers's objective strivings. The claim is about a proposed change of emphasis, rather than a sudden arrival of intellect at 28.

Organization can support freedom

A reliable system can reduce avoidable confusion and make shared work possible. Explicit responsibilities, realistic schedules and accessible information allow people to act without asking one person about every detail. The value lies in what the arrangement enables. A carefully drawn plan that ignores actual capacities or excludes participants can increase frustration while appearing efficient.

Competence can overextend itself

The text portrays confidence that logical organization can solve every problem, including personal ones. Here lies a useful limit to examine. A scheduling conflict may need a timetable; loss of trust may also require conversation, accountability and time. Structural competence becomes one-sided when its successful method is assumed to answer every kind of human question.

Work through an example

A team leader reduces missed handovers with a checklist. Later, a colleague reports feeling excluded from decisions. Adding another checklist may document the complaint while leaving exclusion intact. The leader needs to ask who has a voice and how decisions are made. An organizational adjustment may follow, but it should respond to the relational problem rather than replace the conversation needed to understand it.

Check your understanding

When does an effective method become a limitation?

Read an explained answer

When its success in one kind of problem is taken to prove it can solve every kind. A method should be judged against the actual problem, including its relational and value dimensions.

Apply the idea

Compare a logistical problem and a relational problem in a fictional team. Identify where a plan helps, what conversation is still needed and what evidence would show improvement in each case.
