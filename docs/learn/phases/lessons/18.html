Lesson 18 / 18 · Contribution and synthesis · 30–45 minutes

Synthesis: read a life, test an interpretation, choose a next step

Bring the course's distinctions together in a bounded, evidence-based biographical study.

Reading locator: supplied PDF captures 9–53. See the source map

Start with a question and a boundary

Choose one transition in a fictional life or an ordinary experience you are comfortable considering. State the question before building an explanation. Keep a short chronology and distinguish known facts from remembered impressions. A focused question, such as why work stopped feeling meaningful, is easier to examine than a claim to explain a person's whole destiny.

Use the perspectives together

Describe bodily conditions, lived experience and values. Then distinguish quantitative change from structural development. Ask which priorities became central and what practical conditions shaped the options. Consult the age map only after describing the case, so it can suggest a comparison rather than predetermine the answer. Include one feature that does not fit the expected pattern.

Test your story

State a possible recurring motive and identify at least two supporting events. Add an alternative explanation and a limitation. Consider whether the interpretation reflects the person's own understanding or an observer's wish for a tidy story. A strong biographical study can end with uncertainty. Its quality depends on distinctions, evidence and openness to revision, not on agreement with every spiritual proposition.

Translate insight into a modest action

Choose a reversible next step: a conversation, a learning trial, a household adjustment or a change in responsibility. Identify the resources and consent it requires, the evidence you will notice and a time to review it. This is an original learning project based on the supplied opening. It does not reproduce the absent Chapter Seven or offer a substitute for psychotherapy.

Work through an example

Marina, 34, feels stuck after eight years in one role. She is sleeping poorly while caring for a relative. She enjoys training new staff but dislikes being measured only by speed. A possible motive is helping people learn. Another explanation is that training temporarily relieves repetitive work. A sensible trial is a supported training assignment with adjusted workload, alongside attention to rest and care support. Its outcome can refine the interpretation instead of being made to confirm it at any cost.

Check your understanding

What would count as a good synthesis even if it disagrees with the book's timetable?

Read an explained answer

An accurate account of the book's concepts, clear facts and interpretations, a plausible alternative, attention to material conditions and a reasoned application. A well-supported disagreement demonstrates understanding.

Apply the idea

Write 600–900 words or discuss for 20 minutes: question; chronology; three perspectives; change versus development; proposed value-centre; supporting and contrary evidence; alternative explanation; next step and review. Use a fictional case if preferred. Compare with the rubric in the workbook.

Change the case

Marina's training trial is exhausting and she dislikes it. Should she continue because the leitmotiv predicted it?

Compare your reasoning

No. The result challenges the interpretation or the trial's conditions. Examine both, revise the plan and allow a different explanation. A useful framework must remain answerable to the actual life.
