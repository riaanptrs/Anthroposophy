Workbook and final project

Use these prompts in your own notebook. A fictional life is fully suitable for every task.

1. Reading a biography

After these lessons, explain the main distinctions without looking, then apply them to a different case.

  • A life is more than a sequence of events: Make three columns: event, meaning at the time, meaning now. Use an ordinary event or a fictional person. Add one possible next step and one constraint. Keep uncertain recollections marked as uncertain.
  • Disciplined reflection and listening: Spend ten minutes on one ordinary interaction. Record event, feeling, interpretation, alternative and a question you could ask. Keep it private or use a fictional situation. Revisit the record after a day.
  • Change, growth and development: Choose a familiar skill or group. Describe one change, one quantitative increase and one actual reorganization. Explain what became possible after the reorganization and what problem remained.
  • Body, psyche and spirit in one situation: Draw three overlapping circles labeled bodily conditions, lived experience and values. Put one fictional decision in the overlap. Write one question and one possible practical response for each perspective.

2. Meaning, models and phases

After these lessons, explain the main distinctions without looking, then apply them to a different case.

  • Satisfaction, fulfilment and a guiding motive: Compare one recurring need, one passing wish and one commitment in a fictional week. Describe how each is recognized, what meeting it involves and how you would notice a conflict between them.
  • Creativity, wisdom and an active middle: Review a small fictional project. Write the strongest reason to act now, the strongest reason to wait, the information still missing and a decision date. Explain how listening would change the proposed action.
  • Four images of the human being: Make a table with four model names, a useful question for each, and something each might overlook if used alone. Apply all four to one ordinary fictional problem.
  • Phases, transitions and the limits of age maps: Draw a timeline of a fictional life with overlapping bands rather than rigid boxes. Mark two transitions, the evidence for them and a condition that may explain their timing. Add an event that does not fit the proposed pattern.

3. Development and adolescence

After these lessons, explain the main distinctions without looking, then apply them to a different case.

  • Three life courses and two kinds of achievement: Compare two kinds of achievement in a fictional workplace. For each, identify an observable sign, a practical support and something it cannot tell you about the person's overall worth.
  • Childhood: trust, imagination and the search for truth: Design a short activity that includes reliability, imagination and a truthful question. Describe what the adult would say and how the plan would change if the child were uninterested or distressed.
  • Adolescence: identity, ideals and responsibility: Use a fictional young person's project. List the ideal, an unknown practical condition, an adult's useful support and a decision the young person can genuinely own. Add a way to review the result.
  • Freedom, creative play and social conditions: Plan a 30-minute group activity with one fixed constraint, two genuine choices, clear responsibilities and a review. Include a low-cost or no-cost way for everyone to take part.

4. Early adult life and organization

After these lessons, explain the main distinctions without looking, then apply them to a different case.

  • Early adulthood: creating a way of living: Design a fictional shared-space agreement covering one repeated activity. Identify the value behind it, who should be consulted and how someone can ask for a change. Include people with different schedules or capacities.
  • Work, feedback and learning through experience: Create a learning plan for a fictional repetitive job. Identify one skill, one small variation permitted by the role, a person who can give useful feedback and evidence of improvement after a month.
  • Attraction, recognition and historical gender roles: Use a fictional relationship or friendship. Describe an attraction or shared interest, a difference in aims and one conversation that respects both people. Keep practical constraints and the possibility of disagreement visible.
  • The organizational phase: strength and limitation: Compare a logistical problem and a relational problem in a fictional team. Identify where a plan helps, what conversation is still needed and what evidence would show improvement in each case.

5. Contribution and synthesis

After these lessons, explain the main distinctions without looking, then apply them to a different case.

  • Midlife as a possibility of renewed contribution: Draft a fictional transition plan with something to continue, something to reduce and one new contribution to test. Name the support required and a sign that the plan needs revision. Avoid tying it to a birthday.
  • Synthesis: read a life, test an interpretation, choose a next step: Write 600–900 words or discuss for 20 minutes: question; chronology; three perspectives; change versus development; proposed value-centre; supporting and contrary evidence; alternative explanation; next step and review. Use a fictional case if preferred. Compare with the rubric in the workbook.

Final project: a bounded biographical study

Write 600–900 words or discuss for 20 minutes: question; chronology; three perspectives; change versus development; proposed value-centre; supporting and contrary evidence; alternative explanation; next step and review. Use a fictional case if preferred. Compare with the rubric in the workbook.

  1. State the question and keep a short factual chronology.
  2. Describe bodily conditions, lived experience and values.
  3. Identify growth or structural development with an example.
  4. Propose a guiding motive and compare it with the actual priorities.
  5. Name supporting evidence, a counterexample and another explanation.
  6. Choose a feasible next step, required support and review point.
A model outline: Marina

Marina, 34, feels stuck after eight years in one role. She is sleeping poorly while caring for a relative. She enjoys training new staff but dislikes being measured only by speed. A possible motive is helping people learn. Another explanation is that training temporarily relieves repetitive work. A sensible trial is a supported training assignment with adjusted workload, alongside attention to rest and care support. Its outcome can refine the interpretation instead of being made to confirm it at any cost.

No. The result challenges the interpretation or the trial's conditions. Examine both, revise the plan and allow a different explanation. A useful framework must remain answerable to the actual life.

Review rubric

For each criterion use 0 = absent, 1 = partial, 2 = clear and supported. The total (0–12) is an optional self-review aid, not a clinical or spiritual score. Rework partial areas. Reasoned disagreement with the book can meet every criterion.

  • Accurate concepts and attribution
  • Clear facts versus interpretations
  • Three interacting perspectives
  • Attention to context and variation
  • Alternative explanation and contrary evidence
  • Feasible next step and review
