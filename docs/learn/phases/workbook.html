Workbook and final project

Use your own notebook. Every question can be answered through a fictional life; personal disclosure and agreement with the book are not required.

1. Surveying the Terrain

Check your understanding

How can a phase map illuminate a biography without replacing it?

Read an explained answer

Distinguish change, growth and development; consider bodily conditions, experience and values together. Use age patterns to ask questions, then test them against a particular account and contrary evidence.

Practice prompts for this chapter
  • Change, growth and development

    Choose a familiar skill or group. Describe one change, one quantitative increase and one actual reorganization. Explain what became possible after the reorganization and what problem remained.

  • Body, psyche and spirit in one situation

    Draw three overlapping circles labeled bodily conditions, lived experience and values. Put one fictional decision in the overlap. Write one question and one possible practical response for each perspective.

  • Satisfaction, fulfilment and a guiding motive

    Compare one recurring need, one passing wish and one commitment in a fictional week. Describe how each is recognized, what meeting it involves and how you would notice a conflict between them.

  • Creativity, wisdom and an active middle

    Review a small fictional project. Write the strongest reason to act now, the strongest reason to wait, the information still missing and a decision date. Explain how listening would change the proposed action.

  • Four images of the human being

    Make a table with four model names, a useful question for each, and something each might overlook if used alone. Apply all four to one ordinary fictional problem.

  • Phases, transitions and the limits of age maps

    Draw a timeline of a fictional life with overlapping bands rather than rigid boxes. Mark two transitions, the evidence for them and a condition that may explain their timing. Add an event that does not fit the proposed pattern.

2. The Course of Life

Check your understanding

What changes across the life phases, and what remains a question for each person?

Read an explained answer

The book proposes shifting emphases in receiving, expansive activity, organization, values, contribution and detachment. Exact timing, circumstances, aims and the meaning of transitions must be examined individually; no phase guarantees wisdom or failure.

Practice prompts for this chapter
  • Three life courses and two kinds of achievement

    Compare two kinds of achievement in a fictional workplace. For each, identify an observable sign, a practical support and something it cannot tell you about the person's overall worth.

  • Childhood: trust, imagination and the search for truth

    Design a short activity that includes reliability, imagination and a truthful question. Describe what the adult would say and how the plan would change if the child were uninterested or distressed.

  • Adolescence: identity, ideals and responsibility

    Use a fictional young person's project. List the ideal, an unknown practical condition, an adult's useful support and a decision the young person can genuinely own. Add a way to review the result.

  • Freedom, creative play and social conditions

    Plan a 30-minute group activity with one fixed constraint, two genuine choices, clear responsibilities and a review. Include a low-cost or no-cost way for everyone to take part.

  • Early adulthood: creating a way of living

    Design a fictional shared-space agreement covering one repeated activity. Identify the value behind it, who should be consulted and how someone can ask for a change. Include people with different schedules or capacities.

  • Work, feedback and learning through experience

    Create a learning plan for a fictional repetitive job. Identify one skill, one small variation permitted by the role, a person who can give useful feedback and evidence of improvement after a month.

  • Attraction, recognition and historical gender roles

    Use a fictional relationship or friendship. Describe an attraction or shared interest, a difference in aims and one conversation that respects both people. Keep practical constraints and the possibility of disagreement visible.

  • The organizational phase: strength and limitation

    Compare a logistical problem and a relational problem in a fictional team. Identify where a plan helps, what conversation is still needed and what evidence would show improvement in each case.

  • When success no longer answers the question

    Compare an achieved goal, its expected reward and what was actually experienced. Propose two interpretations and a reversible learning trial. Use fiction if preferred.

  • The forties: values that become a way of living

    Choose a fictional commitment and show three daily decisions that would embody it. Identify a conflict with another legitimate responsibility and explain a proportionate response.

  • Bodily transitions and the limits of historical physiology

    Create a three-column case: reported symptom, possible contextual influence and question still requiring assessment. Keep conjectures distinct from established facts.

  • The fifties: authority, mentorship and a second creative peak

    Design a mentoring conversation with one observed achievement, one question, one new responsibility and a support plan. Include how the learner can disagree.

  • After 56: detachment, mortality and what can endure

    Make a fictional three-part review: complete, hand over, release. For each item identify its value, a practical action and the support required.

  • A culture for later life

    Design a cultural activity with an accessible setting, varied roles, rest options and a way to decline. State whose views should shape it before it begins.

3. Male and Female Development—Marriage

Check your understanding

How does recognition differ from projecting an ideal or a double onto a partner?

Read an explained answer

Recognition lets actual encounters correct an image. It includes listening, freedom, responsibility and boundaries. Examine one’s own contribution without denying another person’s real conduct; family culture adds rhythms, stories and shared participation.

Practice prompts for this chapter
  • Equality, difference and the book's historical assumptions

    Identify one equality statement, one empirical claim and one symbolic interpretation in the chapter. Explain why they need different kinds of evaluation.

  • Anima, animus and the person behind an ideal image

    Compare a fictional person's ideal image with three observed qualities of their friend. Identify what supports the image and what requires revision.

  • Partnership across a changing biography

    Describe a partnership at two points in time. Identify a continuing value, a changed need and a conversation that could produce a new agreement.

  • The double, blame and seeing one's own contribution

    Use a mild fictional disagreement. Separate events, interpretations, each person's action and a boundary. State what evidence would challenge a projection hypothesis.

  • Family culture: rhythm, stories and shared preparation

    Plan a low-cost rhythm, a story activity and a shared preparation. Identify how each can adapt to fatigue, different ages or a changed schedule.

4. Basic Life Orientations

Check your understanding

Why do aptitude, temperament, orientation and leitmotiv not select one inevitable profession?

Read an explained answer

They answer different questions about capacity, manner, characteristic engagement and aim. Six orientations can appear in the same occupation and combine in one person; creativity is a modality across them. Guidance supplies information and reflection without taking ownership of choice.

Practice prompts for this chapter
  • Basic orientations: the key of a life, not its job title

    Describe one task through all six orientations, then distinguish which capacities and motives would be needed. Avoid assigning people to fixed boxes.

  • Six orientations working together

    Review a fictional project and identify which orientation is missing. Propose a concrete contribution rather than appointing a permanent personality type.

  • Choosing a direction when more than one path fits

    Compare two feasible paths: activities, learning demands, resources, uncertainties and a low-risk trial. State what you would need to learn before deciding.

5. Career Prospects and Personnel Policy

Check your understanding

What must an organization provide if development is more than an individual demand?

Read an explained answer

Meaningful practice, transparent requirements and feedback, resources, real choices, fair participation and realistic openings. Include succession and retirement, while recognizing structural limits rather than blaming every outcome on personal will.

Practice prompts for this chapter
  • Education should open a future

    Map a fictional learner's route from school to work, including one bridge to further learning and one avoidable barrier. Explain who could change that barrier.

  • Learning careers: apprentice, journeyman and specialist

    Design two routes from the same entry role: specialist and coordinating. Identify shared learning, different requirements and a point for an informed choice.

  • Renewing work without prescribing a midlife career change

    Try the three practices in a fictional working week. For each, state the observation, the intended change and a limit that must remain visible.

  • Personnel policy as the conditions for development

    Audit a fictional policy under four headings: information, resources, participation and outcomes. Propose one change and how employees could evaluate it.

  • Succession and retirement: making room without discarding people

    Make a fictional handover plan with tasks, support, decision rights, a completion date and a boundary after departure. Include a new activity only if the person wants it.

6. Images of Man, Biography and Psychotherapy

Check your understanding

How does a model of the person affect the help proposed?

Read an explained answer

Models select explanations and methods. Compare heredity, learned responses, past conflicts, aims, meaning and spiritual individuality without collapsing them. Distinguish peer support and professional care, respect privacy and preserve continuity. Understanding a theory does not validate all its clinical claims.

Practice prompts for this chapter
  • Models, methods and what counts as an explanation

    Explain one event using two models. State the useful evidence and the unanswered question in each.

  • Nativism: heredity as condition or destiny?

    Compare a selection-only pathway with one including teaching and review. Identify when assessment helps and when it closes inquiry too early.

  • Empiricism: learning, conditioning and the purpose of education

    Design one practice task and one changed-case task. Specify what success in each would and would not demonstrate.

  • Personalism: agency, understanding and the third force

    Describe one event as an observation, an experience and an intended next action. Explain how each could be checked.

  • Rogers: helping without taking over a life

    Write a short fictional exchange showing a question, a paraphrase, relevant information and a choice left with the learner.

  • Maslow: healthy development and everyday creativity

    Describe one ordinary activity performed mechanically and responsively. Identify the observable difference without judging the whole personality.

  • Freud: conflict, repression and an explanation from the past

    Describe a mild fictional conflict through desire, internal rule and external reality. Add a non-psychoanalytic explanation of the same conduct.

  • Adler: aims, compensation and encouragement

    Give one fictional action a possible past cause and a possible aim. Propose evidence that would distinguish or connect them.

  • Jung: persona, shadow and individuation

    Compare one fictional role with the person beyond it: useful expectations, hidden capacities and a situation requiring a different response.

  • Frankl: meaning, possibility and therapeutic reserve

    Distinguish pleasure, usefulness and meaning in a fictional activity. Show where they overlap and where they differ.

  • Assagioli: psychosynthesis and the regulating will

    Draw the model with clearly labeled conceptual regions. Add an ordinary example of coordinating wishes and state what it cannot prove about the model.

  • Steiner's model within Lievegoed's synthesis

    Separate an ethical principle, a physiological claim and a metaphysical claim in the lesson. State what kind of support each would need.

  • Biographical work: context, individuality and realistic next aims

    Formulate a value, a distant aim and two feasible next steps for a fictional case. Include resources and one reason to revise.

  • Three levels of help and the limits of a classification

    Compare the purpose, competence and limits of a fictional peer group and a professional service. Identify how a handover could preserve continuity.

  • Groups: encounter, privacy and the right to remain silent

    Draft a group activity with purpose, options, privacy boundaries and a response if someone needs support beyond the group's scope.

  • Encounter, daily rhythm and acquiring new contents

    Design a low-pressure learning trial with one task, a rhythm, another person's agreed support and a review. Explain its educational limits.

  • Psychiatry, communication and the dignity of the person

    Analyze a fictional service handover: what information, consent, communication support and continuity are required? Keep the activity educational rather than diagnostic.

7. Personal Development and Biography

Check your understanding

How do outward inquiry, inward reflection and cultural action belong together in the conclusion?

Read an explained answer

The author proposes disciplined world observation as preparation for inner discernment, with imagination, inspiration and intuition related to truth, beauty and goodness. Explain his spiritual realism and Christian horizon as claims, then distinguish ordinary educational applications and test their actual consequences.

Practice prompts for this chapter
  • Personal development begins with preparation

    Compare two fictional learning paths: aims, preparation, evidence of progress, freedom to disagree and practical demands.

  • Four worldviews and the proposed new realism

    Make a four-row comparison of what each worldview treats as primary and the question Lievegoed believes it leaves open.

  • The Mysteries: initiation as the author's historical model

    Compare justified teaching prerequisites with an unjustified appeal to secret authority. Give one concrete feature distinguishing them.

  • Goethean observation: from separate details to a living pattern

    Observe a safe familiar plant or a sequence of photographs. Describe three stages, propose a relation and name a detail that resists your first interpretation.

  • Two paths and two thresholds

    Compare a neutral scene description and your imagined fictional observer's response. Identify one inference and a way to test it.

  • Imagination, inspiration and intuition in this book

    Make a three-row table: ordinary analogy, claimed spiritual capacity and what the analogy cannot prove. Use the source's truth–beauty–goodness relations.

  • A rich inner life and modest daily practice

    Choose a five-minute observation or reflective reading. Record one detail, one interpretation and one question. Later test whether it changed a response in an ordinary situation.

  • The unfinished letter: freedom within a given life

    Write a fictional unfinished letter: the given situation, possible responses, responsibilities to others and one support that could widen choice.

  • From personal development to cultural responsibility

    Propose one community improvement: need, participants, knowledge required, value disagreement, practical action and review. Separate ordinary evidence from spiritual interpretation.

Whole-book project

Start with a 150–250-word sketch or a five-minute discussion: one transition, two interpretations, one counterexample and a bounded next action. If useful, develop it into the workbook’s seven-chapter project (1,200–1,800 words or 35–45 minutes), including the limits of what the case can establish.

Model outline: Marina

Marina, 46, cares for a relative and feels stuck in a skilled role. She enjoys helping trainees but has little protected time. A possible motive is making learning accessible; another explanation is relief from repetition. Her caring orientation does not determine a teaching career. A supported training trial, fair workload and attention to her own wishes can test the interpretation. If it exhausts her, revise the conditions or the account instead of forcing it to confirm the theory.

Review rubric

Score each criterion 0 = absent, 1 = partial, 2 = clear and supported. The optional total of 0–12 guides revision; it does not measure a person’s psychological or spiritual level. A reasoned disagreement can meet every criterion.

  • Connections across all seven chapters
  • Facts, interpretations and spiritual claims distinguished
  • Individual context, relationships and material conditions
  • Alternative explanation and contrary evidence
  • A feasible action with consent, support and review
