Bernard Lievegoed · 18 lessons · 5 modules

Phases: Understanding Adult Development

Read a life through its changing conditions, experiences and purposes. Learn the ideas step by step, test them through examples and turn understanding into a considered next step.

Begin with the orientation →
About this source

Supplied excerpt: opening, Chapter One and Chapter Two through the incomplete opening of The Organizational Phase. Later chapters are named in the contents but absent.

What you will learn

  • Distinguish change, growth and development.
  • Bring bodily conditions, psychological experience and values into one account.
  • Read age patterns critically and compare them with particular lives.
  • Explore adolescence, early adult life, work, relationships and organization.
  • Build and revise a biographical interpretation with evidence and practical limits.

No prior anthroposophy study is required. Plan 30–45 minutes per lesson, plus 1–2 hours for the final project: approximately 10–16 hours overall. Move at your own pace; the time is a planning estimate.

How to study

  1. Read the explanation and retell the distinction in your own words.
  2. Work through the example before opening its answer.
  3. Try the exercise, then test the idea in the changed case.
  4. Use the page map with your own copy of the book when available.

The course is self-contained for learning the supplied excerpt. Participation never requires sharing personal history. Use your own notebook for reflections and the workbook prompts.

Your course path

1. Reading a biography

  1. A life is more than a sequence of events

    Learn how Lievegoed turns biography into a question about continuity, meaning and future possibilities.

  2. Disciplined reflection and listening

    Separate remembered events, present feelings and interpretations when studying a life.

  3. Change, growth and development

    Recognize when improvement within an existing arrangement differs from reorganizing the arrangement itself.

  4. Body, psyche and spirit in one situation

    Use three questions to understand an event without turning a person into three separate pieces.

2. Meaning, models and phases

  1. Satisfaction, fulfilment and a guiding motive

    Distinguish meeting a recurring need from pursuing something experienced as worth doing.

  2. Creativity, wisdom and an active middle

    Explain why outward action and receptive judgement can support each other.

  3. Four images of the human being

    Compare what different explanatory models notice and what their exclusive use can miss.

  4. Phases, transitions and the limits of age maps

    Read recurring patterns without treating a birthday as a developmental deadline.

3. Development and adolescence

  1. Three life courses and two kinds of achievement

    Understand why the book refuses to measure a whole life with one rise-and-fall curve.

  2. Childhood: trust, imagination and the search for truth

    Follow the preparatory sequence without treating educational ideals as guaranteed outcomes.

  3. Adolescence: identity, ideals and responsibility

    Explain the value of adolescence in itself and the tension between conviction and experience.

  4. Freedom, creative play and social conditions

    Connect self-discovery with real opportunities to participate and learn.

4. Early adult life and organization

  1. Early adulthood: creating a way of living

    See how ordinary arrangements express values and create a shared culture.

  2. Work, feedback and learning through experience

    Distinguish developing competence from making self-worth depend on every external reaction.

  3. Attraction, recognition and historical gender roles

    Understand the book's relationship distinctions while examining the assumptions surrounding them.

  4. The organizational phase: strength and limitation

    Explain the opening discussion of consolidation without supplying the missing continuation.

5. Contribution and synthesis

  1. Midlife as a possibility of renewed contribution

    Study the available overview of later life without treating crisis or wisdom as inevitable.

  2. Synthesis: read a life, test an interpretation, choose a next step

    Bring the course's distinctions together in a bounded, evidence-based biographical study.
