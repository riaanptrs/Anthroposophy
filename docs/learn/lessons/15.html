Karma

How do deeds connect present action with continuing consequences?

What becomes of a deed once it has been done? Steiner begins karma with two consequences: an action changes the world and changes the person who acts.

Read · Source passage

Rudolf Steiner · Theosophy · GA 9 · Monges / Church, 1971

Printed p. 65 · PDF p. 93

Through an action that the soul has performed, there lives in the soul the energetic predisposition to perform another action that is the fruit of the first action.

Anthroposophic Press, 1971; Henry B. Monges, translator; Gilbert Church, reviser. English excerpt checked against the supplied PDF image; Brazilian Portuguese is a new course study translation. Line breaks and line-end hyphenation normalized.

Course explanation

What the author means

The selected passage describes the inward consequence. Acting develops capacities and a disposition toward further action. The outward consequence includes changed surroundings and relationships. Someone who repairs a house leaves a repair in the world and carries the experience of repairing within themselves.

Steiner extends this relationship across earthly lives. Karma names the connection between deeds and the later field of experience of the continuing individuality. He makes a strong claim: joys, sorrows and encounters can be related to deeds of previous incarnations. This goes beyond the ordinary observation that practice develops skill or that actions affect other people.

The chapter distinguishes three relationships: bodily heredity, the soul’s karma and the spirit’s repeated embodiment. Reincarnation names continuing embodiment; karma concerns consequences meeting that individuality. Present action still matters because it creates new capacities, effects and relationships. The explanation is therefore not a reason to give up responsible action.

Key concept

Karma is Steiner’s proposed relationship between deeds, their enduring effects and later experience; it is distinct from reincarnation itself.

Everyday example

Course illustration: Mira breaks a promise, loses a friend’s trust and learns how difficult trust is to rebuild. The changed relationship is an outer effect; altered judgment is an inner effect. Extending this pattern across lives remains the author’s spiritual claim.

Keep this distinction

Karma is not a course-issued verdict that someone deserves suffering. Understanding the claim supplies no knowledge of another person’s former deeds and no warrant for assigning blame.

The reading follows a deed both into its outward effects and into the capacities and dispositions left within its actor.

Why does present action still matter in this account?

Reveal the explanation

Present activity belongs to the process the chapter describes. Karma is not presented as a reason for passivity.
