Who was Rudolf Steiner?

Rudolf Steiner (1861–1925) was an Austrian philosopher, spiritual researcher, lecturer and educator who developed anthroposophy. His life’s work explored the human being and our relationship with the natural and spiritual worlds. This lesson introduces his life and the main fields of his work, so you can recognise the person behind the ideas we will study.

Next lesson: What is anthroposophy?

Steiner grew up in a family connected with the railway. He was interested in the natural landscape, machines and geometry, and later studied scientific subjects in Vienna. Early in his career, he edited the scientific writings of Johann Wolfgang von Goethe, the German poet and natural researcher. Working with Goethe’s studies of nature deepened his interest in understanding living things through their development.

Questions about knowledge and freedom became central to Steiner’s philosophical writing. In The Philosophy of Freedom, he explored thinking and the possibility of acting from an individually understood moral insight. His books and lectures also developed a spiritual understanding of the human being and the world. This wider work became known as anthroposophy. Theosophy is one of his introductory books on this subject. The next lesson will explain anthroposophy more fully.

Steiner brought this understanding into practical work with teachers, artists and other collaborators. In 1919, he helped establish the first Waldorf school in Stuttgart, Germany. He also worked in drama, architecture and eurythmy, an art of expressive movement connected with speech and music. His agricultural lectures in 1924 became a foundation for biodynamic agriculture. Education, art and agriculture therefore belong to the story of his life: each grew from his concern to bring spiritual understanding into everyday human activity.

The connection running through these fields is Steiner’s exploration of the human being and our relationship with the world. His biography gives us a first picture of how study, spiritual research and practical work developed during one life. Later lessons will introduce the ideas and activities individually.

Follow the stages of his life

The early scientific studies, the work on Goethe and the philosophical writing give a starting point for following Steiner’s development. His later books, lectures and collaborations extend the story into spiritual research and practical work.

Place the book titles

The Philosophy of Freedom belongs to Steiner’s exploration of thinking and free action. Theosophy introduces his spiritual understanding of the human being. Recognising these titles helps you place two important books within his wider life’s work.

Connect the person with the fields of work

A Waldorf school and biodynamic agriculture may be your first encounters with Steiner’s name. His biography shows where these movements enter his life: education through the first school in 1919, and agriculture through the lectures of 1924.

Key concept

Rudolf Steiner developed anthroposophy and worked across philosophy, spiritual teaching, education and the arts.

Everyday example

Imagine encountering a Waldorf school, a building designed by Steiner and a biodynamic farm. These belong to education, architecture and agriculture: three fields introduced in his biography. Their connection is the work Steiner and his collaborators undertook to bring anthroposophy into practical life.

Keep this distinction

Anthroposophy is the broader spiritual conception. Waldorf education is one educational movement connected with it. Knowing this relationship helps you place the different courses on the site.
