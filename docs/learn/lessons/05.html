Body, Soul and Spirit

Why does Steiner distinguish three relationships within one encounter?

One encounter can be described in more than one way. Steiner begins his account of the human being by distinguishing bodily contact, personal inner experience and the understanding of relationships.

Optional close reading · excerpt included
Read · Source passage

Rudolf Steiner · Theosophy · GA 9 · Monges / Church, 1971

Printed p. 4 · PDF p. 32

Through his body man is able to place himself for the time being in connection with things; through his soul he retains in himself the impressions they make on him; through his spirit there reveals itself to him what the things retain for themselves.

Course explanation

Understand the idea

The selected sentence gives three directions. Through the body we enter a temporary connection with things. Through the soul their impressions become part of our inner life. Through spirit something belonging to the things themselves can be understood, beyond our liking or dislike.

The flower example makes time important. On a return to a meadow, the earlier flowers are gone. The remembered joy belongs to the observer, while a correctly understood relationship of the species can be recognized in new flowers. Passing object, retained experience and intelligible relationship do not have the same dependence on this one encounter.

Soul here includes more than emotion. Sensation, pleasure and pain, desire and willing make the world inwardly significant and return inner activity to the world. Spirit is also more than having an opinion: Steiner connects knowing with a significance not exhausted by a private response.

These are related aspects of one human being. Later distinctions make bodily, soul and spiritual life more specific. The opening example prepares that account; it does not independently demonstrate everything Steiner subsequently says about spiritual reality.

An encounter has several aspects

Consider hearing a melody. The body receives sound through its organs; the soul may feel pleasure and remember it; thinking can recognise a musical relationship. The distinctions describe aspects of one encounter.

Body and soul are not synonyms

Sound reaching the ear and the enjoyment of that sound answer different questions. One concerns bodily contact with the world; the other concerns inward experience. A physical description alone does not describe what the music means to this listener.

What spirit contributes to the account

Spirit concerns participation in meaning and understanding beyond a passing personal impression. Recognising a relationship in the melody can be discussed with others, even when each listener’s feeling differs. The three aspects remain connected in one person.

Key concept

Body connects with the world; soul inwardly experiences and retains impressions; spirit participates in understanding what belongs to the things themselves.

Everyday example

Two friends watch the same stars. Both see the lights, but one feels wonder and the other homesickness. They can nevertheless examine the same explanation of their apparent movement. This course example separates personal response from a shared intelligible relationship.

Keep this distinction

Body–soul–spirit is not another spelling of physical–etheric–astral or thinking–feeling–willing. Each classification answers a particular question and needs its own context.

Reveal the explanation

The joy is retained as personal experience. The flowers and the understood relationship have different connections with time and the observer.

Body, soul and spirit describe connected aspects of one human being in the opening account.

Reveal the explanation

The three relationships concern one person’s encounter, rather than three independent occupants of a body.

Describe seeing a flower through body, soul and spirit.

Pause and answer in one sentence, aloud or in your notes.

See a way to approach it

Body: eyes receive its visible appearance. Soul: the sight awakens feeling and can be remembered. Spirit: the person seeks to understand the plant and its relationships. These are different aspects of one experience.
