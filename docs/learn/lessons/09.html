The I

Foundations · Lesson 9 / 36

The Question

What changes between feeling irritation, recognizing it as yours, and considering how to respond?

Begin with Experience

Suppose you accuse someone while angry, then discover that your accusation was false. Recognizing the anger as yours already illustrates self-awareness. Examining the evidence and acknowledging your mistake illustrates working on your response; an apology can put that revised understanding into action. The evidence does not become true because you prefer it. This example concerns taking responsibility for your response, rather than proving that you have reached a spiritual stage.

Steiner’s Idea

From “I feel irritated” to “How shall I respond?”

You hear the bell, feel irritated and want it to stop. You can also recognize: “This is my irritation.” You might then ask, “Do I want to act on it?” Steiner uses the I for the individuality that recognizes experiences as its own and can work with them. The feeling, recognizing it as yours, and considering your response are distinguishable aspects of one person’s experience.

Recognizing an experience as mine

When two people say “I”, each refers to themselves. In Steiner’s account, the I concerns the person who can recognize a feeling, memory or desire as their own. It is not another passing feeling alongside irritation or enjoyment, nor a little observer inside the head. Recognizing “I am angry” is self-awareness; asking “How shall I respond?” introduces working on the experience. Considering a response does not guarantee that you will immediately act as you intend.

Self-awareness without perfect self-control

Here, “I” does not mean being selfish or self-important. Nor does it mean an inner controller that can instantly command every feeling. The I does not disappear when you make a mistake or struggle to change a habit. Steiner describes an individuality that can gradually work with its experiences. The more detailed relationships with consciousness soul and spirit self appear in the optional explanation and Lesson 11. Next we distinguish thinking, feeling and willing as three activities involved in responding.

Important Distinction

I or Ego does not simply mean selfishness, ordinary personality or every passing thought. The I, consciousness soul and spirit self are related but distinct terms.

An ordinary example can clarify a distinction without establishing Steiner’s further spiritual claims. Understanding the account does not require accepting it as established fact.

Apply It

Recall a minor irritation. Distinguish having the feeling, recognizing it and choosing how to act. Does self-awareness guarantee a wise choice?

Check Your Understanding

What changes between feeling irritation, recognizing it as yours, and considering how to respond?

Compare with an explained answer

Irritation is the feeling. Recognizing “this is my irritation” relates the experience to yourself. Considering how to respond means examining what to do with it. Steiner connects these latter relationships with the I; they do not require the feeling to vanish or guarantee perfect control.

If you recognize your anger but still make a mistake, does that mean the I has disappeared?

Compare with an explained answer

No. Recognizing the anger already illustrates self-awareness. Working on a response can take time, practice and correction. The I is not a reward for behaving well or a guarantee of perfect self-control.
