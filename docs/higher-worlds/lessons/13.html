Course 2 · Lesson 13

Waking, dreaming and dreamless sleep

Distinguish the three states and explain Steiner's conditions for connecting initially isolated spiritual experiences.

What does continuity of consciousness mean in this account?

1 · Read the passage

Begin with the book

Rudolf Steiner · How to Know Higher Worlds · GA 10 · § 9

For a person who has reached this stage, experiencing does not cease during those times when the physical body rests and no impressions reach the soul through the sense organs.

Original German text; English and Brazilian Portuguese study translations. Related discussion in the supplied English PDF: The continuity of consciousness · PDF p. 86, column 3.

Compare with the German passage

What this passage means

The chapter's conclusion concerns an ideal of conscious experience extending through both dreaming and dreamless sleep while the body rests. Earlier paragraphs call for patience, distinguish unfamiliar from everyday-related experiences, and oppose constructing a complete system from isolated impressions. The continuity described here is the end of a long path, and the author still requires a connection with waking life; it is not a proposal to dispense with sleep.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

Understand the idea

This chapter distinguishes waking life, dreaming sleep and dreamless sleep. Steiner gives dreams an independent place rather than treating all sleep as a single state. The transformed dream life described previously is only a beginning: its experiences must also become available while awake before the student may regard them as knowledge. Further development is then said to introduce isolated conscious experiences into previously unconscious deep sleep.

Descriptions of these experiences depend on simile. Steiner compares the dream-derived world with vision and the first deep-sleep perceptions with hearing tones and words. He expressly warns that ordinary language is adapted to the physical world and supplies only approximate images for supersensible life. A further symbolic language would be learned through training. The comparison therefore should not quietly become a claim that a physical sound has occurred, or that vivid inner imagery already fulfils the account.

The first experiences of deep sleep are described as faint and difficult to recall clearly. Quiet receptivity, composure and patience are emphasized; force and impatience are said to delay development or make a momentarily acquired faculty disappear. The book distinguishes two kinds of experience: those entirely unfamiliar, which should provisionally be left alone, and those connected with questions already considered in ordinary life. The latter allegedly turn obscure concepts into living experience and disclose the spiritual connections behind physical things.

Steiner again connects perception with forming organs of the soul. Concentration directs attention to definite ideas, while meditation means living within them. Gestation and birth illustrate how a higher soul-being is said to mature; sleep initially protects its delicate activity from stronger sensory processes. Yet even these experiences are not fully valid knowledge until the awakened soul can be carried into waking consciousness. The emergence of an impression during sleep and its claimed integration with everyday perception are distinct stages.

The author also warns against prematurely building a complete system from isolated experiences. Such a construction could mix fantasy with observation and yield a world unrelated to the spiritual reality being sought. His instruction is to clarify individual experiences, practise self-control and await connections that emerge with further experience. This is a rule about the course of the knowledge he claims; it does not make an unsupported interpretation reliable merely because its author waited before presenting it.

Continuity of consciousness names the proposed ideal in which consciousness extends across both dreaming and dreamless sleep while the physical body rests. Steiner calls it the end of a long path, not an immediate result of one exercise. The student must still connect the experience with the physical environment and retain a firm footing in daily life. Continuity does not mean foregoing sleep; the chapter repeatedly locates the expanded experience during bodily rest.

Use the three-state distinction to read the sequence precisely. The book claims a passage from unconscious activity to consciously experienced spiritual worlds, not simply improved dream recall. Our activity studies that sequence and its stated limits. Understanding it does not require changing one's sleep, reporting special experiences or supplying a personal system about what those experiences mean.

Keep this idea

The proposed continuity extends consciousness across three states while requiring patience, disciplined interpretation and waking integration.

Words for the reading

  • Dreamless sleep — the state ordinarily described as unconscious, distinct from dreaming sleep.
  • Continuity of consciousness — Steiner's ideal of retaining conscious spiritual experience through sleep.
  • Simile — a comparison that helps describe an experience without making it identical with the physical example.
  • Premature synthesis — joining isolated experiences into a system before their connection is established.

See the idea in an example

A fictional notebook records three separate dream impressions. Its author initially writes a grand theory connecting them, then rereads the chapter and marks which connections were actually observed and which were invented.

What this helps explain

The example illustrates the warning against premature synthesis. Removing an invented connection clarifies the record; it does not by itself prove that the remaining impressions disclose a spiritual world.
