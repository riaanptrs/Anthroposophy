Course 2 · Lesson 07

Patience, desire and tact

Trace how the practical chapter connects inner order, motives and relationships with the development of knowledge.

How does Steiner join patience with developing capacities?

1 · Read the passage

Begin with the book

Rudolf Steiner · How to Know Higher Worlds · GA 10 · Some practical aspects · PDF p. 43, column 2

And he does whatever must be done to meet the affront with calm and composure, and not in a spirit of anger.

English edition: Dead Authors Society, 2018; translator not identified in the supplied capture. Line breaks and line-wrap hyphenation normalized.

Open the source or edition index →

What this passage means

The immediately preceding sentence says the affront does not change the recipient’s intrinsic worth; the following sentence explicitly rejects simply accepting every affront. Keep those two directions together. The practical chapter is concerned with how the response is formed, and then develops self-knowledge, educated desire and tact. Calm action in this passage can include a firm intervention.

The focus of this lesson

Trace how the practical chapter connects inner order, motives and relationships with the development of knowledge.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

Understand the idea

The practical chapter explains the preceding training by analogy with bodily organization. Steiner says thoughts and feelings acquire an order comparable to the arrangement of bodily organs, and describes the soul and spirit as becoming capable of their own seeing, hearing and speaking. His descriptions of colour and structure belong to his claim about spiritual perception. The chapter then gives patience, desire, self-knowledge and consideration for others a concrete place in that development.

Patience requires doing what one can while relinquishing a demand for immediate illumination. Merely pushing impatience out of awareness can, the author says, conceal rather than overcome it. The proposed attitude joins active work to willingness to await a result. Attention to a small gain, repose and steadiness matter more than a timetable imposed on the higher powers described in the book.

An affront supplies a precise example. Steiner says an insult does not alter the recipient’s intrinsic worth, and that the response should be as composed as a response on someone else’s behalf. He explicitly allows an appropriate intervention: calmness is not acceptance of every insult. This connects inner change to how a response is formed, rather than to a requirement that all outward disagreement disappear.

Self-knowledge means recognizing faults without inventing excuses for them. The text contrasts a selfish curiosity about unusual information with joy in knowledge that can help one serve. Desire is to be educated through knowledge, not mechanically extinguished. Its example is a wish to know a previous life: the author requires study of the eternal first, and even the ability to relinquish that particular wish while studying.

Anger is described as hiding spiritual currents that the learner might otherwise perceive. Yet the chapter explicitly says that overcoming a little anger does not immediately produce an organ of vision. The training concerns a whole configuration of qualities, including fear, superstition, vanity, ambition and prejudice. Steiner criticizes judgments based on rank, sex and race here; compare that stated demand with the racial hierarchy asserted later in the book, rather than omitting the tension.

Tact begins by considering what the speaker means and feels before planning a reply. The student retains an opinion, but lets attentive listening shape its expression. Steiner makes the aim another person’s discovery of what is right, rather than victory for one’s own difference of opinion. Gentleness and patient reserve are then presented as conditions under which inner capacities grow.

The chapter returns the learner from a period of inward quiet to customary affairs. It opposes attempts to force spiritual results and warns against fragmentary instructions promising faster routes. These warnings are part of the author’s method: the kind of result matters along with the speed of obtaining it. Our activity examines these relationships in the text without treating unusual experiences as a course requirement.

Environment also receives attention. Nature is described as favouring harmonious development, but a city dweller is offered study of spiritual texts as another source of nourishment. The concluding examples show differing occasions of development—a journey, an event of destiny, or long quiet perseverance. They express the author’s rejection of a single outward schedule, and lead to the next chapter’s more explicit conditions.

Outward politeness alone does not disclose a motive. A response may come from fear of disagreement or from an understood concern for another person. Patient listening can help us learn their actual aims; it does not require assuming that we already know what is best for them. Understanding another person’s aim does not mean endorsing every action. We can listen, ask for advice and examine reasons while retaining our own judgment. Steiner’s account of free action includes acting on a justified principle; independence does not require rejecting cooperation or agreed responsibilities.

Additional connection: The Philosophy of Freedom (GA 4), markers 85–89, 126–127 in the 147-page Basis transcription. Locate by chapter in another edition. Find the book.

Begin with a capacity already present

The Four Temperaments proposes starting from an existing interest, sensitivity or capacity rather than relying on reproach. Try this as an original application of patient attention: describe a fictional learner’s actual response, offer one fitting invitation and identify what would make you revise it. This adds a practical example; it does not require assigning the learner a fixed temperament.

The practical chapter joins patient effort and honest motives to considerate, purposeful action.

Words for the reading

  • Patience — active effort without insisting on an immediate result.
  • Educated desire — a wish shaped by knowledge of its object and purpose.
  • Tact — attention to another person that guides the form of a reply.

See the idea in an example

Sam is blamed for a missed deadline that changed without notice. Sam checks the messages, asks how the misunderstanding arose, and explains the relevant facts firmly.

What this helps explain

Sam’s response illustrates the difference between surrendering a position and choosing its expression carefully. The scene makes the text’s account of an affront and tact concrete without claiming a spiritual result.

No. Listening should shape the reply, and the aim is a helpful contribution to understanding rather than an immediate victory.
