Course 2 · Lesson 08

Seven conditions: health, participation and inner life

Reconstruct the first three conditions and their place within freely chosen training.

What does the first condition ask concerning health?

Optional close reading · excerpt included
1 · Read the passage

Read the supporting excerpt

Rudolf Steiner · How to Know Higher Worlds · GA 10 · The conditions of esoteric training · PDF p. 49, column 1

It should be emphasized that the complete fulfilment of any one of these conditions is not insisted upon, but only the corresponding effort.

English edition: Dead Authors Society, 2018; translator not identified in the supplied capture. Line breaks and line-wrap hyphenation normalized.

Open the source or edition index →

What this passage means

This qualification precedes all seven conditions. It explains why the first condition can acknowledge that health is not wholly under individual control, while still asking for care of bodily and inner life. The conditions belong to freely chosen esoteric training in the author’s account. They are not requirements for entry to the reading course, nor a basis for ranking people by illness or outward circumstances.

The focus of this lesson

Reconstruct the first three conditions and their place within freely chosen training.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

Understand the idea

The chapter presents its conditions as consequences of the knowledge sought, using the analogy that learning to paint requires willingness to use a brush. It insists on freely chosen participation and calls the teacher’s contribution advice; the footnote directs readers to the editorial preface and appendix. Wanting information while refusing any change in one’s thoughts and feelings is, for Steiner, insufficient. These are his conditions for esoteric training, rather than entrance requirements for this reading course.

Before enumerating the conditions, the author says complete fulfilment is not required: the corresponding effort of will matters. The first condition concerns advancement of bodily and spiritual health. Health is not wholly under personal control, and the unhealthy are explicitly not rejected. What is asked is a willingness to care for one’s life within the possibilities of one’s circumstances.

The first condition distinguishes duty, pleasure and asceticism. Steiner makes the demanding claim that duty can stand above health and even life, while pleasure must not stand above them. He also rejects deprivation pursued for the same self-satisfaction as indulgence. Read these assertions as the author’s proposed hierarchy of motives. They neither supply medical advice nor make illness or deprivation a measure of a reader’s worth.

The condition also concerns the life of thought and feeling: clear thinking, steadiness and an effort to do justice to circumstances are contrasted with exaggeration, fanaticism and a merely imagined world. The author says a change of social position is unnecessary for training; any kind of work can contribute to humanity. His emphasis is on what one can cultivate within a real life, rather than on claiming a superior outward role.

The second condition is feeling oneself a link in the whole of life. A teacher whose pupil struggles is asked to consider the contribution of the teacher’s own conduct. A criminal provides a more demanding example: different education and circumstances might have changed the life in question. Steiner uses these cases to move from immediate condemnation toward reflection on shared human conditions and one’s own responsibilities.

This second condition is initially an inward orientation. The text explicitly distinguishes it from political demands directed at others, insisting that the student begin with self-reform. Preserve that limitation when explaining the passage. The claim of shared responsibility is broader than a factual judgment that everyone caused the same event or deserves the same blame.

The third condition assigns thoughts and feelings a cosmic importance comparable to actions. Steiner makes the strong assertion that hatred harms as a blow does, and that inner purification benefits the world. This is part of his spiritual account of causation, rather than merely a suggestion to think politely. Explain its meaning without treating an involuntary thought as an intentional deed or using it to assign guilt to another person. The fourth condition will make explicit the spiritual conception of the human being on which this argument depends.

Keep this idea

The first three conditions connect care for life, participation in humanity and responsibility for inner activity.

Words for the reading

  • Condition — a prerequisite the author gives for esoteric development.
  • Link in the whole — participation in a shared life with other human beings.
  • Cosmic significance — effects attributed to inner activity beyond its outward expression.

See the idea in an example

A teacher receives an incomplete assignment. Instead of assuming laziness immediately, she checks whether her instructions, the available materials and the pupil’s circumstances contributed to the difficulty.

What this helps explain

The scene follows the book’s teacher-and-pupil example: participation changes the questions the teacher asks. It does not establish that she alone caused the result or remove the pupil’s agency.
