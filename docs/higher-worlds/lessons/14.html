Course 2 · Lesson 14

Thinking, feeling and willing under conscious guidance

Explain the claimed separation of the three soul-forces, their required coordination and the three errors of imbalance.

What coordinates thinking, feeling and willing after their automatic link loosens?

1 · Read the passage

Begin with the book

Rudolf Steiner · How to Know Higher Worlds · GA 10 · § 6

The organs of thinking, feeling and willing then stand entirely independently of one another.

Original German text; English and Brazilian Portuguese study translations. Related discussion in the supplied English PDF: The partition of human personality during spiritual training · PDF p. 90, column 1.

Read the original chapter or lecture (German) →

Compare with the German passage

Die Organe des Denkens, Fühlens und Wollens stehen sodann ganz frei für sich da.

What this passage means

The independence of the three forces creates a task of coordination, not a goal of disconnection. Steiner assigns their combined activity to awakened higher consciousness and then describes three failures: domination by will, dependence through feeling and withdrawal through thinking. The adjacent claim about a threefold brain is explicitly beyond ordinary instruments in his account; it should not be presented as an anatomical finding. Preparation and ordinary duties remain essential to the chapter's intended balance.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

Understand the idea

Steiner begins from the claim that the soul remains active in higher worlds during sleep, although ordinary consciousness does not perceive that activity. Training would make it conscious. Before this transition, higher cosmic beings are said to guide the soul; afterwards the student must undertake some of that guidance independently. Greater freedom therefore brings responsibility for connections previously maintained without conscious participation.

The chapter repeatedly locates the purpose of this development on earth. Spiritual knowledge should enable a person to bring creative insight into the physical world, rather than evade daily tasks. Steiner attributes dangers to impatience, arrogance, insufficient knowledge and selfish use of powers; he also asserts that correctly conducted training avoids harm. These assurances belong to his account. They do not provide independent evidence about the health effects of an esoteric practice.

Thinking, feeling and willing ordinarily occur in connected sequences: perceiving a stuffy room, feeling discomfort and opening a window; hearing a question and answering it. Steiner relates these connections to links in finer bodies and counterparts in the physical organism. Training is said to release the three forces from their automatic links so that higher consciousness must coordinate them. The familiar distinction between a thought, a feeling and an action is understandable on its own; the asserted alteration of finer bodies is an additional claim.

He extends the description to a division of the brain into thought-, feeling- and will-brains. The text explicitly says this division cannot be demonstrated by ordinary instruments and claims it is perceptible to clairvoyance. Present it as Steiner's supersensible physiological assertion, rather than established neuroanatomy. The claimed gain is deliberate command over combined activity. Freed soul-forces would also relate to corresponding world-forces: hatred, for example, is described as a visible coloured cloud that can be met in spiritual perception.

Three errors arise if the forces separate before higher consciousness can hold them together. Predominant will can become uncontrolled violence; predominant feeling can become unlimited dependence that suppresses judgment and initiative; predominant thinking can become cold withdrawal into a loveless pursuit of knowledge. The author gives each deviation a distinct mechanism: the balancing influence of the other two forces no longer restrains the strongest one.

The necessary preparation is harmonious development before separation, with self-mastery, presence of mind and regular waking life continuing afterwards. Steiner uses historical medical language when describing how imbalance might appear to outside observers. That comparison should not become a spiritual diagnosis of a reader or a substitute for health care. His own practical criterion remains the ability to meet life's demands with body, soul and spirit in balance.

A study of this chapter should therefore distinguish freedom from disconnection. Its desired result is consciously coordinated activity, not the cultivation of emotional numbness, dependence or uncontrolled impulse. Our fictional example examines ordinary coordination as an aid to understanding the argument. It does not reproduce the esoteric change or establish the claimed transformation of the brain.

Chapter 8 of GA 4 distinguishes understanding, personal feeling and acting upon a situation. Its 1918 addition presents living thinking as containing feeling and will in their deeper reality, and describes its engagement with the world in terms of spiritual love. This does not make every thought caring or correct. Understanding a need, caring about a person and offering help can belong to one response. The distinction helps orient the reading; it does not establish GA 10’s account of the spiritual separation of these activities.

One situation, three aspects

Consider the aspects of this everyday response together. They are not isolated compartments or a required sequence.

Thinking: understanding the situation

A participant says she cannot see the board. You ask whether changing seats would help and consider the options.

Feeling: personal significance

You feel concern and want her to participate. The feeling matters, but alone it does not reveal which solution will help.

Willing: carrying out an action

With her agreement, you move a chair to a place from which she says she can see. The action changes the shared situation.

Distinguish the participant’s report, your interpretation, your feeling and your action. Then ask how the motive to help arose. Carrying out an action does not by itself demonstrate that it was free.

Keep this idea

The chapter's ideal is conscious coordination of thinking, feeling and willing, prepared by harmony and exercised through earthly responsibility.

Words for the reading

  • Soul-forces — thinking, feeling and willing in Steiner's account.
  • Higher consciousness — the agency said to coordinate these forces after their automatic links loosen.
  • Imbalance — domination by one force without the others' corrective influence.
  • Earthly task — applying capacities in physical life rather than escaping its responsibilities.
