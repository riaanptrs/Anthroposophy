Course 2 · Lesson 14

Thinking, feeling and willing

Explain the proposed separation of these forces and the responsibility to coordinate them.

Recall from Course 1: Lesson 6 →

Start with an example

Morgan understands a neighbour’s difficulty, feels concern, and chooses a useful action. Another response might stop at abstract analysis without attending to what is needed.

What this helps explain

The comparison illustrates coordination among understanding, feeling and action. It does not demonstrate an occult separation of organs or identify a medical condition.

Understand the idea

In ordinary life, thinking, feeling and willing work in connected patterns: noticing a situation can bring a feeling and an intention to act. Steiner uses familiar examples before making the stronger claim that spiritual training can loosen the connections among these forces.

His account then places responsibility for their coordination in a higher consciousness. It describes possible excesses of will, feeling or thought when this coordination fails. This is more than the ordinary ability to distinguish a thought from an emotion. Nor should the three forces simply be substituted for every use of body, soul and spirit in GA 9.

The chapter extends its claim to the brain and uses historical language about mental disturbance. These assertions are part of the text to examine, not contemporary neuroscience or a diagnostic guide. Its repeated practical emphasis is continued participation in earthly life and the integration of capacities.

Chapter 8 of GA 4 distinguishes understanding, personal feeling and acting upon a situation. Its 1918 addition presents living thinking as containing feeling and will in their deeper reality, and describes its engagement with the world in terms of spiritual love. This does not make every thought caring or correct. Understanding a need, caring about a person and offering help can belong to one response. The distinction helps orient the reading; it does not establish GA 10’s account of the spiritual separation of these activities.

Additional connection: The Philosophy of Freedom (GA 4), markers 71–74 in the 147-page Basis transcription. Locate by chapter in another edition. Find the book.

One situation, three aspects

Consider the aspects of this everyday response together. They are not isolated compartments or a required sequence.

Thinking: understanding the situation

A participant says she cannot see the board. You ask whether changing seats would help and consider the options.

Feeling: personal significance

You feel concern and want her to participate. The feeling matters, but alone it does not reveal which solution will help.

Willing: carrying out an action

With her agreement, you move a chair to a place from which she says she can see. The action changes the shared situation.

Distinguish the participant’s report, your interpretation, your feeling and your action. Then ask how the motive to help arose. Carrying out an action does not by itself demonstrate that it was free.

Original course example: GA 4, Basis markers 71–74; Brian, Chapter 8, 07:24–10:53 and 17:01–18:17.

Keep this idea

Distinguishing capacities is different from establishing their proposed spiritual separation.

Reading: Rudolf Steiner, GA 10, Partition of personality. Page markers 85–92.

Words for the reading

  • Thinking — forming and relating ideas.
  • Willing — the orientation toward and activity of action.
Try it

Work with the idea

Analyse Morgan’s response under thinking, feeling and willing. Then state precisely what the chapter claims beyond this ordinary distinction.

A study activity created for this course. Assess understanding of the text; personal or spiritual experiences need not be reported.

Check your understanding

What is the chapter’s stronger claim?

Show a suggested answer

That the connections among these forces can loosen through spiritual development and require conscious coordination.

Are these forces simply synonyms for body, soul and spirit?

Show a suggested answer

No. Compare classifications in their contexts rather than imposing a one-to-one equivalence.

Should these passages be used to diagnose a classmate?

Show a suggested answer

No. They describe the author’s spiritual account and contain historical bodily and medical claims.

How to assess your response

Give yourself 0–2 points for each criterion: accurate meaning, clear distinctions, support from the text and quality of reasoning. 0 = missing; 1 = partial; 2 = clear and supported. Revisit incomplete points. Well-supported disagreement can receive full marks.
