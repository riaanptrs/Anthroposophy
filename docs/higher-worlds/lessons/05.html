Course 2 · Lesson 05

Human desire, moral preparation and courage

Explain why contemplation of another person is joined to character, dignity, patience and courage.

Why does character development precede observation of another person?

1 · Read the passage

Begin with the book

Rudolf Steiner · How to Know Higher Worlds · GA 10 · The Control of Thoughts and Feelings · PDF p. 31, column 2

For every one step that you take in the pursuit of higher knowledge, take three steps in the perfection of your own character.

English edition: Dead Authors Society, 2018; translator not identified in the supplied capture. Line breaks and line-wrap hyphenation normalized.

Open the source or edition index →

What this passage means

The rule precedes the observation of human desire and its fulfillment. It gives moral preparation priority over new knowledge and expressly excludes selfish advantage over another person. Read it with the following insistence on human dignity and freedom.

The focus of this lesson

Explain why contemplation of another person is joined to character, dignity, patience and courage.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

Understand the idea

Before turning from natural objects to human life, Steiner sets a moral prerequisite: for each step toward higher knowledge, take three steps in the perfection of character. The learner must exclude the intention to use knowledge of another person for selfish advantage or harm. This places the human exercises within the earlier aim of knowledge for service, rather than treating insight as a power to possess.

The first exercise recalls a person filled with desire while it is still uncertain whether the desired object will be obtained. Attention concentrates on that remembered state, with inner tranquillity and patient repetition. Steiner describes a feeling corresponding to the other person’s state and a later luminous astral picture. Its flame-like quality is compared with yellowish-red centrally and reddish-blue or lilac at the edges.

The paired exercise considers someone whose wish has been fulfilled. With the same stated conditions, Steiner describes a different spiritual flame, yellow in the center and green at the edge. Desire awaiting fulfillment and desire satisfied are therefore distinct stages of the example. The lesson studies their place in the argument; it does not ask classmates to identify invisible states in each other.

The chapter immediately binds such observation to reverence for human dignity and liberty. A person must never become merely an object for investigation. The text recognizes the danger of becoming uncharitable and requires unlimited respect for the individual’s own sphere, even in recollection. For the course activity, fictional scenes allow us to consider the difference between observing conduct and claiming authority over another person’s inner life.

A further instruction concerns reticence. Steiner says that premature descriptions in ordinary language can mix the experience with invented additions, because the available words and habits of judgment do not yet fit the proposed perception. He asks for silence about spiritual experiences until the learner can speak from steadiness and certainty. This is the author’s rule for the practice he describes; source questions and discussion remain part of our reading course.

Patience, inner composure and outward conduct are described as supporting each other. Before initiation, the learner must also develop courage and fearlessness. Ordinary life is offered as a school: in difficulty, attention should move toward what can be done rather than remain absorbed by fear. Our example uses a manageable responsibility; understanding the chapter does not require seeking out danger.

The reason for courage is more exact than enthusiasm. Steiner says that higher knowledge would reveal constructive and destructive forces, birth and death, and one’s own soul without its usual disguise. Motives such as vanity and greed would lose their force, making new reasons for action necessary. The learner must be able to meet failure without despair and face the future with renewed effort. The section ends with spiritual names of things, understood as expressions of their being, before the initiation chapter begins.

Keep this idea

Study another person’s situation with respect, and connect the pursuit of knowledge with the formation of character.

Words for the reading

  • Character — the moral dispositions that guide the use of knowledge and action.
  • Human dignity — the worth of a person who must never be merely an observation object.
  • Courage — steadiness before difficulty and self-knowledge, rather than a search for peril.

See the idea in an example

In one fictional scene, Jo is waiting for a promised result and repeatedly checks for a message. In another, Jo receives the result and becomes visibly relieved. A friend notices the change without announcing that they have seen Jo’s hidden spiritual nature.

What this helps explain

The two scenes separate desire before and after fulfillment. Observable behavior can help a discussion, but respect leaves room for Jo’s own account. The example also keeps another person from becoming material for an unsupported classification.
