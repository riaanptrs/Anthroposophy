Inner tranquillity and the higher human being

The student must seek the power of confronting his own self, at certain times, as a stranger.

Open the source or edition index →

What this passage means

The stranger standpoint belongs to a limited interval of review. The surrounding hill comparison explains how details change proportion when surveyed from outside the immediate reaction. Later pages add contemplation beyond personal circumstances; the whole chapter is broader than self-review.

The focus of this lesson

Trace the movement from calm self-review to clear meditation and the awakening of the higher human being.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

Understand the idea

The first practical rule sets aside moments of inner tranquillity in which the learner distinguishes the essential from the nonessential. Steiner says that five minutes can suffice when that is all one’s duties allow; the manner of attention matters more than extending the interval. The rule is introduced with a law requiring an initiate’s words and actions to respect another person’s free will. Advice should strengthen independence rather than establish a teacher’s mastery.

The initial exercise reviews one’s joys, sorrows, cares, actions and experiences as if they belonged to someone else. The aim is the quiet standpoint of an observer or judge, rather than a fresh absorption in the original reaction. A view from a hill illustrates the change: things encountered close at hand acquire different proportions when the whole scene can be surveyed. The essential can then be distinguished from what merely demanded immediate attention.

The book distinguishes earlier experiences from current blows of fate, which need not become the material of this review. Its stated value lies especially in developing the power of collected attention. A precise account of an action allows responsibility without turning a mistake into a verdict on one’s entire being. Our fictional activity studies this standpoint; it does not require anyone to disclose a painful personal experience.

Steiner relates the practice to a higher human being within the everyday human being, which each person must awaken for themselves. Like a plant needing room to grow, this higher being needs the space provided by inner calm. Daily work continues, but quiet intervals are said to make the learner steadier, more confident and more able to direct how an impression affects them. The higher human is a spiritual reality in this account, rather than simply an improved work habit.

Concrete cases show the intended connection with everyday life. A discouraging thought about a task gives way to the intention to bring all available strength to it. A wounding remark need no longer dictate one’s inner response. Waiting, formerly filled with impatience, can become useful observation. The purpose is to acquire an inward guide for action instead of letting circumstances continually determine one’s state.

Self-review is only one side of the chapter. The learner must also rise beyond their particular situation to questions that would concern a human being under quite different circumstances. This contemplative movement leads, in Steiner’s description, into a living world of thought and an inner word through which spiritual beings speak. Meditation means contemplative reflection that can expand into life in spiritual being. He explicitly requires clear, sharp and definite thoughts, rather than indefinite sensations.

The proposed starting material includes writings that Steiner regards as arising from spiritual investigation. Their thoughts should be understood and worked through instead of blindly accepting every idea that happens to occur. Guidance is presented as the advice of a friend, with freedom retained. The chapter connects meditation to an eternal individuality and memory beyond birth and death, continuing the questions of spiritual identity and reincarnation from Theosophy.

This spiritual direction is meant to return strength to daily life. Ordinary labor and even an insignificant action are placed within a cosmic whole; meditation should deepen engagement with duties. The chapter ends by naming probation, enlightenment and initiation: the development of spiritual senses, the kindling of spiritual light, and intercourse with higher beings. These functions prepare the next lessons without turning self-review into the whole path.

In GA 4 Chapter 3, observation includes inward experiences such as feelings and memories, as well as sensory encounters. Noticing a memory does not establish that the remembered event happened exactly that way. To review thinking, first work out an ordinary departure time; afterwards reconstruct the reasons and distinguish them from feelings about the result. This is a change in what you attend to, not a requirement to leave your body or empty your mind. The comparison does not establish the wider spiritual results described in GA 10.

Additional connection: The Philosophy of Freedom (GA 4), markers 17–28 in the 147-page Basis transcription. Locate by chapter in another edition. Find the book.

Keep this idea

Calm self-review creates distance; clear contemplation directs that space toward the wider questions of human life.

Words for the reading

  • Higher human being — the spiritual being within the everyday person that Steiner says must be awakened.

See the idea in an example

Yesterday, Alex interrupted a colleague. From within the moment, Alex remembers only irritation. On review, Alex distinguishes the interruption, the unfinished explanation, the assumption that caused the interruption and the possibility of listening to the whole account next time.

What this helps explain

The changed standpoint alters the proportions of the event without removing responsibility. It illustrates the first part of the chapter; contemplation of universal human questions and the higher human being extends beyond this everyday example.
