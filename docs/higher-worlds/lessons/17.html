Course 2 · Lesson 17

The appendix: clear thought, self-study and ordinary life

Use the 1918 clarification to distinguish activity, perception, illustrative language and personal instruction.

What distinction does the appendix make about pure thinking?

Optional close reading · excerpt included
1 · Read the passage

Read the supporting excerpt

Rudolf Steiner · How to Know Higher Worlds · GA 10 · Appendix to the author's latest edition, 1918 · PDF p. 106, column 1

With pure thought we live in the supersensible; but we experience this alone in supersensible fashion; we do not, as yet, experience anything else supersensibly.

English edition: Dead Authors Society, 2018; translator not identified in the supplied capture. Line breaks and line-wrap hyphenation normalized.

Open the source or edition index →

What this passage means

The sentence separates an activity of thinking from further contents of supersensible perception. Steiner calls the former already supersensible, yet does not claim that thinking alone discloses every spiritual reality described in the book. The following argument requires the wider experience to preserve the clarity of this activity.

The focus of this lesson

Use the 1918 clarification to distinguish activity, perception, illustrative language and personal instruction.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

Understand the idea

The appendix begins by warning against misidentifying the path described in the book with superstition, visionary dreaming or mediumship. Steiner places it within conscious soul and spiritual activity. This ending supplies criteria for rereading the whole work: increased clarity, active participation and independence matter more than the mere occurrence of an unusual experience.

Steiner first considers pure thought. In ordinary experience, thinking is usually mixed with perception, feeling, willing and memory. He argues that an inner effort can separate a self-sustaining thinking activity from these other contents, and that a person experiencing this activity knows themselves outside bodily dependence. Understand both the proposed distinction and the philosophical claim made from it; naming a thought 'pure' alone does not establish its independence from the body.

The next passage addresses an objection: if a person must prepare their soul, might they simply create the content they then call a revelation? Steiner answers that passivity does not guarantee objectivity. He distinguishes the claimed active independence of this path from mediumistic or visionary states which, in his account, increase dependence on bodily processes. The important reading task is to reconstruct that answer and ask how a genuine finding would be distinguished from self-produced content.

Pure thought is already called a supersensible activity, but Steiner explicitly says that it does not yet perceive other supersensible contents. The activity and the further contents are different. He requires the wider development to continue the clear consciousness achieved in thinking. Dropping below that clarity is described as the opposite of the intended path, rather than evidence of a deeper revelation.

The appendix also addresses language. The distance between a supersensible experience and the words describing it is said to be greater than in descriptions of sensory events. Images and symbolic writing are therefore illustrative. Learning external signs does not, by itself, constitute the spiritual reading described earlier; the signs are intended to express an experience that the learner can consciously understand. Figurative wording communicates what the author regards as a reality, rather than turning that reality into an arbitrary fiction.

The book is then offered as a conversation between author and reader. Its written instruction can replace essential oral teaching that had once been reserved for a narrower circle. Someone may still need assistance, but the appendix rejects the idea that cardinal points have been left out for a teacher to disclose separately. Read this alongside the opening language about masters and the Guardians' references to teachers.

The apparent demand to transform the whole person receives another qualification. Steiner describes a special inner condition for moments of supersensible knowledge, consciously distinguished from the healthy ordinary activity that continues alongside it. The proposed development should preserve interest, competence and productivity in daily life. It involves the whole person as an organ of knowing, yet does not require being a spiritual investigator throughout the day.

The final page returns to lotus flowers and the etheric body. Their descriptions are meant to locate the perceiver within the claimed supersensible world, just as awareness of one's physical body locates a person within sensory experience. The reader must therefore distinguish physical anatomy, illustrative vocabulary and the supersensible organization the author believes he is describing. This closing explanation joins self-knowledge with conscious perception.

Words for the reading

  • Pure thought — self-sustaining thinking considered apart from sensory and bodily contents in the author's argument.
  • Illustrative language — words or images used to indicate a reality that they do not depict literally.
  • Self-consciousness — awareness of oneself within an experience.

See the idea in an example

A student can explain a geometrical relation with clear reasoning, but has not thereby perceived a spiritual being. Another student mistakes the disappearance of clear reasoning for proof that a deeper world has been reached.

What this helps explain

The first case illustrates the distinction between an activity of thinking and a further content of perception. The second conflicts with the appendix's insistence that clarity be preserved. Neither case proves the author's account of supersensible thought.

Check your understanding

What distinction does the appendix make about pure thinking?

Reveal the explanation

The appendix calls pure thinking a supersensible activity but distinguishes it from further supersensible perception. The proposed path must preserve clear consciousness.

Original open question and answer

Does experiencing pure thought already mean perceiving other supersensible beings?

Show a suggested answer

No. The appendix explicitly distinguishes the activity of pure thought from perceiving further supersensible contents.

Does the book dismiss every need for individual help?

Show a suggested answer

No. It permits assistance, while arguing that essential points are available in a complete reading of the book itself.

Why retain the lotus and etheric-body descriptions?

Show a suggested answer

Steiner says the learner needs awareness of their own supersensible form to stand self-conscious within the world he describes. The descriptions serve that claim, rather than ordinary anatomical instruction.
