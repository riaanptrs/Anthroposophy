What this passage means

The opening principle makes the descriptions that follow an explanation of effects on body, soul and spirit. Read the colour footnote, the distinct organs, the eight functions and the six attributes as parts of that explanation. The chapter contrasts conscious development with mediumship and warns that incomplete or distorted development can mislead. Memorizing the organ names alone omits the purpose of the chapter.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

Understand the idea

The chapter opens with full consciousness of action: Steiner says a student should understand the proposed effects on body, soul and spirit before attempting a practice. He contrasts exact clairvoyance with mediumship. The following descriptions are offered as an explanation of results, not a replacement for the preceding character work. Their object is the soul organism or astral body, which the author says is visible only through spiritual perception.

Thoughts and desires are described as forms and radiances in this organism. The footnote on colour is crucial: spiritual red names an experience comparable to the physical experience of red, not simply an ordinary coloured image. Wheels, chakrams and lotus flowers are likewise comparative names, as calling lungs wings need not imply literal wings. Steiner nevertheless attributes supersensible reality to these organs; figurative description does not make his account merely decorative.

Different organs have different proposed perceptions. The sixteen-petalled lotus near the larynx discloses thoughts and laws through forms; the twelve-petalled lotus near the heart adds soul warmth and cold, including growth and decay. The ten-petalled lotus near the pit of the stomach concerns capacities of souls and the workings of nature. The six-petalled lotus in the centre of the body is later connected with beings of the soul world. These locations orient the account without identifying physical anatomical structures.

For the sixteen-petalled lotus, Steiner says eight petals arose in an earlier, dim state of human consciousness, while eight are to be developed consciously now. He names eight functions: (1) acquiring ideas that faithfully reflect the world; (2) forming well-considered resolutions; (3) meaningful, measured speech; (4) action attentive to other people and circumstances; (5) orderly management of life and health; (6) endeavour matched to abilities and worthy aims; (7) learning from experience; and (8) periodic self-examination of knowledge, duties and purpose. The formerly developed petals are said to reappear through this conscious work.

Truthfulness must appear in deeds, not merely good intentions, and these functions must gradually become habitual. The book connects premature judgments and excessive talk about spiritual experiences with distorted development. Its news example recommends suspending an opinion until reliable grounds are available; a footnote compares the functions with the Buddha’s path while distinguishing that comparison from teaching Buddhism. The author’s warning concerns both errors of spiritual perception and instability in ordinary life.

The twelve-petalled lotus is associated with six attributes: (1) control of thought, meaning a coherent sequence; (2) control of action, meaning consistency between deeds; (3) perseverance toward a goal still regarded as right; (4) tolerance through understanding other beings and situations; (5) impartiality or faith, meaning openness to testing and correcting one’s existing opinions; and (6) equanimity through joy and sorrow. Six earlier petals are said to reappear as these six develop. The eight functions and six attributes are related but distinct lists.

For the ten-petalled lotus, the central issue is unrecognized influence from sense impressions. A traveller’s sudden memory may have been prompted by a passing face, and an apparent presentiment may follow an unnoticed newspaper report. Steiner uses these examples to explain how unconscious associations can be mistaken for independent knowledge. He proposes deliberate attention and investigation of the origin of thoughts; the ordinary explanation of a mistaken presentiment should remain distinct from the additional claim that such discipline develops a spiritual organ.

The six-petalled lotus requires harmony of body, soul and spirit. Duty should be performed out of freely developed love rather than under an inner slave-driver. The chapter explicitly says deprivation is of no value when it harms the body, and describes wise renunciation of an unattainable ideal as better than forcing attainment under unsuitable conditions. This qualification belongs alongside its demanding account of purification and mastery.

Distorted or premature development is said to bring illusion and confusion, not reliable knowledge. Even coordinated lotus flowers are insufficient on their own: the student must regulate their movement consciously and develop further organs associated with the etheric body and the inner word. This transition explains the next lesson. The eight functions, six attributes and four organ descriptions here form an argument about coordinated development, not a collection of isolated shortcuts.

A further distinction from According to Luke

Lecture 1 of According to Luke helps clarify why spiritual colour language is not a chart for judging people. Steiner distinguishes imaginative perception from inspired understanding and intuitive participation. He uses a plant’s colours to introduce his account of spiritual images, but an ordinary imagined colour is not thereby a spiritual perception. In your notebook, distinguish “I observed”, “I pictured” and “I inferred”. This is a reading exercise, not a method for identifying another person’s inner qualities.

Practise colour description before extending its meaning

Colour, Lecture 2 (7 May 1921), proposes a yellow centre radiating outward, a blue boundary gathering inward and a more evenly distributed red. These arrangements offer an ordinary artistic exercise in describing transitions. Lecture 6 then uses light in a spiritual sense. Keep those contexts distinct: making a wash can refine attention, but does not by itself establish spiritual perception or the development of an organ.
