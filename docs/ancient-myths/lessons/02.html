What this passage means

The lecture’s direction is a renewed form of knowing, not simply a return to ancient imagery. Its discussion of language and abstraction asks what was lost and how it might be transformed.

The focus of this lesson

Distinguish an illustration of abstraction from a claim about humanity’s history.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

Understand the reading

In Lecture II Steiner relates mythical images, changes in language and writing, and abstract thought to his account of changing consciousness. He interprets the loss of an older Imaginative life as both a loss of connection and a condition for greater independence. He also discusses puberty, reproduction and nationalism within this framework. These are broader claims than our card example can test. The task is to follow the relationship he proposes, then distinguish its premises from the experience of comparing image and word.

See the idea in an example

Imagine a leaf drawn on a card, the written word “leaf,” and the general concept of a leaf. Each can direct attention differently. Does comparing them prove that all societies pass through the same mental stages?

The drawing presents selected visible features. The word depends on a learned convention and can refer beyond one drawing. A concept lets us ask what different leaves share. The comparison illuminates different ways of relating to an object. It does not reconstruct the actual history of writing or tell us that people using images cannot think abstractly. Keep those limits visible when moving from an exercise to a historical interpretation.
