What Is Biodynamic Agriculture?

Agricultural question

What makes biodynamics an agricultural approach rather than a collection of preparations?

Imagine being shown a compost heap, a cow horn and a planting calendar. The objects do not yet explain how a farmer understands the place or why those particular practices belong together. To understand biodynamics, begin with its organizing agricultural questions: how life develops, how fertility circulates, and how a farm relates to its surroundings.

What Steiner is proposing

Here, biodynamic agriculture means the agricultural approach developed from Steiner's 1924 indications and their later interpretation and practice. The word points to life and active processes or forces; that is an orientation, not a definition of every procedure. In Appendix C, Ehrenfried E. Pfeiffer attributes the name Biodynamic Agricultural Method to the Experimental Circle, rather than to Steiner. This is a later historical account, not wording from a 1924 lecture. The name and preparation labels should not be projected back as though they were the titles and vocabulary of every Koberwitz lecture. GA 327 identifies the collected Agriculture Course material, not a contemporary farming standard.

Steiner's opening move is relational. In Lecture 1, he argues that a beet cannot be understood adequately by listing its constituents: soil, season and surroundings matter. He uses the compass needle, whose direction requires reference to the Earth, as an analogy for looking beyond an isolated object. He then extends the agricultural surroundings to the cosmos. The analogy explains his reasoning; it does not demonstrate that every proposed planetary influence exists.

The approach has several connected elements. Lecture 2 organizes soil, crops, livestock and manure around a farm individuality. Later lectures address manuring, preparations, weeds, pests, plant disease and animal feeding. The preparations are intended to act within this account of farm life. Learning only their materials or numbers would leave unanswered why Steiner introduces them and what relations he thinks they influence. Roderick Shouldice's 1993 foreword accordingly presents the course as a foundation for the later method, while describing an audience of experienced farmers.

Agricultural example

In an invented mixed farm, a gardener notices that the vegetable plots, fodder fields and livestock yard are managed as separate jobs. Mapping the links changes the questions: Which crops feed animals? Where does their manure go? What returns to the vegetable soil? This makes the whole-farm approach intelligible before any preparation is discussed. A later choice of preparation would need its own source explanation and evidence; the map alone supplies neither.

Anthroposophical interpretation

Within Steiner's account, cosmic means more than ordinary sunlight or weather. He proposes differentiated influences from the Sun, Moon and planets, mediated through substances and living processes. Etheric and astral are further terms for life-forming activity and sensitivity in this spiritual model. They will be explained where the agricultural processes require them. These terms are not alternative names for already measured soil properties.

Modern agricultural context

Biodynamic and organic farming share concerns such as soil care, organic matter and fertility management. The specific inheritance from GA 327 includes an anthroposophical interpretation, preparations and attention to rhythms. Shared practices do not show that every organic farmer accepts that interpretation, or that those practices originated exclusively with biodynamics. Today's labels and standards also have a later history; they cannot be read directly off the 1924 lectures. When comparing farms, ask which practices and outcomes are actually being compared.

What this does and does not establish

Distinguish the original teaching, later practical development and modern evaluation. A practice can be historically derived from Steiner without its proposed mechanism having been demonstrated. Likewise, observing a useful compost or soil outcome does not identify which part of a whole management system caused it.

What does the compass analogy establish in this lesson?

Reveal the explanation

The compass provides an analogy for relational explanation. Extending that reasoning to particular planetary mechanisms adds claims that require their own assessment.

Explain biodynamics to a beginner without beginning with a list of preparations.

Pause and answer in one sentence, aloud or in your notes.

See a way to approach it

Start with a farm understood through its soil, crops, animals and fertility relationships. Then identify Steiner's additional spiritual interpretation and the later practical development from his course. Explain that preparations belong within this system rather than defining the entire approach.

Observe

Use an accessible place or an existing farm plan. On three visits or dated records, trace one relationship between two activities, such as harvested plants becoming compost or fodder becoming part of animal feeding.

  • The two activities and the material or action linking them
  • What you can directly observe or verify in a record
  • An unanswered question about the relationship
  • Any anthroposophical interpretation in a separate paragraph

Then interpret: A documented connection can support a description of the farming system. It does not turn an additional spiritual interpretation into an observed fact.
