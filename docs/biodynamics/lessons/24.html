Biodynamic Agriculture · Part VI of VI · Lesson 24 of 24

Biodynamics as a Whole

Agricultural question

How do soil, plants, animals, landscape, fertility, rhythms and human work form one understandable agricultural relationship?

A whole account explains why changing one relationship matters elsewhere. Feed supply affects animals and manure; soil conditions affect plant growth; trees alter the setting of a field; harvesting moves products away. Steiner’s model adds a spiritual interpretation of these connections. At the end of the course, the task is to reconstruct that relational account in a particular place and make its different claims legible.

What Steiner is proposing

The starting point is a place whose soil, water, warmth and surroundings participate in growth. Steiner pictures soil as an organ and proposes earthly–cosmic mediation through substances. Distant-planet activity received below through silica and conducted upward by clay differs from the nearer relationships drawn downward through lime. These paths cross the ground boundary. Plant forms, reproduction and nourishment are interpreted through their interplay; cosmic does not simply mean “above the soil.” His material account also treats substances as carriers of formative relationships.

Plants then enter a reciprocal farm relationship. Their growth supplies food and feed; animal activity transforms feed into manure; manure and plant-residue compost provide distinct return routes. Woods, shrubs, wet meadows and different animals widen the landscape account. In Steiner’s model, above-ground astrality and below-ground etheric regulation have different roles. The cycle reconnects roots and subsequent growth, while exported products require replenishment. Internal support is an aim qualified by economic conditions, not complete isolation or a universal herd formula.

Preparations enter this continuing fertility work. The two horn treatments are proposed to act differently through winter manure and summer mineral exposure; the six manure/compost ingredients have differentiated roles. Their familiar numbers are later labels. Horsetail belongs to the water-mediated plant-disease account, with a different status. Weeds, animal damage and abnormal plant development are distinct problems, and rhythms serve particular processes. The system becomes intelligible through these connections, without turning every difficulty into one diagnosis or every timing indication into a universal calendar.

The June 11 address to the Experimental Circle makes practical individuality and active cooperation central. Steiner compares the indications to letters that must be brought together, asks farmers for knowledge from their own places, and emphasises persevering work. Later practice, certification and research are further developments with their own sources. A current standard states requirements; a measured comparison asks about outcomes; the historical model explains its proposed relationships. Keeping these layers identifiable lets a whole portrait retain both meaning and uncertainty.

Agricultural example

The fictional Riverside Farm cannot be understood from its preparation list alone. A dry season changes hay supply and introduces purchased feed; animal manure still returns toward soil, while vegetable sales carry products outward. The orchard shades one edge and supports observed animal life. The grower records these relationships before interpreting them through Steiner’s model. This makes the farm individuality concrete: dependencies and limitations remain visible. It does not calculate spiritual forces from a diagram or make the holding a sealed system.

Anthroposophical interpretation

Etheric life names the formative living activity proposed across soil and plants. Astral activity connects sensitivity and animal organization with the landscape account. Ego-potential at roots, attributed to manure in Lecture 8, is distinct from the internally developed human ego. Earthly and cosmic processes describe different proposed mediations, not a division into visible and invisible locations. These terms belong together in Steiner’s explanation; observed organisms, sunlight or soil measures alone do not identify them.

Modern agricultural context

FiBL’s DOK account teaches the value of prolonged, specified comparisons and the limits of attributing a package’s outcomes to one preparation. The 2026 Demeter standard teaches the separate question of present compliance. NRCS defines soil health through functions and assessment, and notes that carbon-stock changes usually need several years of records. The final portrait therefore documents a place over time and asks sharper questions. Its three visits build observation and interpretation; they do not become a long-term carbon study.

What this does and does not establish

A whole model can be coherently reconstructed without claiming every proposed cause is established. A portrait should mark missing inputs, unidentified organisms and uncertain destinations. Historical source understanding, later certification and research each contribute different information; none should silently supply what another layer has not established.

Portrait of a Living Place

Choose an accessible place you can revisit, or a documented place with dated records. Create a portrait of relationships over time, using the same viewpoint and a clear boundary. The aim is to understand how conditions and activities connect, while distinguishing observation, reported information and Anthroposophical interpretation. A place without livestock can still have animals, organic inputs and outside suppliers worth recording.

  • Draw a base map of soil areas, water routes, plants, animals or habitats, organic matter, buildings and people’s activities. Identify what lies beyond the boundary.
  • Add sun, shade and wind as conditions at each visit, stating the time and what was actually observed rather than inventing a complete daily or seasonal record.
  • Return on at least three dated occasions. Record changes in soil surface, moisture, growth, flowering, animals seen, work and material movement. Keep comparable viewpoints and distinguish a reported fact from direct observation.
  • Trace organic-return paths, including manure or compost only where present or documented. Show outside inputs and outgoing products; do not force every path into a closed circle.
  • Make an observation layer and a separate Anthroposophical interpretation layer. Link the latter to specific course sources and explain its proposed relationship without treating it as a measured property.
  • List unknown materials, species, quantities, destinations and management details. An absence of observation is not proof that an organism or process is absent.
  • Finish with one question about an outcome and one about a possible cause. State what additional comparison, method or time would be needed; three visits cannot establish long-term soil-carbon change.
An original worked example

This longitudinal model is entirely fictional. A learner observes the garden-and-orchard edge of Riverside Farm in a temperate Northern Hemisphere setting from the same public viewpoint. The base map includes a low damp corner, vegetable beds, a hedgerow, orchard trees, a pasture visible beyond the garden and a compost area. Boundaries, water movement, organic returns and people’s work are recorded, but several quantities and destinations remain unknown.

12 April 2027 — spring: Under an overcast sky, the observer sees wet soil, water standing near the path, orchard buds and cattle in the neighbouring pasture. A light wind moves the hedge. The farm description reports purchased hay, but gives no quantity; this is labelled reported information, not directly observed intake. The map marks that outside input and the documented manure return. There is no inference that a puddle measures soil life or establishes its cause.

12 July 2027 — summer: At the same visit time, the open beds receive sunlight while the orchard casts shade across one edge. A breeze comes from the west, beans are flowering and insects and birds are visible. A work record says people added mulch and changed irrigation. One bed looks greener, but weather, watering and cover changed together. The observation layer records the appearance and work; the cause column remains open. No preparation-specific effect is inferred.

12 October 2027 — autumn: The observer records fallen leaves, lower sunlight at the visit time, harvested beds and unidentified fungi in the damp corner. The garden record sends some plant residues to compost, while manure has a separately documented return route; quantities are missing. Harvest baskets leave as outputs and a purchased compost bag has an unknown supplier. Fewer birds are seen on this visit, which does not establish absence from the place. The map now has several return paths and outward and inward arrows.

The separate interpretation layer connects the root–soil field with Steiner’s etheric account, the orchard and animal relationships with Lecture 7, and manure with Lecture 8’s attributed root ego-potential. It distinguishes that potentiality from the human ego and avoids claiming that the plants are conscious. Seasonal change invites a source-linked rhythm question, not a diagnosis from the calendar. The conclusion is a portrait of relationships and unresolved questions: no carbon samples were taken, the three visits establish no carbon-stock increase, and the spiritual explanation remains an attributed interpretation.
