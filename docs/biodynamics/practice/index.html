Farm observation

Compare two soils

Choose two accessible soil surfaces and record the date, recent weather, moisture, visible aggregates, roots and organisms. Use comparable viewpoints or documented samples. Describe what you see before explaining it; appearance alone does not give organic-matter content. Revisit after a change in weather or management.

Steiner’s original indication
Lecture 2 treats soil as an organ within a living agricultural individuality and attributes formative activity to its relationships. This explanation goes beyond visible structure.
Later practice
Contemporary assessments choose physical, chemical and biological indicators for a particular question. Their methods and sampling conditions must be recorded separately from the historical interpretation.
Modern evidence
NRCS soil-health guidance supports functional assessment and appropriate sampling. A visual comparison does not measure etheric activity, establish carbon stocks or identify the cause of a difference.

Follow plant development

Follow one identified plant or a clearly marked patch. Make dated sketches of leaves, stems, flowers and seeds as they appear. Record whether roots were actually visible or remain unknown. Note light, moisture and human work; keep a description of form separate from a proposed explanation.

Steiner’s original indication
Lectures 1–3 relate plant development, reproduction and nourishment to differently mediated earthly and cosmic processes. The account is more specific than assigning every root to earth and every flower to cosmos.
Later practice
Later observation journals can track growth stages and conditions. A calendar category is an additional interpretive system and needs its own attribution.
Modern evidence
Repeated records can establish a sequence of visible development. They do not isolate a planetary cause when weather, variety, soil and management also vary.

Map the farm individuality

Draw a boundary and locate crops, water, animals, hedges, woods, compost and human activities. Add incoming feed, fertiliser or organic material and outgoing products. Use a different mark for observed flows, reported flows and unknown quantities. Revisit the map as the place changes.

Steiner’s original indication
Lecture 2 proposes an agricultural individuality with reciprocal internal support; Lecture 8 explicitly qualifies self-support under economic conditions. It is an organizing ideal rather than an unconditional ban on inputs.
Later practice
The Demeter 2026 standard has present requirements for fertility, livestock or documented cooperation within specified scopes. These institutional arrangements are later developments.
Modern evidence
A flow map makes dependencies visible. Without quantities, boundaries and a defined accounting period, it is not a nutrient budget or evidence of ecological self-sufficiency.

Observe animals and landscape

Record animals actually seen, their activity and the habitat used. Map trees, shrubs, wet areas and cultivated ground. Use dated observations rather than an assumed species inventory. Add documented feed and manure routes where available; mark animals or destinations that could not be identified.

Steiner’s original indication
Lecture 7 differentiates trees, shrubs, damp areas and animal groups within attributed etheric and astral relationships. It does not give one universal stocking formula for every place.
Later practice
Later habitat planning and livestock arrangements require local information. Present certification provisions should be read in their actual scope rather than inferred from the lecture.
Modern evidence
DOK biodiversity summaries concern defined groups, plots and sampling periods. Visible animals in one visit neither measure astrality nor establish the biodiversity of an entire landscape.

Fertility

Trace the manure return

Use a farm record to trace feed origin, animal group, manure handling and destination. Distinguish direct return to soil from a compost route where it occurs. Record storage and application dates if documented. Leave missing quantities and undocumented handling visible.

Steiner’s original indication
Lectures 4 and 8 connect manure with continued fertility and attribute living formative activity and root ego-potential to it. That potential is distinguished from a human ego.
Later practice
Current standards describe permitted fertility inputs and preparation treatment. The Demeter 2026 requirements and exceptions are separate from Steiner’s words and from any recipe inferred from them.
Modern evidence
NRCS assesses biological, physical and chemical soil functions. A manure record documents material management; it does not measure root ego-potential or isolate the cause of a crop response.

Follow the compost cycle

Map plant residues entering compost and any manure that actually follows that route. Keep direct manure-to-soil returns separate. From existing records, note feedstocks, turning, moisture observations, dates and destination. Follow the two return paths in the lesson diagram rather than forcing all organic material through compost.

Steiner’s original indication
Lecture 4 differentiates manure and plant-residue compost; Lecture 5 retains ordinary fertility work while proposing enhancements. Compost belongs to the farm relationship but is not a compulsory intermediate for every manure return.
Later practice
Later compost practice and current preparation-treatment requirements need their own sources. Demeter section 6.2 states mandatory measures; Appendix 8 supplies recommendations, not an identical set of obligations.
Modern evidence
FiBL reports differently processed manure among DOK systems. A whole-system result therefore cannot be read as a preparation-only result without an appropriate matched comparison.

Preparations

Horn manure — 500

Read an existing preparation record alongside Lesson 13. Identify the material, enclosure, season, destination and claimed role. Mark which details come from the 1924 account and which belong to a later protocol. This is a provenance exercise, not an instruction to make or apply a preparation.

Steiner’s original indication
Lecture 4 describes cow manure in a cow horn exposed below ground through winter, then a stirred water preparation directed toward land. Steiner attributes concentration of etheric and astral activity to the horn and a relation to soil and root processes.
Later practice
500 is a later designation. Present preparation use falls under Demeter 2026 section 6.2 with its scope and exemptions; Appendix 8 recommendations are separately identified. Current operating protocols require their own verification.
Modern evidence
DOK includes 500/501 even in NOFERT. That treatment is not a preparation-free control; the reviewed whole-system summaries do not isolate the effect of 500 or verify its proposed spiritual mechanism.

Horn silica — 501

Compare a documented silica preparation with the horn-manure record. Use the lesson table to distinguish material, seasonal exposure, application destination and intended function. Do not turn their contrasting roles into a rule that more of one automatically replaces the other.

Steiner’s original indication
Lecture 4 describes finely ground quartz in a horn through summer and allows orthoclase/feldspar as an alternative in the passage. The plant-facing treatment complements horn manure within his account of growth and formative relationships.
Later practice
501 and standardised application conventions are later developments. Current certification requirements are not a reason to attribute every modern dose or regional schedule to the 1924 lecture.
Modern evidence
The reviewed FiBL summaries compare specified management systems with several differences. They do not establish a universal 501 response or identify cosmic mediation as the cause of an observed result.

Compost preparations — 502–507

Use Lesson 15’s six-row table to annotate a written preparation list. Add botanical material, enclosure or absence of enclosure, exposure, manure/compost destination and attributed role. An ingredient name alone does not document the historical method. Keep unresolved translation and identification details visible.

Steiner’s original indication
Lecture 5 differentiates yarrow, chamomile, nettle, oak bark, dandelion and valerian within continued manuring. Their forms and claimed roles differ; nettle has no animal enclosure, and valerian is an extract rather than another buried enclosure.
Later practice
502–507 are later numbers. The current Demeter standard has manure/compost treatment requirements and approved alternative routes; its recommended production guidance does not make every historical detail mandatory.
Modern evidence
Compost handling and feedstocks are important co-varying features in a system comparison. The reviewed evidence does not isolate six ingredient effects or establish Steiner’s exceptional material-transformation claim.

Horsetail and the later 508 label

Locate horsetail in the preparation map and explain why it sits in a different group. In an existing record, separate the plant identification, historical indication, later name and any current treatment claim. Connect the entry to the disease lesson rather than assuming another compost additive.

Steiner’s original indication
Lecture 6 places horsetail tea in the account of excessive lunar activity mediated by water and abnormal plant growth. It has a different role from the six manure/compost ingredients.
Later practice
Gardner’s editorial note at canonical capture 184 says the tea is sometimes called preparation 508. That is separately attributed editorial evidence for later usage, not a number spoken by Steiner in 1924 or a universal treatment requirement.
Modern evidence
A symptom observation does not establish its cause or the efficacy of a treatment. The reviewed modern sources supply no isolated horsetail-efficacy result; diagnosis and an appropriate comparison remain separate questions.

Farm challenges

Record spontaneous plants

Map one patch and record date, plant identity if known, growth stage, ground cover and recent disturbance. Describe abundance in the same area at later visits. Keep an unidentified plant unidentified and document management changes rather than calling every spontaneous plant a problem.

Steiner’s original indication
Lecture 6 proposes a seed-ash indication to work against reproductive activity within the earthly–cosmic model. Steiner’s explanation and confidence are historical claims, not established efficacy.
Later practice
Later management choices require plant identification, local conditions and their own practical sources. This library provides observation and source study, not an operational ash-treatment protocol.
Modern evidence
Changing cover across visits can be recorded, but disturbance, moisture, seed supply and other care can change together. A repeated count alone cannot isolate a treatment or celestial cause.

Distinguish animals from damage

Record the crop, damage location, date and organisms actually observed. Sketch the affected and unaffected areas. Mark whether the organism was seen causing the damage or merely nearby. Use existing management records; this exercise does not involve killing animals or making ash preparations.

Steiner’s original indication
Lecture 6 gives different accounts for mammals and invertebrate pests, including animal-derived ash indications and particular timing assumptions. The organism-specific distinctions belong to the historical explanation.
Later practice
Present pest management needs appropriate identification and lawful, humane methods. Later biodynamic descriptions require separate provenance; they do not turn the lecture into a verified contemporary protocol.
Modern evidence
An animal’s presence does not identify a damage cause, and less visible damage after changed management does not isolate one treatment. None of the reviewed modern summaries verifies these historical pest methods.

Describe plant symptoms

Make a dated record of affected plant parts, visible changes, wetness, weather and surrounding conditions. Record the same plant area again, noting any documented work. Separate a visible symptom, an identified organism and a proposed diagnosis. Ask a qualified local adviser for a practical diagnosis when needed.

Steiner’s original indication
Lecture 6 interprets certain abnormal growth through excessive water-mediated lunar activity and a figurative second ground-level. This is a particular account, not the claim that every disease has one cause.
Later practice
Plant pathology and current field management use different questions and diagnostic methods. A later treatment claim must be sourced separately from the 1924 explanation.
Modern evidence
Symptoms and wet conditions can be observed together without showing that one proposed mechanism caused the other. The course’s reviewed evidence does not supply a pathogen diagnosis for an observed plant.

Rhythms

Keep a seasonal observation journal

Revisit a fixed viewpoint and record date, time, daylight, shade, weather, soil surface and plant stage. Add documented watering, sowing or harvesting. If you record a calendar category, name its source and keep it in a separate interpretation column. Three entries begin a journal; they do not establish a timing effect.

Steiner’s original indication
GA 327 connects particular seasons, lunar relationships and planetary conditions with particular agricultural processes. The indications differ by context and do not constitute every contemporary planting-calendar rule.
Later practice
Vreede’s provisional guidance, Gardner’s editorial discussion and later calendars have separate attribution. Record which system is being used instead of treating all timing guidance as Steiner’s own words.
Modern evidence
Seasonal records reveal change but weather, development and management also change with time. A timing-effect claim needs a defined comparison and repeated measurements; a dated diary does not demonstrate planetary causation.
