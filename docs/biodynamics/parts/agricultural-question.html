The Agricultural Question

Why did agricultural difficulties lead Steiner to consider the relationships of an entire farm?

Source lectures: GA 327 Lecture 1, 7 June 1924; Lecture 2, 10 June 1924; report to Society members, 20 June 1924. Later context: Roderick Shouldice's 1993 foreword.

What you will learn

  • Explain who requested the 1924 course, what agricultural concerns motivated it, and why its indications were intended for practical trials.
  • Distinguish Steiner's original agricultural model, later biodynamic development, and questions that contemporary research can examine.
  • Explain farm individuality through soil, plants, animals and manure while retaining Steiner's qualification about self-sufficiency.

Lessons

  1. Why Was the Agriculture Course Given?

    What were the farmers asking Steiner to help them understand and improve?

  3. The Farm as an Individuality

    What does Steiner mean by a farm approaching a self-contained individuality?

Part synthesis

The course begins with a practical difficulty: farmers want their land, crops and animals to thrive. Steiner proposes that the answer requires relationships rather than isolated treatments. His later report connects the indications to farm information and trials; Lecture 2 gives the relationships an organizing centre in the farm individuality.

The fertility circuit makes that centre concrete. Soil supports crops; crops feed animals; animals provide manure that returns to the land. Steiner gives this circuit an additional spiritual interpretation and an ideal of internal support. Neither the visible circuit nor the later biodynamic name establishes every mechanism he proposes.

Why does manure help explain farm individuality, and why would observing a manure cycle leave some of Steiner's claims untested?

Show a model answer

Manure joins activities that can otherwise look separate. A farm's plants provide fodder; its animals transform that fodder; their manure returns material to the soil supporting later crops. For Steiner, an appropriate animal complement helps the farm approach internal self-support, although he explicitly says this ideal cannot be fully implemented.

Records can establish where fodder comes from, how much manure is available, and where it goes. Those observations do not by themselves establish his further claim that particular cosmic qualities pass through plant, animal and manure in a way especially suited to that farm. Testing outcomes and assessing the proposed explanation are different tasks.
