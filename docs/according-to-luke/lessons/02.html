Bodhisattva, Buddha and the teaching of love and compassion

By the end of this lesson: Explain Steiner’s transition from spiritual teaching to a human capacity, and his connection with the shepherds in Luke.

In Steiner’s account, a Bodhisattva is a spiritual teacher preparing a particular human capacity. Buddha names the stage at which that mission is fully embodied in a human life. Here a capacity means an ability people can develop and exercise. These are the terms as this lecture uses them.

Steiner’s sequence is: teaching love and compassion, embodying that mission in Buddha, then developing the capacity independently. Receiving a rule is different from understanding how to act through it. The proposed gift does not make everyone compassionate automatically; people still have to develop and exercise it.

Steiner then identifies the heavenly image seen by Luke’s shepherds with Buddha’s continuing spiritual activity. He claims to learn this through the Akashic record, an enduring spiritual record of past events. Luke 2:8–14 names the angel and heavenly host without naming Buddha. This connection is Steiner’s spiritual interpretation, rather than established historical or scientific knowledge.

A volunteer helps by filling in a newcomer’s form without asking. Another asks what is difficult and offers to work together. Thoughtful help depends on the person’s needs: if reading is difficult and they ask you to write their answers, direct assistance may be appropriate. This original example illustrates independent judgment; it cannot establish Steiner’s spiritual account.

What this passage means

Steiner’s account describes a spiritual teacher bringing capacities into human development. The transition from Bodhisattva to Buddha explains why love and compassion can become humanly understood capacities, preparing the connection with Luke.

The reading’s key idea

From the Bodhisattva’s teaching to the shepherds in Luke

Terms for this argument

Bodhisattva: a spiritual teacher preparing a particular human capacity in this lecture. Buddha: the stage at which that mission is fully embodied in a human life. Faculty: a capacity that people must develop and exercise. Akashic record: the enduring spiritual record that Steiner claims to investigate. These definitions describe this lecture’s usage, rather than all Buddhist traditions.

Steiner follows the same individuality through the earlier teaching role and its completion. A teaching is what someone communicates; a faculty is an ability a person can develop and exercise. Keeping these distinct makes it possible to follow the transition he proposes.

What the transition changes

In Steiner’s account, the Bodhisattva teaches love and compassion before human beings can understand them independently. Becoming Buddha means fully embodying that mission in a human life. The gift is then available for people to develop through their own judgment and action. Steiner does not present the change as making everyone compassionate automatically.

In the bodhi-tree account, Steiner links the change with the teachings of compassion and love becoming independent human faculties. He also says human beings must continue developing them. The proposed availability of a capacity therefore differs from its mature exercise by every person. His account of humanity’s spiritual development needs its own assessment; a useful learning analogy does not verify it.

Creeger 2001 EPUB, Lecture 2: mission and embodiment, then the bodhi-tree discussion; verified entry index_split_009.html, near anchors page0049–page0050 and page0060.

Why the argument returns to the shepherds

Steiner then says Buddha no longer needs a physical incarnation and continues influencing earthly events from the spiritual world. He identifies the image seen by the shepherds with that transfigured being and relates its influence to the infant Jesus. This supplies the connection left open in the earlier excerpt-based lesson.

Read Luke 2:8–14 separately: its angel and heavenly host are the narrative being interpreted. The identification with Buddha comes from Steiner’s claimed Akashic research. Reconstructing his interpretation neither attributes that name to Luke’s wording nor establishes the spiritual account as historical or scientific fact.

Creeger 2001 EPUB, Lecture 2: concluding interpretation of the heavenly host and the child; verified entry index_split_009.html, near anchor page0063. Compare Luke 2:8–14.

Use the analogy with care

A volunteer follows the rule ‘Always help’ by completing a newcomer’s form without asking. Another volunteer asks what is difficult and offers to work through it together. Now change the situation: the newcomer says reading the form is difficult and asks the volunteer to fill it in while they explain the answers.

Understanding the purpose of help can change the action. Working together may support learning; completing the form with the person’s agreement may be the appropriate help in the changed case. The point is thoughtful care for this person, rather than one fixed technique. This everyday analogy helps explain an independently exercised capacity; it supplies no evidence for Steiner’s account of Buddha’s spiritual activity.
