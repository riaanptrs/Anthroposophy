What this passage means

Steiner’s account describes a spiritual teacher bringing capacities into human development. The transition from Bodhisattva to Buddha explains why love and compassion can become humanly understood capacities, preparing the connection with Luke.

The focus of this lesson

Explain the relation between a spiritual teacher and a human capacity in Steiner’s account.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

The reading’s key idea

The selected passage describes Buddha’s earlier Bodhisattva role as a teacher of love and compassion. The lecture’s existing source summary places this teaching within an account of love and compassion becoming a capacity human beings can understand from within. Keep the teacher’s role, what is taught and the capacity being developed distinct.

Steiner’s connection between Buddha and Luke belongs to his spiritual interpretation of the Gospel. The retained selection supplies one part of that argument: the earlier teaching role. The fuller transition and the interpretation of the shepherds’ announcement need their surrounding lecture text. That text is absent from the supplied PDF captures of the 2001 edition, so this lesson offers a close reading of the available passage and a guide to the questions still open.

Reading Buddha’s earlier teaching role

Follow the word “previously”

The retained sentence makes Buddha its subject and places the Bodhisattva designation earlier in his history. It describes that earlier figure as a teacher. Read these words together: Steiner is following the same teacher through an earlier designation, rather than introducing an unrelated teacher. The sentence establishes a temporal relationship; the account of how that transition occurs belongs to the surrounding lecture.

Use the terms in their local context first. Bodhisattva names the earlier teaching role assigned to Buddha here. This short passage does not define every use of either term across Buddhist traditions or across Steiner’s work.

Retained German selection, Lecture 2, § 24; English and Portuguese study translations.

Preserve love and compassion together

The teacher’s subjects are love and compassion, together with everything connected with them. Both named subjects matter. Reducing the sentence to “be helpful” loses the stated breadth of the teaching. At the same time, the selected sentence does not list the connected contents, so a close reading should leave their particulars open.

First mark who teaches, when this role is placed and what is taught. A paraphrase should retain those three relationships. This provides a textual basis for discussing the spiritual teacher before considering an everyday application.

Retained German selection, Lecture 2, § 24: earlier designation, teaching function and subjects.

Distinguish a teaching from an understood capacity

The course’s earlier source explanation situates this teaching within Steiner’s account of human development: the Bodhisattva-to-Buddha transition is described as enabling love and compassion to become humanly understood capacities. That is the earlier explanation of the broader argument; it is not a second statement quoted from the retained sentence.

A person can receive an instruction without yet understanding how to act through it. The volunteers’ example illustrates this difference: repeating a general rule to help someone differs from understanding what help this person needs. Use the example after reconstructing the source relationship. Its educational value does not establish Steiner’s spiritual history.

Words for the reading

Bodhisattva: the Buddha’s earlier role as a spiritual teacher in this selection. Buddha: the figure whose earlier role the sentence describes; the full lecture is needed for the account of the transition. Teaching: communication of an ideal or understanding. Capacity: an ability that a person can exercise. Compassion: the concern for another’s suffering named in the source, considered alongside love.

Read → understand → explain → apply. The chapter explanation is open from the start.

See the idea in an example

A volunteer repeats, “Always help.” When a newcomer struggles with a form, she completes it without asking. Another volunteer asks which part is difficult and offers to work through it together. The second response may support the newcomer’s independence; the first may remove a chance to learn.

The first volunteer acts on a general instruction; the second tries to understand how help could support this person. The example clarifies the distinction between receiving an instruction and exercising an understood capacity. It illustrates a relation in learning; it does not reproduce the Bodhisattva-to-Buddha transition described by Steiner.
