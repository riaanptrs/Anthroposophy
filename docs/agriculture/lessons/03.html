Course explanation

Understand the idea

A farm approaches its own character, Steiner says, when it produces what it needs from within its relationships. He explicitly calls complete self-containment unattainable: it is an ideal to approach. In that ideal, imported fertilizers are compared with remedies for a sick farm. The metaphor expresses his preferred organization of fertility; it does not make every actual imported input a diagnosis of disease.

The relation is reciprocal. Plants provide fodder; animals transform it and return manure; manure supports the ground on which future plants grow. He proposes that appropriate numbers and kinds of livestock should provide the suitable amount and mixture of manure for the land. He expressly welcomes testing this statement. He supplies no universal stocking number that could replace a farm’s particular conditions.

His picture of soil as a diaphragm places the farm’s “head” below ground and its “belly” above, with plants growing into this belly. He says the human comparison is not exact but helpful for illustration. Its teaching purpose is to make exchange across the soil surface visible, not to turn the farm into a literal human body.

Animal form completes the account. Steiner interprets the front and rear of an animal through direct solar and reflected lunar influences, with the heart as a boundary and other planetary activities on either side. The broader practical point is that livestock belong within the individuality’s relationships: their food, bodily processes and manure cannot be treated as unrelated items.

Key concept

Farm individuality names a reciprocal organization of land, plants and animals, guided by an ideal that is approached rather than perfectly attained.

Course example

Draw a farm’s actual flow of fodder, livestock, manure and crops. Mark both internal flows and purchased or exported materials. Ask where one activity depends on another. This is a learning map of relationships; it does not calculate an appropriate stocking rate by itself.

Keep this distinction

Self-containment is an organizing ideal, not absolute isolation or a guarantee of sufficiency. Modern feed budgets, nutrient balances and animal care provide concrete information that the ideal alone does not supply.
