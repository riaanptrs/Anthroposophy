Partial fulfillment is still particular

Notice what a comparison of satisfaction can and cannot show.

Optional close reading · excerpt included
1 · Read the passage

Read the supporting excerpt

Rudolf Steiner · The Philosophy of Freedom · GA 4 · Chapter 13, The Value of Life (Optimism and Pessimism) · PDF p. 141, column 1

Moral ideals spring from the moral imagination of man. Their realization depends on his desire for them being intense enough to overcome pain and misery.

Michael Wilson translation, Rudolf Steiner Press, 2012, eighth English edition. Line breaks and line-wrap hyphenation normalized.

Open the source or edition index →

What this passage means

The ideal is wanted, rather than a merely external demand. Read this together with the closing qualification and 1918 addition: intensity alone does not make an impulse ethical or free; intuitive ethical content must sustain willing.

The focus of this lesson

Notice what a comparison of satisfaction can and cannot show.

See the idea in an example

Two books need simple protective covers. You finish one. Another person planned to cover four and also finishes one. The same number completed stands in a different relation to each plan.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

Understand the idea

Relative to the stated targets, one of two is one half and one of four is one quarter. This makes the relation between an aim and its fulfillment visible. It does not measure the worth of either person or the full quality of either experience.

The unfinished work can matter without making the completed cover worthless. Satisfaction and disappointment can coexist. Counting outcomes is useful only when we remember what the count represents and what it leaves out.

Suppose the next book needs expert conservation. Stopping and seeking help can serve the original aim better than pressing on. Persistence is not automatically admirable, and a higher completion count may conceal damage.

Nor does an aim's fulfillment establish its ethical character. A wish can be fulfilled and still be harmful or moved by vanity. The earlier questions about ethical content, motive and realization remain necessary.

Keep this idea

Use a simple proportion to understand partial fulfillment, not to turn human experience or freedom into a score.

Optional: Brian Gray’s commentary
Study with the lecture

Partial fulfillment matters; unwanted excess changes the experience

Brian Gray · Chapter 13 (00:00:14–00:21:32). Course synthesis comparing the lecture with Steiner’s book.

The lecturer's hunger examples make two sides of the book's comparison concrete. A small meal can give real satisfaction while leaving part of a need unmet. More food after appetite has ended may become unwelcome rather than add enjoyment. First identify the actual desire and what meets it; an abstract quantity labeled pleasure can conceal these differences (PDF 135–137).

The books-written analogy and the food examples must not become a productivity score for a person's life. The exact PDF 136 fraction uses experienced pleasure and total needs, and its purpose is to illustrate their relation. If the concrete aim is nourishment, pleasant entertainment does not supply it. Where you genuinely accept either of two activities simply for amusement, comparing their enjoyment may instead be appropriate (PDF 140).

Apply the same care to an ethical project. A partial repair can matter, a new fact can justify changing the method, and additional difficulty need not improve the result. The lecturer emphasizes persistence, but the book allows hindrance to weaken desire and distinguishes ethical intuition from other determinants of willing (PDF 138, 142–143). Keep fulfillment, obstacle, practical revision and the reason for acting in separate parts of your account.

Work through an example

You want a filling lunch and receive only a small bowl of soup. It provides some nourishment without meeting the whole need; a pleasant game would not supply the remainder. After a sufficient lunch, another large serving may be unwanted. Likewise, stabilizing a damaged book's cover can be useful even if the binding requires later expert work; adding more amateur repair is not necessarily an improvement.

Check the distinction: Does incomplete fulfillment make the satisfaction already experienced worthless?

Show an explained answer

No. The comparison allows partial satisfaction to retain value while acknowledging the unmet need. State what was met and what remains; this assesses a concrete fulfillment rather than calculating anyone's human worth.

Optional source comparison question
Why might a pleasant substitute fail to satisfy a particular desire?

Reveal the explanation

A wish to repair a book or obtain food has a particular object. Amusement does not supply that result. Some alternatives are interchangeable, but that relation must be established.

Check your understanding

Does one half mean that the first person has twice the human worth?

Show a suggested answer

No. The proportion compares completion with a stated target. It says nothing about human worth and does not capture an entire experience.

Can disappointment coexist with a worthwhile result?

Show a suggested answer

Yes. An aim can be partially fulfilled. What remains undone does not erase what was achieved.

Does completing every planned cover prove free action?

Show a suggested answer

No. Completion concerns realization. The ethical content and the action’s driving force remain separate questions.

Go deeper: the chapter’s argument

This detailed explanation extends the first reading. The additional questions are optional.

You set aside an afternoon to repair a friend's book. The task takes longer than expected. A film would be more immediately pleasant, but leaves the book unrepaired. Ask whether these alternatives are interchangeable for your actual aim. Then ask what you should do if your attempted repair might damage it further.

Start with two different comparisons. A balance puts pleasures and pains into opposing columns. A satisfaction-to-desire relation asks how far a particular experienced fulfillment meets the need or aim in question. The chapter's fraction on PDF 136 uses experienced pleasure as numerator and total needs as denominator. It illustrates partial fulfillment and a fixed satisfaction meeting a larger or smaller demand. It is not a validated numerical scoring system for a person's life.

In the bread illustration, one piece meets half of a two-piece need, but only a quarter of a four-piece need. The satisfaction supplied is unchanged; the proportion of the particular need met changes. A pleasant walk supplies neither missing piece, so equal enjoyment would not make it interchangeable with food. The numbers clarify a relation, not a measure of human worth or a nutritional prescription. In the repair example, a film likewise cannot substitute for the object being repaired unless the actual aim was merely some amusement.

Next compare desire with hindrance. The book argues that a concrete aim may remain wanted despite considerable effort or pain. It also allows hindrance to overwhelm and weaken desire. Its mountain-view and apples examples are illustrations of that relation. They do not show that every obstacle should be endured. Asking an expert or changing a harmful repair method can serve the original aim better than continuing blindly. Persistence alone does not prove wise judgment.

Now reconnect with ethical content. Satisfaction in preserving a valued object can accompany an understood reason for helping; enjoyment does not automatically turn that reason into selfishness. Yet admiration may be the real aim, and strong desire can pursue something unethical. The ending and 1918 addition restrict the freedom claim to willing sustained by ideal intuition. Distinguish the pleasure of the outcome, the intensity of wanting it, and the ethical content that you claim determines the deed.

Write an assessment that lets partial success count without making productivity a person's worth. You may have stabilized the cover, learned that the binding needs specialist work, and stopped before causing damage. State what was achieved relative to the concrete aim and what remains unresolved. This follows the chapter's attention to an individual's aims while leaving its broader philosophical claims open to examination. The project helps clarify categories; it does not calculate the value of a human being.

Words for the reading

Partial fulfillment — some satisfaction with demand still unmet; interchangeability — alternatives adequately meeting the same aim; hindrance — a difficulty affecting the pursuit of an aim; determining ethical content — the understood ideal that sustains an act in Steiner's freedom account.

Value a concrete fulfillment in relation to its aim, and examine obstacles without confusing persistence with freedom.

More questions to explore

Is a pleasant substitute always enough?

Show a suggested answer

No. A specific desire may require its particular object: amusement does not repair a book or supply food. Some alternatives are interchangeable, but that must be established.

Does partial fulfillment become worthless because the full aim was not reached?

Show a suggested answer

No. The ratio illustration allows partial satisfaction to retain value. This is different from assigning a value to the person.

Does changing the plan refute ethical commitment?

Show a suggested answer

No. New facts may show a better way to serve the aim. Persistence, intensity and freedom must be judged separately.

Make two small maps for the repair: pleasure/pain, then concrete desire/partial fulfillment/obstacle.
