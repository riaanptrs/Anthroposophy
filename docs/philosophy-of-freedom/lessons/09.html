What this passage means

Wilson's mental picture means representation, including a nonvisual recollection. The preceding page explains how a concept retains reference to one perceptual encounter. Use the bicycle example to distinguish the general concept, its particularized reference and the two kinds of recognition. The chapter then adds the individual significance of feeling.

The focus of this lesson

Distinguish a shared concept from a memory of one particular thing.

See the idea in an example

Outside a shop stand three blue bicycles. You know that all three are bicycles. To find yours, you look for its scratched basket. Recognizing a kind and recognizing this particular bicycle are different achievements.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

Understand the idea

The concept “bicycle” helps you understand what you encounter. Your previous encounter with your own bicycle gives that concept a particular reference. Steiner calls the resulting individualized concept a representation.

A representation need not be a vivid picture in the mind. You might remember the scratch without picturing every spoke. What matters here is its connection with an individual experience.

Feeling adds another relation: this bicycle may matter to you because a friend repaired it. That personal significance is real, but it does not make the other bicycles less fully bicycles. Shared understanding and individual experience belong together.

Experience, in this account, is not simply the number of places visited. It grows when what we encounter becomes intelligible through concepts. A familiar street can teach something new when we understand a relation we previously missed.

Keep this idea

A concept lets you recognize a kind; a representation connects understanding with a particular encounter; feeling relates that encounter to your own life.

Optional: Brian Gray’s commentary
Study with the lecture

Understanding something and caring about it

Brian Gray · Chapter 6 (00:00:05–00:23:59). Course synthesis comparing the lecture with Steiner’s book.

The lecturer's discussion of a biography and skilled work makes individualized concepts concrete. Experience grows when particular encounters become understood and retain their references. A general concept can apply to many cases; your representation carries the reference to a particular one. That individual stamp comes partly from your position and history. It does not make shared conceptual content an arbitrary private truth, and representation need not take the form of a vivid visual image.

The discussion of memorable teachers adds feeling to understanding. Enthusiasm, concern for learners and a love of the subject make a personally significant relation visible. Steiner distinguishes this from the individual stamp of representations: particular organization also shapes feelings and their intensity. Two learners can understand one explanation and value it differently. Emotional force alone does not establish its truth; feeling is itself content that can be understood in relation to a concept.

The lecturer recommends artistic activity as a way of educating feeling together with thought. Steiner's closing ideal is the integration of knowledge and developed feeling, rather than a choice between detached concepts and private emotion. Attentive listening, making and discussing can offer practice in that integration. This is the lecturer's practical application, not a book claim that art is the only possible means. The aim is to connect personal significance with a relation you can explain.

Work through an example

A music teacher asks Jo and Rafa to identify the repeating four-beat pattern in a short piece. Both can count it and explain where it repeats. Jo recalls a family celebration and feels warmth; Rafa recalls a difficult first lesson and feels tension. The teacher invites each to listen again and describe what changed. Their memories and feelings give different personal significance to a relation both understand; neither response changes the number of beats.

Check the distinction: How can the learners share understanding while the experience remains individual?

Show an explained answer

The repeating relation can be understood by both. Each representation retains references shaped by particular encounters and history; each feeling expresses the encounter's significance for its bearer, with its own intensity. Steiner's two sources of individual coloring therefore remain distinct. Discussing the piece can relate those feelings to what is understood, without turning pleasure into proof or requiring identical emotional responses.

Go deeper: the chapter’s argument

This detailed explanation extends the first reading. The additional questions are optional.

You understand what a bicycle is. One afternoon you see a bicycle with a blue basket beside a gate, and later you recall that encounter. The general concept can apply to many bicycles. Your representation retains its reference to this particular encounter. The next morning you must distinguish recognizing another bicycle of the same kind from recognizing that very bicycle again.

The chapter begins with the relation between self and world. Steiner rejects the assumption that the skin is an absolute barrier through which information from otherwise disconnected objects must enter. Subject and tree belong to the same world process. As a knower, rather than a creator of the world, a person finds their conceptual connection through thinking. This is the philosophical premise that supports the subsequent account of representation.

The physiological objection concerns different sensations from a disturbance applied to different organs. Such observations establish relationships among a disturbance, an organ and a sensation; they do not by themselves establish that the external process disappears without that organ. The example of rotating horse pictures likewise relates successive images to perceived movement. These are arguments about what follows from observation, not exercises requiring learners to apply pressure or electricity to their senses.

When a percept is encountered, its fitting concept acquires a reference to that particular percept. After the encounter disappears, this individualized concept remains as a representation, called a mental picture in Wilson's translation. It need not be an inner photograph. Steiner's lion example distinguishes explaining a general concept to someone who has never met a lion from supplying the vivid reference of a personal perceptual encounter.

Recognition has two forms. A new bicycle may connect with the same general concept as the first, identifying its kind. Recognizing the earlier bicycle again involves the concept individualized by the earlier encounter. A similar blue basket is not necessarily enough to establish identity: the retained reference must be applied to the new evidence. The difference concerns the judgment being made, even when both judgments employ the word bicycle.

Experience requires perception and conceptual apprehension together. Steiner contrasts an unthinking traveler, who encounters many things without understanding their relations, with an abstract scholar whose concepts lack vivid contact with particular things. Neither supplies the whole process. The sum of individualized concepts gives experience its richness; more encounters alone and more detached definitions alone do not guarantee that richness.

Feeling adds another relation. Through pleasure and displeasure we relate the encounter to ourselves as individuals. Two students can understand the same explanation while one feels relief and the other frustration. Thinking connects us with the broader world; feeling gives that relation personal value. Steiner therefore distinguishes the significance a feeling has for its bearer from its conceptual place within an account of the world.

There are two sources of individual coloring in this discussion. A person's position and history determine which percepts become linked with concepts and hence which representations they acquire. Their particular organization also determines the feelings and degrees of intensity accompanying those percepts. These distinctions explain individuality without turning a shared conceptual relation into an arbitrary private truth.

The closing ideal brings knowing and feeling together. Detached universality can lose the concrete person, while a life of feeling without thinking can lose its connection with the world. Steiner describes a developed individuality whose feelings reach into the region of ideas, giving concepts concrete life. Understanding a subject, caring about it and responding to another person can therefore develop together; the argument does not require choosing between impersonal knowledge and personal feeling.

Words for the reading

Representation or mental picture: a concept retaining reference to a particular percept; individualized concept: the same relation expressed in conceptual terms; experience: the connected range of particular encounters and concepts; feeling: the individual relation expressed in pleasure or displeasure; recognition: identifying either a kind or the same object again.

Representation retains a particular encounter; feeling gives it personal significance, and developed individuality connects both with understanding.

More questions to explore

What makes a concept individualized in a representation?

Show a suggested answer

It retains its reference to a particular perceptual encounter. The term does not mean that a general concept becomes an arbitrary private opinion.

How do the two kinds of recognition differ?

Show a suggested answer

One identifies a new object as belonging to an already understood kind; the other reconnects a new encounter with the individualized concept of the same previously encountered object.

Why does the chapter include feeling as well as knowledge?

Show a suggested answer

Feeling gives the relation to the world individual significance. Steiner's closing ideal educates feeling together with knowing rather than treating either alone as the whole person.
