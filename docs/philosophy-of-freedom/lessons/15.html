An aim is present before its result

Distinguish an intended purpose from an effect that happens afterward.

What this passage means

The represented later deed influences present action through a mental picture already present. Steiner is not proposing backward physical causation. Compare this with the chapter's account of a lawful root-and-blossom connection.

The focus of this lesson

Distinguish an intended purpose from an effect that happens afterward.

See the idea in an example

A librarian rearranges a display so that children can reach the books. The improved access is still in the future, but an idea of it is already present and guides today's work.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

Understand the idea

The future event does not reach backward to move the shelves. The present representation of the intended result helps cause the action. This is the relation Steiner examines when discussing purposeful human activity.

Now imagine that two visitors meet because they stop at the new display. Their conversation is an effect of the arrangement, but it was not necessarily the librarian's purpose. Later usefulness does not prove earlier intention.

A plan can also fail: the shelf may still be too high. That failure does not erase the purpose that guided the work. Aim, action and result must be distinguished.

When roots develop before flowers, the sequence and its intelligible relations do not by themselves establish that a future flower was represented as a goal by a planner. Steiner challenges the automatic transfer of human purpose to nature. This does not deny natural order. His additional claims about spiritual reality are philosophical teachings, not findings established by this example.

Keep this idea

To identify a purpose, look for an intended result guiding activity. A useful outcome or an orderly sequence alone does not establish one.

Optional: Brian Gray’s commentary
Study with the lecture

A represented result guides action now

Brian Gray · Chapter 11 (00:00:14–00:12:07). Course synthesis comparing the lecture with Steiner’s book.

The lecturer's example of studying to become a teacher clarifies an apparent reversal: the desired later result influences what the student does earlier. No future classroom travels backward in time. A representation of teaching exists now and helps determine present decisions. This is the detour through a mental picture that the book requires for purpose (PDF 115).

Keep four items distinct: an intended result, its present representation, the means chosen and the actual outcome. Failure to achieve the result does not mean the intention never guided the action. Conversely, an unintended benefit does not become your original purpose simply because it occurred. A mental picture here can be a concrete understanding of an arrangement; it need not be a vivid inner image.

The lecture contrasts a self-set purpose with a preassigned mission. The book also distinguishes natural lawfulness from purpose in this strict sense, and its 1918 addition rejects reducing everything to material mechanism (PDF 116–118). The lecturer's claims about divine intentions are commentary. For the learner, the task is to explain how an aim becomes one's reason, without assuming that choosing a goal already proves an ethical motive.

Work through an example

Rafael wants neighbors to find the food pantry's opening hours without making an extra trip. Before printing anything, he represents a readable notice at the entrance and asks about languages and access. He installs it, but some visitors still arrive late. The notice's intended role, the present plan, its production and its actual effect are four different parts of the account.

Check the distinction: If the notice fails to reach everyone, was the activity purposeless?

Show an explained answer

No. The represented result may genuinely have guided the activity even though the outcome was incomplete. Examine what Rafael intended and how that intention affected his choices; then assess whether the means worked.

Go deeper: the chapter’s argument

This detailed explanation extends the first reading. The additional questions are optional.

You plan a small library display. Before moving the shelves, you imagine readers finding the books more easily. That possible result influences what you do now. Nearby, a plant forms a bud. Both events can be described through connected stages, but have you established the same kind of purpose in each?

Steiner defines purpose as a particular relation: the idea of an intended later effect helps determine the earlier action. The future display does not physically reach backward in time. Your present mental picture of it guides your present arrangement. This detour through a represented outcome distinguishes purpose from a sequence in which an earlier event causes a later one. A purpose need not be fulfilled to have influenced an action, and an accidental outcome is not automatically the purpose you had.

Cause and effect are also understood through thinking. Observing two events gives appearances that must be connected by the appropriate concepts if we are to understand their relation. But finding a conceptual connection does not show that the idea of the effect operated as the cause. In the root-and-blossom example, the blossom has not yet appeared when the root develops. Calling the blossom the root's purpose therefore requires more than knowing their lawful relation. State what would count as the represented aim and whose aim it would be.

The chapter criticizes the projection of human toolmaking onto nature. We construct a machine by arranging parts according to an idea of their intended operation. A natural organism's lawful organization does not, on that account alone, reveal a designer who first imagined it. Steiner locates the organism's intelligible connection within its own reality rather than adding a plan imposed from outside. In contemporary conversation, function can have broader meanings. Mark the strict sense of purpose used here so that a change of vocabulary does not conceal a disagreement.

Hamerling's counterargument appeals to the coherent whole: a limb belongs to a body, and natural forms belong to an organized totality. Steiner accepts lawful connection but disputes calling that connection purpose in the same sense as a human plan. The distinction is between a law apprehended in explaining a phenomenon and an agent's concept functioning as a reason for producing an intended effect. Coherence is not disorder; rejecting a designer's purpose does not require denying natural regularity. Explain both sides before judging whether the proposed boundary is adequate.

Monism extends this criticism to a predetermined human mission. In its account, my life receives purposes from the aims I set and take up, rather than fixed marching orders issued by an unknowable being. A statement that history moves toward freedom describes or interprets a sequence; it does not by itself establish that history is an agent executing an intended plan. Individuals can act together toward a shared aim. Its formation and consequences still need explanation through their actual participation.

The 1918 addition prevents a misleading inference. The author says that rejecting purpose outside human action does not reduce thinking to material mechanism: he presents thinking as spiritual. He also says that the spiritual world contains something higher than human purpose and that humanity's combined activity yields more than its individual aims. These are claims within his philosophical account. The absence of a demonstrated cosmic plan neither proves a materialist universe nor supplies evidence for every claimed spiritual event.

In the display example, easier access is the intended outcome; the present representation of that outcome guides arranging the shelves; bodily work moves them; readers’ later use shows what actually resulted. If nobody finds the books more easily, the purpose was present but unfulfilled. If an unplanned conversation begins beside the display, it is an effect without necessarily having been the purpose. A lawful sequence, an intended aim and an actual result are therefore distinguishable even within one project.

Words for the reading

Purpose — an intended result represented as a reason for action; cause — what brings about an effect; lawfulness — an intelligible connection among phenomena; mental picture — an individualized representation relating an idea to a possible or experienced event.

A represented aim guides an agent; lawful connection alone does not establish such an aim.

More questions to explore

Does the future result physically act backward on the doer?

Show a suggested answer

No. A present mental picture of the intended result guides present action. The later result may differ from what was intended.

Does rejecting a purpose in nature mean rejecting its lawful organization?

Show a suggested answer

No. Steiner distinguishes intelligible law within a phenomenon from an agent's represented goal operating as a reason to produce it.

Does the 1918 addition simply turn the book into materialism?

Show a suggested answer

No. Steiner presents thinking as spiritual and says the spiritual world exceeds the human category of purpose. That statement is part of his account, not independent verification of later spiritual claims.
