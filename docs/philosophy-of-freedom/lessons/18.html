Why pleasure is not the whole question

Distinguish a balance of pleasant feelings from the satisfaction of a particular aim.

Optional close reading · excerpt included
1 · Read the passage

Read the supporting excerpt

Rudolf Steiner · The Philosophy of Freedom · GA 4 · Chapter 13, The Value of Life (Optimism and Pessimism) · PDF p. 141, column 1

Moral ideals spring from the moral imagination of man. Their realization depends on his desire for them being intense enough to overcome pain and misery.

Michael Wilson translation, Rudolf Steiner Press, 2012, eighth English edition. Line breaks and line-wrap hyphenation normalized.

Open the source or edition index →

What this passage means

The ideal is wanted, rather than a merely external demand. Read this together with the closing qualification and 1918 addition: intensity alone does not make an impulse ethical or free; intuitive ethical content must sustain willing.

The focus of this lesson

Distinguish a balance of pleasant feelings from the satisfaction of a particular aim.

See the idea in an example

You want to repair a much-used library book. Watching an enjoyable film would be easier, but it would not fulfill that particular wish. The alternatives cannot be understood only by adding up pleasant and unpleasant feelings.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

Understand the idea

Steiner examines theories that judge life's value by a balance of pleasure and pain. He asks us also to notice willing: a person pursues a particular content, and its fulfillment matters as fulfillment of that desire. A different pleasure is not always a substitute.

The repair may be tiring yet satisfying. That does not make suffering good in itself, or mean that you should continue despite damage or danger. You can change the plan, seek skilled help or stop. The relation between a desire and its obstacles needs attention to the actual situation.

Enjoying a helpful action also does not automatically make it selfish. Pleasure may accompany fulfillment rather than be the action's sole aim. Conversely, a very strong desire is not thereby ethical or free. This chapter's analysis of satisfaction does not replace the earlier account of ethical intuition.

Suppose you enjoyed applause, then learned it was meant for someone else. The belief that prompted the pleasure was mistaken; the pleasure you experienced still occurred. Correcting a judgment about its object is different from pretending the feeling never existed.

Keep this idea

Ask what is being sought and how it is fulfilled, as well as how it feels. Neither pleasure alone nor persistence through difficulty determines the value or freedom of an action.

Study with the lecture

Striving, fulfillment and an ethical reason are different

Brian Gray · Chapter 13 (00:00:14–00:21:32). Course synthesis comparing the lecture with Steiner’s book.

The lecturer usefully separates enjoying the pursuit of an aim from enjoying its achievement. Someone may value preparing a contribution even before it is complete; disappointment at the outcome does not prove every earlier moment was painful. The book also includes pleasure and pain that occur without prior wanting, such as an unexpected gift or illness (PDF 129–130). Keep all these experiences visible instead of reducing life to fulfilled versus frustrated desire.

A particular goal may remain wanted despite difficulty, which differs from seeking the largest quantity of interchangeable amusement. The lecturer's books-written example helps illustrate partial fulfillment, but the book's fraction actually relates experienced pleasure to total needs (PDF 136). Neither formulation measures a person's dignity. More effort does not automatically make a project better, and intense wanting does not establish ethical content; the 1918 addition requires willing sustained by intuitive thinking (PDF 143).

One lecture claim needs correction from the primary text. It presents Hartmann as urging individual suicide; Steiner's account explicitly says that individual suicide would hinder Hartmann's proposed collective redemption (PDF 134). Keep this active pessimism distinct from Schopenhauer's renunciation of will. The lecture's personal anecdotes and spiritual explanations of Hartmann's suffering are not established here. Understanding the philosophical disagreement does not require accepting those claims.

Work through an example

Marina prepares a local-history booklet. She enjoys comparing photographs, finishes several useful pages and discovers that two captions need correction. A frustrating delay and enjoyment of the work can both be real. Preserving accurate memories may be her understood reason; seeking praise may also influence her. Identify the different experiences and examine the motive without converting page totals into her personal worth.

Check the distinction: If Marina enjoys producing the booklet, does that make the act selfish?

Show an explained answer

No. Enjoyment can accompany an understood ethical aim. The relevant distinction is whether that aim determines the act or whether the project chiefly serves approval or another motive. Pleasure, persistence and output do not establish the answer by themselves.

Detailed study 29 / 36

Optimism and pessimism are arguments to examine

Reading by PDF capture: Wilson 2012, 173-capture file, 130–145; Amrine 2022, 84-capture abridgment, 49–51. Numbers identify file positions, not printed pages.

1 Steiner introduces opposing evaluations of life. Optimism regards the world as good or as yielding a favorable balance; pessimism emphasizes suffering and the failure of desire. These are positions the chapter examines, not simple labels for cheerful and unhappy personalities. Personal temperament does not settle their philosophical arguments.

2 Schopenhauer's proposed release from striving differs from Hartmann's account of participating in a cosmic process of redemption. Keep the positions distinct before evaluating Steiner's replies. In particular, Hartmann's world-purpose is an opponent's premise, not a task Steiner assigns to the student. The book's earlier rejection of imposed cosmic purpose remains relevant.

3 The chapter questions whether a theoretical calculation of pleasure and pain can dictate what a person must value. Feelings themselves must enter the account as experienced facts. Its historical argument concerning suicide is not reliable evidence about an individual's wellbeing or risk; reconstructing that argument does not make it a tool for judging another person's life.

Make it concrete

A reader calls the chapter “positive thinking.” That misses its work. It distinguishes theories of value, tests proposed ways of counting pleasure and asks what actually moves willing. The conclusion is not that painful circumstances should be ignored.

Test the distinction

Why should Hartmann's cosmic redemption not appear as the course's advice?

Read an explained answer

It is a position presented for criticism. Confusing an opponent's claim with Steiner's conclusion reverses the argument. Identifying the speaker is essential, especially where an abridgment shortens the historical discussion.

Work with the idea

Write one sentence each for Schopenhauer, Hartmann and Steiner's question to them. Do not merge their views.

Detailed study 30 / 36

Pleasure experienced and pleasure remembered

Reading by PDF capture: Wilson 2012, 173-capture file, 130–145; Amrine 2022, 84-capture abridgment, 49–51. Numbers identify file positions, not printed pages.

1 A retrospective account can exaggerate joy or minimize pain. Steiner accepts that memory and disposition can distort an attempted balance. Correcting that distortion means trying to describe the actual experiences more faithfully. It does not authorize deleting a real feeling merely because a later judgment disapproves of its object.

2 Suppose someone enjoyed public praise and later discovered that the praise was insincere. The belief about the admirers was mistaken, but the pleasure occurred. Removing it from a numerical pleasure account would quietly change the criterion from quantity of experienced feeling to the worth of its object. Both questions can matter, but they are not the same calculation.

3 The chapter thus reconnects ethics with the earlier account of knowing: conceptual judgments need perceptual content, and feeling can be encountered content. A theory cannot simply announce a surplus that contradicts the experiences it claims to total. At the same time, this critique does not make ordinary pleasure estimates perfectly accurate or comparable across people.

Make it concrete

Someone fondly remembers a competition while forgetting its anxiety. That calls for correction of memory. If they also enjoyed an undeserved compliment, discovering the error does not erase the earlier enjoyment. The two corrections concern different issues.

Test the distinction

What is wrong with subtracting every pleasure based on an illusion from a supposedly quantitative total?

Read an explained answer

It excludes feelings that actually occurred because their objects are judged defective. This introduces a qualitative standard into an account advertised as measuring experienced quantities. The standard needs separate justification.

Work with the idea

Invent two cases: inaccurate memory of a feeling and accurate memory of pleasure based on a false belief. Explain the difference.

Detailed study 31 / 36

Desire, satisfaction and the value of a chosen aim

Reading by PDF capture: Wilson 2012, 173-capture file, 130–145; Amrine 2022, 84-capture abridgment, 49–51. Numbers identify file positions, not printed pages.

1 Steiner shifts from a global balance of pleasures and pains to the relation between a desire and its satisfaction. Wanting food, completing a task and helping a friend have particular contents. The question is not only how much pleasant sensation exists in the abstract, but whether the desired content is realized and what costs the person accepts for it.

2 His ratio is experienced pleasure divided by the total demand of the relevant desire. In the idealized comparison, 1 means full satisfaction and a positive fraction below 1 means partial fulfillment. Imagine four equally desired portions and the enjoyment of only three: the illustrative ratio is 3/4, not zero. The remaining need matters, but it does not erase the enjoyment that occurred. This is different from subtracting pain from pleasure to calculate a whole-life balance. Above-demand enjoyment can initially increase the ratio, but unwanted excess can become displeasure. These are conceptual comparisons, not calibrated measurements or a universal wellbeing score. The kind, duration and intensity of desire also matter.

3 An individual can value a deed strongly enough to accept effort or pain in carrying it out. Enjoying the realization of an ethical aim does not automatically make the aim selfish. The 1918 addition also prevents equating all willing with freedom: the free case still concerns a motive rooted in intuition, rather than the mere persistence of a desire.

Make it concrete

A musician rehearses through frustration because making the piece audible matters to them. The difficulties do not vanish, but neither does the chosen aim reduce to a search for the easiest amusement. A moral example similarly requires examining the deed's content, not just whether its maker feels satisfaction.

Test the distinction

Does satisfaction in helping prove that the real motive was personal pleasure?

Read an explained answer

No. Pleasure can accompany realizing an aim without being the aim that directed the deed. To claim selfish motivation, one needs evidence about what moved the person, rather than the bare fact that fulfillment felt good.

Work with the idea

Analyze an effortful project: desired content, satisfaction, cost and motive. Explain why no single pleasure total settles all four.

Go deeper: the chapter’s argument

This detailed explanation extends the first reading. The additional questions are optional.

You want to repair a friend's damaged book. Watching a film would also be enjoyable, but it would not fulfill this particular aim. The repair requires patience and may need expert help. Ask why the wanted result matters, what satisfaction belongs to it, and what difficulty would justify adapting the plan. A chosen project is an example for examining the argument, not a measure of anyone's worth.

The chapter first presents competing philosophies. Leibniz's optimism connects the best possible world with a good and wise God and invites cooperation with its order. Schopenhauer's pessimism takes blind, endlessly renewed willing as a source of suffering and seeks its cessation. Hartmann instead joins will with wisdom: created suffering serves the eventual redemption of a suffering primal being, so people should actively contribute to that cosmic task. These are the positions as Steiner presents them. Hartmann's active pessimism must not be confused with Schopenhauer's proposed withdrawal from willing.

Steiner first challenges the equation of striving with pain. Anticipation of a remote aim can bring enjoyment before success; fulfillment adds another enjoyment. Frustration is a further experience, not a proof that all wanting was painful. He also distinguishes pleasure or pain without a prior desire: an unexpected inheritance and an illness are examples. A fair inventory must include enjoyment in striving, fulfillment and unexpected good fortune, alongside boredom, frustrated striving, unwanted work and uninvited pain. Do not reduce this list to 'fulfilled desire is pleasure' and its converse.

Hartmann proposes rational correction of our accounts. Steiner separates two operations. Biased recollection can misreport how intensely praise or rejection was felt, and that needs correction. But recognizing that applause rested on an illusion does not erase pleasure actually experienced at the time. If the question concerns quantity, all actual feelings belong in the account. Judging the object of pleasure worthless introduces a different criterion. This does not make the illusion true or the enjoyment ethical; it distinguishes experienced feeling, its remembered intensity and an assessment of its object.

An abstract calculation must answer to actual feeling, which the book treats as a percept. The merchant analogy says a calculated loss must be checked against the affairs it claims to describe. Steiner later allows approximate comparisons of pleasures and pains by intensity and duration; his decisive objection concerns what a surplus would establish about willing. The chapter also makes broad historical assertions about suicide and uses business failure as an analogy for life. Those passages help locate his argument, but do not provide a reliable psychological explanation of individual deaths. Note 3 on PDF 162 emphasizes actual experience over an imagined balance.

The argument then changes its comparison. A particular satisfaction has value in relation to the desire it meets. The hunger example distinguishes getting nourishment, enjoyment of taste and remaining unsatisfied need. On PDF 136 the proposed fraction has experienced pleasure as numerator and total needs as denominator: partial satisfaction still counts, while the same satisfaction meets a smaller proportion of a larger demand. This is an explanatory analogy, not a validated numerical measure of a life. Excess enjoyment can become displeasure when it exceeds what is wanted. Unsatisfied need and actual suffering are acknowledged, not canceled by someone else's pleasure.

A concrete desired object is not always replaceable by an equally pleasant one. A walk does not satisfy hunger; a film does not repair the book. Steiner therefore compares the force of a particular desire with hindrances to it, rather than always subtracting pain directly from pleasure. If hindrance overwhelms desire, striving may weaken or stop. His good-and-rotten-apples example illustrates valuing a wanted result despite incidental costs. His claim that hard-earned enjoyment can gain value is not a general rule that additional suffering improves a project. Changing an unsafe or unsuitable plan can express the original aim more faithfully.

The chapter allows pleasure-based choice where alternatives are genuinely interchangeable: if you only want some amusement and either game or conversation will do, their expected enjoyment can guide selection. It rejects making that pattern the whole of human willing. Even a demonstrated excess of pain would challenge a philosophy that defines life's value solely as a surplus of pleasure; it would not automatically show that every particular aim is irrational. Hartmann's attempt to create unselfishness by first proving egoistic satisfaction impossible is challenged as the abandonment of unattainable grapes, rather than the positive formation of an ethical aim.

An ethical ideal can itself become something understood and wanted. Enjoyment in realizing it does not, by itself, make the deed selfish. Repairing the book because its preservation matters differs from using the repair chiefly to obtain praise, though both may be enjoyable. Conversely, intense wanting does not make a goal ethical. The positive account is a strong will sustained by ideal intuitions, whose content gives direction to desire. Moral imagination supplies concrete expression; practical technique still matters. The author measures achievement against personally apprehended aims within this account of willing, not as a score of human dignity.

The closing qualification and 1918 addition are essential. Steiner expressly rejects invoking the argument to excuse every immature impulse or refusing every received idea merely because it came from someone else. The will can be determined by factors other than intuition. Freedom and ethical value belong, in his argument, specifically to actions sustained by individually apprehended spiritual intuitions. Return to the repair: identify the ethical reason, the concrete plan, the desired satisfaction and the real obstacles. Persistence, enjoyment and technical success each tell you something, but none alone establishes the free motive.

Words for the reading

Optimism — the philosophical claim that the world is fundamentally good; pessimism — the opposed assessment of existence through suffering; eudemonism — the account valuing life through a surplus of pleasure; value of satisfaction — its relation to a particular desire; ethical desire — wanting directed by apprehended ethical content.

A wanted ethical aim can sustain effort; pleasure, pain, persistence and freedom still require different judgments.

More questions to explore

Does the chapter deny that pleasure and pain can be compared?

Show a suggested answer

No. It allows approximate comparison and pleasure-based choice among interchangeable alternatives. It disputes treating that balance as the sole explanation of every concrete aim.

Does correcting an illusion erase the pleasure originally felt?

Show a suggested answer

No. The earlier pleasure remains an experienced fact even if the object's value is later reassessed. Misremembering its intensity is a different issue.

Does pleasure in a helpful deed prove selfishness, or strong desire prove ethical freedom?

Show a suggested answer

Neither. Joy in realizing an ethical aim differs from using a deed for reward; intensity alone does not establish ethical content. The closing condition is willing sustained by ideal intuition.

Map the book repair as aim, enjoyment of striving, possible fulfillment, obstacle and practical revision. Explain why a film may not replace it.
