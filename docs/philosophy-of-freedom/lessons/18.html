2 · Work through the explanation

A worked example

You practise a short piece of music because you want to play it for a friend. The passage is difficult, yet discovering its rhythm can be enjoyable before you master it. If you do not finish today, disappointment and pleasure in the practice can both belong to the same afternoon. Neither cancels the fact that the other was experienced.

Understand the idea

Begin by identifying whose argument you are reading. Steiner presents Leibniz’s optimistic account of the best possible world, Schopenhauer’s account of unsatisfied will, and Hartmann’s pessimistic account of a world process directed toward release from suffering. These are philosophical positions being examined, rather than descriptions of cheerful and unhappy personality types.

Keep the two pessimists distinct. In Steiner’s presentation, Schopenhauer recommends quieting the will, while Hartmann requires continued work for a supposed world purpose. At Basis 115, Steiner explicitly explains why individual suicide would hinder that purpose within Hartmann’s system. The supplied lecture’s contrary summary should not replace this passage.

Steiner first challenges the assumption that striving already counts as suffering. Anticipation and the activity of trying can themselves give pleasure; fulfillment adds another experience. His next qualification matters just as much: not every pleasure or pain begins with a desire. An unexpected gift may please us, and illness may hurt without a prior wish. So “all pain is frustrated desire” is not the chapter’s full account.

He then distinguishes a mistaken memory from a pleasure whose occasion later proves illusory. Suppose you felt pleased when you thought your performance was praised, then discovered that the message referred to someone else. The belief was mistaken, but the earlier pleasure occurred. Correcting the belief does not erase the experience; disappointment afterward is another experience. Counting a pleasure is also different from approving its cause morally.

The chapter asks that judgments about feelings answer to actual experience, while allowing that memory and assessment can be mistaken. It does not merely declare comparison impossible. We will next examine its deeper question: even if pain outweighed pleasure, would that settle what makes an aim worth pursuing? Use the music example to study the reasoning; nobody needs to disclose or score their own life.

Keep this idea

Distinguish the desire, the activity, the outcome and the feelings before judging their relationship.

Check your understanding

Does an unfinished practice session prove the whole experience was painful?

Show a suggested answer

No. Enjoyment during practice and disappointment about the result are distinct experiences. Either, both or neither may occur.

If praise was based on a misunderstanding, was the pleasure imaginary?

Show a suggested answer

The belief may have been false, but the pleasure was experienced. A later correction must not silently remove it from an account of earlier feelings.

Does Steiner present Hartmann as recommending individual suicide?

Show a suggested answer

No. At Basis 115, his account says it would hinder Hartmann’s proposed world purpose. That is a report of Hartmann’s system, not Steiner endorsing the system.

How to assess your response

Check that you explained the distinction, gave reasons and used the assigned passage. Separate the example, Steiner’s argument and your assessment. Revisit what remains unclear. Well-supported disagreement can demonstrate understanding; agreement or reports of spiritual experiences are not required.
