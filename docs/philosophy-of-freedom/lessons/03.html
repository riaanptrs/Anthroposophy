This then is indisputable, that in thinking we have got hold of one corner of the whole world process which requires our presence if anything is to happen.

Open the source or edition index →

What this passage means

The argument concerns our participation in producing conceptual activity. The calculation illustrates the difference between forming a connection and recalling its result; the optional billiard practice distinguishes that activity from causing the observed physical event. Read the 1918 objections as part of assessing the claimed access to thinking.

The focus of this lesson

Distinguish the event from the thinking that explains it.

See the idea in an example

One billiard ball rolls into another. After contact, both move. You can describe what you saw in order. To explain it, you connect motion, contact and the properties of the balls.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

Understand the idea

The explanation adds a relationship to the sequence you observed. That relationship does not push either ball. The collision happens whether or not a spectator understands it. Thinking produces an account of the event, not the event itself.

Understanding can also support a prediction. But “I saw a collision” is not enough to calculate the next positions. You need direction, speed and relevant conditions. A fitting explanation can still be applied badly if information is missing.

What we observe can also be inward. You may notice a remembered sound or a dream. Observing a dream of a horse establishes that the dream was experienced; it does not establish a horse in the room.

Keep this idea

The practice separates three things: what happened, how you connect its parts, and what you expect next. Each prediction depends on the connection and its application, not on confidence alone.

Optional: Brian Gray’s commentary
Study with the lecture

Separate the event from the connection you make

Brian Gray · Chapter 3 (00:00:16–01:33:58). Course synthesis comparing the lecture with Steiner’s book.

The lecture slows down the billiard scene by asking who produces each process. The moving balls supply an occurrence you encounter; you supply the conceptual work connecting impact, direction and motion. Constructing that explanation does not move a ball. Steiner’s opening argument in Wilson’s translation also postpones the further question of whether this thinking activity is independent or necessary.

A useful difference appears when the later movement is hidden. A spectator who has no conceptual connection must wait for another observation. Someone who understands the relevant relation can make a conditional prediction. A prediction is reasoning about what should happen under stated conditions; it is not a report of something already seen.

Keep the scope of observation broad. The lecture includes remembered scenes, feelings and dreams alongside sensory encounters. Their appearance gives you content to examine, but calling a content observed does not settle what kind of existence it has. This workshop asks what thinking adds; the core lesson then examines the activity itself after it has been performed.

Work through an example

Two toy carts run on a level track. You see the first contact the second, then both pass behind a screen. Without a model you cannot report their hidden motion. Under stated assumptions about the carts, the contact and friction, you might predict that the second moves forward. Watching it emerge checks that application. The conceptual connection came from your reasoning; it neither supplied a new observation behind the screen nor physically pushed the cart.

Check the distinction: What changes when you predict a hidden movement, and what still requires checking?

Show an explained answer

You move from following the observed sequence to applying a conceptual relation. The prediction depends on your account of the particular conditions. A further observation can check whether that account fits the event; understanding your reasoning does not guarantee that the selected model is adequate.

Does explaining the collision create it?

Show a suggested answer

No. The balls move independently of this spectator’s explanation. What the thinker contributes is an understood relationship between the events.

Why might a prediction fail even when the idea of collision is useful?

Show a suggested answer

You may lack the starting speed or misjudge the surface. Understanding a relationship and applying it to the actual conditions are different tasks.

You dream of a horse and later see a picture of one. Compare what each encounter establishes, then explain why an account of either event does not create its object.

Show a suggested answer

The dream establishes an inward experience, not a horse in the room. Seeing the picture establishes that present encounter, not a living horse depicted there. Thinking seeks connections and interpretations; its explanation does not produce the dream’s external object or physically create the encountered picture. Different observed contents require conclusions proportionate to them.

Go deeper: the chapter’s argument

This detailed explanation extends the first reading. The additional questions are optional.

Two billiard balls are initially still. One is struck, approaches the other and makes contact; afterwards both move. That is the supplied sequence. Calling the contact a collision connects these moments through a concept; predicting exactly where each ball goes requires further information about direction, speed and conditions. The sequence supports a question about causation but does not, by itself, supply those missing quantities.

Steiner distinguishes the physical occurrence from the conceptual process added by the observer. Ball, elasticity, motion, impact and velocity enter a connected account of the particular event. That account requires the thinker’s activity; the observed balls do not wait for it. Producing an explanation does not mean producing the physical event.

Before finding the relevant connection, a spectator must wait for each movement. An understood relation can make prediction possible even when the later movement is hidden. State its conditions: a suitable description of the balls, an initial direction and speed, and assumptions about the surface or collision. A new observation can reveal that your application was mistaken.

Observation in this chapter also includes inward experience: feelings, memories, dreams and even illusions can appear in experience. A dreamed horse is an observed dream-content; that does not establish a physical horse in the room. Conversely, constructing the concept of a horse does not create one. Thinking and observation have different contributions even when the observed content is inward.

The opening deliberately postpones whether thinking is independent or necessary. First establish what conceptual work adds to an encounter. Then, in lesson 4, make that work itself an object of observation. This practice supplies an event–explanation–prediction map for the fuller inquiry; it is not a substitute for the chapter’s later argument or its 1918 addition.

Words for the reading

Observation: encounter with a content of experience, including inward content. Conceptual connection: an understood relation among concepts. Prediction: a conclusion about a further occurrence under specified conditions.

Reconstruct the connection supplied by thinking, and distinguish its prediction from the event actually observed.

More questions to explore

What does thinking add to the billiard sequence?

Show a suggested answer

A conceptual connection among the observed details. It does not push the balls or create the occurrence.

Why label a prediction conditional?

Show a suggested answer

It follows from a connection as applied under specified conditions. Its accuracy depends on that application and can require another observation.

Why does observing a dreamed horse not prove a physical horse is present?

Show a suggested answer

The dream-content is experienced, but the judgment about a physical object requires a different connection and evidence.
