How can we examine thinking?

How can we examine thinking?

Start here

Calculate ((3 × 3) + 1) ÷ 2. Multiply first, add one to the product, then divide the total by two. You may write the operations down. The intermediate results are 9 and 10; the final result is 5.

Understand the idea

Now change the question. Instead of asking for the result, explain how you reached it. Which result did you carry into the next operation? What would change if the division applied only to the 1? Remembering “five” and reconstructing those connections are different activities. There is no speed requirement.

Steiner distinguishes concentrating on the problem from subsequently making one's thinking the object of fresh thinking. The first activity need not have been unconscious: you can be aware of the reasons while solving the problem. In the review, those reasons become what you investigate. A feeling of confidence is something you can notice too, but it is not the operation or its justification.

Why does Steiner give thinking this special place? He argues that we participate in producing its connections and can examine it through the same activity, thinking. This is his proposed starting point for understanding the world. Whether thinking correctly explains other things remains a further question. Explaining the argument fairly does not require accepting all its conclusions.

The 1918 addition distinguishes active thinking from thought-images that arise, and says willing belongs within the thinking described here. A remembered sentence can become material for fresh thought when you investigate its meaning and reasons. This does not mean spontaneous thoughts are bad or that a learner must silence the mind.

Keep this idea

first think through something, then examine its connections; keep understanding distinct from certainty alone.

Try it

write a three-step argument about an ordinary timetable, then cover it and reconstruct it. Compare the reconstruction with the original. Mark a missing assumption or a step that needs checking. If you only recalled the conclusion, work through the steps again.

Check your understanding

Is the answer identical to the activity?

Show a suggested answer

no; the answer is a result, while the activity involves relating operations or reasons.

Does reflection require leaving the body?

Show a suggested answer

no; this exercise changes the focus of attention.

Does confidence establish a correct judgment or a theory of the brain?

Show a suggested answer

no; examine the reasons, the application and any additional claims separately.

How to assess your response

Check that you explained the distinction, gave reasons and used the assigned passage. Separate the example, Steiner’s argument and your assessment. Revisit what remains unclear. Well-supported disagreement can demonstrate understanding; agreement or reports of spiritual experiences are not required.
