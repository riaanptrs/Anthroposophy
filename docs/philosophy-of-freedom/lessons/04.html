Thinking, then noticing how you thought

Distinguish remembering a result from reconstructing its reasons.

What this passage means

The argument concerns our participation in producing conceptual activity. The calculation illustrates the difference between forming a connection and recalling its result; the optional billiard practice distinguishes that activity from causing the observed physical event. Read the 1918 objections as part of assessing the claimed access to thinking.

The focus of this lesson

Distinguish remembering a result from reconstructing its reasons.

See the idea in an example

Work out ((3 × 3) + 1) ÷ 2. First multiply: 9. Add 1: 10. Divide by 2: 5. Now look back at why those steps belong in that order.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

Understand the idea

Someone may remember “five” without doing the calculation. Someone else can explain how the brackets organize it. Both can give the answer, but only the second account shows the relationship that produced it.

While calculating, your attention was on the problem. Afterwards, the way you thought becomes the new subject. Steiner calls this observing thinking. It is fresh thinking about an earlier activity, not trying to watch the very same act while producing it.

Why does this matter? Steiner argues that here we participate in the activity we investigate. We do not merely receive its result from outside. He takes this as a special starting point for knowledge. That is a philosophical claim to examine, not something the correct answer five proves by itself.

An idea flashing through your mind is not necessarily this active work. You might suddenly recall “five” or imagine a number. Reconstructing the operations lets you distinguish a passing thought from an understood connection.

Even clear reasoning can start from the wrong application. Dividing only the 1 by 2 is a different calculation. Knowing how your steps connect does not guarantee that you have solved the problem actually given.

Keep this idea

Observe the activity as well as the answer. The exercise makes Steiner’s proposed starting point understandable; it does not settle every objection about thinking or its relation to the body.

Study with the lecture

Brian Gray · Chapter 3 (00:00:16–01:33:58). Course synthesis comparing the lecture with Steiner’s book.

The lecture’s calculation makes a subtle difference tangible: hearing an expression or remembering its answer differs from actively connecting its operations. Follow ((3 × 3) + 1) ÷ 2 to obtain 9, then 10, then 5. Afterwards reconstruct what you did. In Steiner’s distinction, producing the connection and making that same production an object of observation are separate acts.

The lecture also distinguishes recurring thought-images from deliberate conceptual work. A remembered answer may appear without your working through it now. To investigate the thinking, return to the relation that makes an operation appropriate, not merely to the image or result left behind. This supports the 1918 addition’s distinction between arriving thought-images and actively elaborated, supervised thinking.

Clear access to a reason does not establish every conclusion about the world. A confident feeling is not the conceptual connection itself. The lecturer interprets the brain as a mirror of thinking; Chapter 3’s narrower argument is that examining a conceptual connection need not await knowledge of its physiology. The calculation illustrates participation in reasoning and supplies no proof of that brain theory.

Work through an example

Two helpers must share seedling pots equally. There are three trays with three pots each, plus one extra pot: ((3 × 3) + 1) ÷ 2 gives five pots per helper. First calculate. Then cover the answer and reconstruct why multiplication counts the trays, addition includes the extra pot and division shares the total. If the extra pot belongs to another task, the relevant expression changes. The original reasoning remains understandable, while its application needs correction.

Check the distinction: Why is remembering five insufficient, and what does the changed extra-pot assumption reveal?

Show an explained answer

Remembering five supplies the result; examining thinking requires reconstructing the relations that produced it in a later act. The changed assumption shows that transparent reasoning and correct application are different issues. The calculation fits one stated situation, and revised circumstances can require a different expression.

Detailed study 6 / 36

Seeing an event and understanding its connection

Reading by PDF capture: Wilson 2012, 173-capture file, 41–51; Amrine 2022, 84-capture abridgment, 16–19. Numbers identify file positions, not printed pages.

1 Imagine two billiard balls colliding. Observation supplies a sequence: one ball moves, contact occurs, another moves. Repeating the sequence gives more observations. Thinking introduces the conceptual relations through which the movements become intelligible, such as direction, motion and the connection between successive events.

2 Steiner distinguishes what is encountered from the activity through which a connection is understood. This does not make thinking a license to invent any cause. A proposed concept must fit the observed situation. The distinction concerns the contributions to knowing, not an opposition between good reasoning and careful observation.

3 We often attend to the object thought about and overlook our own thinking. Yet in producing a conceptual connection, we have a different access from our access to a merely given event. Steiner makes this difference the next step of the argument. It is not a claim that the first explanation that occurs to us is correct.

Make it concrete

A broken window and a nearby ball suggest a causal story. Seeing both objects is not yet observing the impact. Thinking can propose an explanation, while additional evidence may reject it. This example separates the need for conceptual connection from certainty about a particular inference.

Test the distinction

Why does “I observed the sequence” not yet explain the sequence?

Read an explained answer

The observed succession and the understood relationship are distinguishable. Thinking connects the observations under concepts. Whether that proposed connection is adequate still depends on the observations and on the coherence of the reasoning.

Work with the idea

Describe an everyday event. Separate what was observed, the connection you supplied and the evidence that could correct it.

Detailed study 7 / 36

Why observing thinking is an exceptional situation

Reading by PDF capture: Wilson 2012, 173-capture file, 41–51; Amrine 2022, 84-capture abridgment, 16–19. Numbers identify file positions, not printed pages.

1 When thinking about a tree, attention is normally directed toward the tree and its relations. To observe that thinking, we turn toward an activity we have already carried out. The thinking that performs this observation is a new act. Steiner does not require two simultaneous acts in which the same thinking both creates and fully observes itself.

2 He calls the situation exceptional because, in the observed thinking, the observer encounters an activity they themselves produced. With a falling stone, the event is given without our producing its inner connection. With a conceptual connection we actively follow why one thought belongs with another. This is the specific transparency he appeals to.

3 Memory supplies access to the earlier act, but repeating its verbal result is insufficient. The 1918 addition emphasizes experiencing thinking actively. A learned sentence can remain an opaque possession until its connection is recreated. Steiner's proposed evidence concerns this activity, not the mere feeling of certainty attached to remembered words.

Make it concrete

First understand why three plus two equals five by carrying out the relation. Afterwards describe what you did. That description is a second thinking activity. Reciting “five” without following the relation would give much less evidence about the activity being investigated.

Test the distinction

Does observing thinking mean catching the very same act from outside while performing it?

Read an explained answer

No. We make a completed act the object of a further act. The exceptional feature is our authorship and experience of the conceptual activity, not a magical exemption from the difference between observing and doing.

Work with the idea

Understand a simple relation, pause, then describe your thinking. Distinguish the first act, its remembered content and your present description.

Detailed study 8 / 36

Thinking, consciousness and the brain

Reading by PDF capture: Wilson 2012, 173-capture file, 41–51; Amrine 2022, 84-capture abridgment, 16–19. Numbers identify file positions, not printed pages.

1 Steiner places thinking at the beginning of his inquiry because judgments about subject, object and consciousness already employ it. Saying “consciousness represents the world” presupposes concepts of consciousness, representation and world. His starting point concerns the order of justification: examine the activity used to form the theory before relying on the theory to explain that activity away.

2 A description of nervous processes and an understood conceptual relation answer different questions. A measurement may show what accompanies arithmetic in the brain; it does not, by itself, state why the arithmetic conclusion follows. Steiner uses the experienced relation between thoughts as evidence for the distinct character of thinking.

3 This is a philosophical argument with a substantial further claim: thinking is not adequately explained as a merely private bodily product. We should distinguish that claim from the observation that meanings and physiological descriptions differ. The latter distinction alone does not settle every question in neuroscience or prove later anthroposophical cosmology.

Make it concrete

A scan taken while someone understands a proof may locate neural activity. To assess the proof, we must still examine its premises and inferential steps. Conversely, a sound proof does not tell us everything about the biological conditions that enable the thinker to work.

Test the distinction

Why should “thinking is my brain” not end the chapter's investigation?

Read an explained answer

It is itself a thought requiring justification. The inquiry must distinguish biological conditions, experienced activity and the validity of a relation. Combining them in one phrase conceals rather than resolves these questions.

Work with the idea

Write one physiological question and one question about meaning for the same act of thinking. Explain what evidence each would require.

Go deeper: the chapter’s argument

This detailed explanation extends the first reading. The additional questions are optional.

Calculate ((3 × 3) + 1) ÷ 2 and keep your steps. First work through the expression. Afterwards examine the activity by which you connected the steps, rather than only remembering the answer.

The billiard example distinguishes two processes: the observed motion and the conceptual connection produced in thinking. Observation and thinking are the book’s proposed starting points. Every claimed principle must either be encountered or presented as a thought that can be rethought. A concept does not physically produce its object, and looking without conceptual work does not supply an explanation.

The calculation gives 3 × 3 = 9, then 9 + 1 = 10, then 10 ÷ 2 = 5. Reconstruct why each operation belongs where it does. Remembering “five” differs from working through those relations. Applying division only to the 1 changes the expression being solved. This course exercise illustrates the distinction; Steiner’s own worked scene is the billiard encounter.

Steiner calls observing one’s thinking an exceptional state. In ordinary thought we attend to the table or problem; afterwards the activity itself becomes the subject of fresh thinking. He argues that producing a thought and contemplating that same current production cannot be one act. Reflection may concern earlier thinking, another person’s reconstructed reasoning or an imagined sequence. It changes the object of attention, not one’s physical location.

He also distinguishes a conceptual account from a feeling about its object. “This is a rose” concerns the object through a concept; pleasure in the rose tells something about the person’s relation to it. Both can be observed, but the thinker participates throughout in making the conceptual connection. A learner should reconstruct that proposed difference rather than assume that every passing image or feeling is already active thinking.

The lightning–thunder example separates understanding a connection among one’s concepts from having the correct concepts for the event. Steiner holds that the connection’s ideal content can be examined directly without first discovering its physiological basis. That is his claim about how thinking is known. The exercise does not establish a theory of the brain, and the transparency of a reason does not guarantee its correct application to a phenomenon.

His discussion of Descartes limits “I think, therefore I am” to what this activity reveals: an existence with content the thinker participates in determining. He then answers comparisons with digestion: thinking can make thinking its object, whereas digestion does not investigate digestion by digesting it. The object and the investigating activity remain within the same element. This is the basis he proposes for understanding other phenomena.

An objection gives consciousness priority because thought occurs within it. Steiner distinguishes the order of existence from the starting point for understanding: to examine consciousness, subject or object, we already use thinking. His argument seeks a secure way to know what is present, not a recipe for creating a world. He likewise distinguishes the fact of thinking from whether a particular application is true or false.

The 1918 addition sharpens two objections. First, actively elaborated thinking differs from thought-images arriving like dreams; acknowledging willing within it does not undo its character as the I’s supervised activity. Second, a critic proposes that observed thought is only the appearance of hidden unconscious activity, like apparent continuous motion from repeated sparks. Steiner replies from the thinker’s participation within the activity rather than an outside view of an effect. Reconstruct that reply and its premise before deciding whether it is sufficient.

Words for the reading

Exceptional state: making thinking itself an object of observation. Thought-image: content that may arise without active elaboration. Active thinking: forming and examining conceptual connections. Epistemic starting point: the basis proposed for understanding what exists, distinct from its origin in time.

Perform thinking and then investigate its connections; its proposed priority rests on participation in the activity, not on certainty of every judgment.

More questions to explore

Why is recalling “five” different from observing the thinking?

Show a suggested answer

Recall gives a result. The exercise asks you to reconstruct the connections that produced it and then make that activity the object of fresh thinking.

Does knowing how your concepts connect prove they correctly describe the event?

Show a suggested answer

No. Steiner explicitly distinguishes the connection among the concepts one has from possessing the correct concepts and applying them correctly.

How does the 1918 addition distinguish thought-images from thinking?

Show a suggested answer

Images can arrive passively like dreams. Thinking as defined here is actively elaborated and supervised by the I; the objection about a hidden activity challenges that claimed access.
