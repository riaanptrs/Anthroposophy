Rudolf Steiner · The Philosophy of Freedom · GA 4 · The World as Percept · correction through further percepts · PDF p. 51, column 2

Every extension of the circle of my percepts compels me to correct my picture of the world.

Michael Wilson translation, Rudolf Steiner Press, 2012, eighth English edition. Line breaks and line-wrap hyphenation normalized.

Open the source or edition index →

What this passage means

A new viewpoint or encounter can revise the earlier picture. The next question concerns what this dependence establishes: it does not by itself prove that knowledge is limited to representations. Apply the same standard to the perceived eye and to the perceived object when reconstructing the later critical-idealist proof.

The focus of this lesson

Separate an appearance, its explanation and a remembered encounter.

See the idea in an example

Hold a round card facing you, then tilt it. Its outline looks narrower. Put it away and recall the view. The card, the changing appearance and your memory are related, but they are not the same thing.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

Understand the idea

The changed view need not mean that the card changed shape. Your position helps explain the appearance. A second view can correct your first judgment. Dependence on a viewpoint does not by itself prove that no object exists.

The book calls encountered content a percept. Here that includes the visible outline. The explanation connects it with the card’s shape and position. Later, your memory belongs to this particular encounter; Wilson calls it a mental picture. It need not be a vivid inner photograph.

Now consider a stronger claim: “Eyes and nerves change what reaches us, so we know only mental pictures.” Steiner asks whether the argument treats eyes and nerves as real causes while declaring every perceived object merely a picture. If it does, why are the eyes exempt from its conclusion?

He also asks how the argument connects an observed brain process with an inward sensation. Describing both ends does not yet explain the transition. These are objections to a particular argument, not a claim that eyes and nerves do no work.

Keep this idea

Rejecting that proof does not make every first impression true. Steiner still owes a positive account of how observation, thinking and memory relate. Keep that unfinished task in view as we move to knowing.

Optional: Brian Gray’s commentary
Study with the lecture

Different views, conceptual relations and your own remembered encounter

Brian Gray · Chapter 4 (00:00:10–01:21:19). Course synthesis comparing the lecture with Steiner’s book.

The lecture makes percept easier to locate by separating the act of observing from the content encountered. A sound is encountered content; attending to it is an activity. An inward feeling or remembered encounter can also become observed content. Wilson’s term is broader than physiological sensation, so the distinction is not simply outside world versus everything inside you.

A camera passed between different seats produces different views of one classroom object. This illustrates how comparing percepts through conceptual relations can identify a common object. In Wilson’s translation, Steiner distinguishes mathematical dependence on viewpoint from qualitative dependence on bodily organization. Shared conceptual relations permit comparison; they do not mean every actual judgment is correct or that everyone thinks the same opinions.

After an object leaves view, the lecture asks what remains connected with your own experience. Distinguish a general concept from the representation of this particular encounter. The lecturer often describes a visual memory, but Wilson’s mental picture does not require a vivid inner photograph. Its fuller account comes in Chapter 6. Thinking’s relation to subject and object remains an argument to reconstruct, rather than a slogan about all experience being private.

Work through an example

Ana sees a loaf from one end of a table and notices its cut face; Bruno sees its crust from the opposite end. They compare positions, visible details and the relation between the parts to identify one loaf. Ana later remembers that particular breakfast, while the concept of a loaf can apply to many other encounters. Her original view, the general concept and her remembered encounter have different roles even if she recalls the breakfast without any vivid visual image.

Check the distinction: Which differences belong to percepts, and which connection permits a shared identification?

Show an explained answer

The differing views belong to the contents each person encounters from a position. A conceptual relation between the views, parts and positions permits identification of one loaf. A later representation belongs to the particular encounter in each person’s experience; it is neither the present loaf nor merely the general concept.

Detailed study 9 / 36

Percept, perception and mental picture

Reading by PDF capture: Wilson 2012, 173-capture file, 52–63; Amrine 2022, 84-capture abridgment, 20–22. Numbers identify file positions, not printed pages.

1 A percept is what is encountered in observation; perception names the activity or process of encountering it. Steiner uses percept broadly enough to include an observed feeling or an object of inner attention. It does not mean only a physical object seen with the eyes, nor does it already mean a memory image stored in the mind.

2 A mental picture is an individual representation associated with experience. It can remain when the original percept is absent. Confusing the encountered content with its later representation makes it too easy to declare that we never encounter anything except private pictures, before the argument has actually established that conclusion.

3 The perceptual field changes with the observer's situation. A distant tree appears smaller; an unfamiliar sound may initially lack a recognized source. These variations motivate the problem of knowledge. They do not by themselves prove either that the tree is unreal or that the first appearance is its complete reality.

Make it concrete

Looking at a cup gives a presently encountered shape and color. Turning away and remembering it gives a representation connected to that encounter. Calling both “my image” hides a difference that Steiner needs to investigate, even if both involve bodily conditions.

Test the distinction

Can a feeling be a percept in this book's vocabulary?

Read an explained answer

Yes, when it is encountered as an object of observation. “Percept” marks its role as given content, rather than classifying it as physical. This broader usage matters in the discussion of pleasure and pain in Chapter 13.

Work with the idea

Describe a sound heard now and its later recollection. Identify the percept, the act of perception and the representation.

Detailed study 10 / 36

The argument that everything is representation

Reading by PDF capture: Wilson 2012, 173-capture file, 52–63; Amrine 2022, 84-capture abridgment, 20–22. Numbers identify file positions, not printed pages.

1 Steiner reconstructs arguments for the view that the perceived world is only our representation. Their familiar starting point is that perception depends on sense organs, nerves and the brain. What we experience would then be the inner effect of an external cause, rather than the cause itself. Read these passages as positions under examination.

2 The crucial difficulty concerns what the explanation assumes. To explain the whole perceived world as a private representation, it appeals to physical organs and processes taken from that very world. If the argument then declares those organs to be mere representations too, its original explanatory foundation has changed. The premises no longer have the status the proof required.

3 Steiner's criticism targets this route to the conclusion. Showing that a proof is circular or self-undermining is not the same as showing that its conclusion is false by every possible argument. The positive account of knowing still has to follow. Otherwise criticism of one theory would become an unsupported return to appearances.

Make it concrete

Someone says, “The eye is a real mechanism that proves all seen things, including the eye, are only private images.” Ask what justifies the eye's status at each stage. The same item is being used as an independently real cause and then deprived of that status.

Test the distinction

Does the dependence of perception on bodily conditions prove that its content is wholly unreal?

Read an explained answer

No. Dependence establishes a condition of appearing. A separate argument is required for the claim that the content has no relation to reality. Chapter 5 proposes how the conceptual contribution can complete the perceptual one.

Work with the idea

Reconstruct the argument in three premises. Mark exactly where the status of the sense organ changes.

Detailed study 11 / 36

Why changing appearances do not settle reality

Reading by PDF capture: Wilson 2012, 173-capture file, 52–63; Amrine 2022, 84-capture abridgment, 20–22. Numbers identify file positions, not printed pages.

1 An appearance gives an aspect under particular conditions. A change of viewpoint can reveal another aspect without turning the first encounter into sheer nothing. The task is to understand the lawful relations among the aspects. Steiner challenges the assumption that a first, isolated percept must either be complete reality or a wholly deceptive copy.

2 The observer's own body also enters the perceived field. A theory cannot quietly place the body outside every difficulty it attributes to other percepts. It must account consistently for how the observer, the observed body and the rest of the world become known. This prevents an unnoticed privileged exception in the explanation.

3 The chapter prepares a constructive question: what does thinking contribute when we correct one appearance by another? Even recognizing that two views belong to the same object involves relations. The answer is developed in Chapter 5, rather than supplied by merely asserting a hidden object behind the appearances.

Make it concrete

A round plate looks elliptical from the side and round from above. Knowing the relation among viewpoint, shape and projection helps explain both appearances. Simply throwing away one view as false would lose part of the evidence used to understand the object.

Test the distinction

Does correction require denying that the earlier appearance occurred?

Read an explained answer

No. What changes is the interpretation of its place in a larger relation. The side view remains an experienced appearance; the mistake would be treating its isolated outline as an exhaustive account of the plate.

Work with the idea

Choose a viewpoint-dependent appearance. Explain its conditions and what conceptual relation lets you connect it with another view.

Optional source comparison question
What is a percept in this chapter’s terminology?

Reveal the explanation

A percept is the content encountered, rather than the act alone. The chapter includes feelings and thinking when they are first observed, so the term is wider than external sensation.

Check your understanding

Did tilting the card necessarily change its shape?

Show a suggested answer

No. A change in viewpoint explains the narrower appearance without requiring a cut or stretch. Compare encounters before deciding what changed in the object.

Can a memory be noticed without being another sight of the card?

Show a suggested answer

Yes. The memory is present inwardly, while the card may be put away. Calling both experiences observable does not make them the same encounter.

Does Steiner’s objection prove that perception never misleads?

Show a suggested answer

No. He questions whether one argument establishes that we know only pictures. The failure of that argument does not prove the opposite or remove the need to correct judgments.

Go deeper: the chapter’s argument

This detailed explanation extends the first reading. The additional questions are optional.

A circular card viewed face-on has a round outline; tilted, it presents a narrower outline. The changed appearance belongs to the encounter from a changed viewpoint. It does not require the card to have been cut into a new shape. After the card is put away, recalling either view supplies a representation of the encounter. Object, appearance, explanatory concept and remembered representation therefore have different roles before the chapter asks how they are related.

Thinking produces concepts, and concepts form connections: organism connects with growth and orderly development. An idea is a fuller, more comprehensive conceptual content, not a different kind of substance. Steiner keeps thinking as his starting point; its asserted self-supporting character cannot simply be assigned to every finished concept. His contrast with Hegel concerns this priority of activity over the concept treated as primary.

In Spencer’s example, a rustle and moving grass lead to discovering a partridge. Steiner asks what makes that an explanation: thinking regards the noise as an effect and searches for a cause. Repeated encounters alone do not, in his argument, explain the concepts through which they are connected. Observation prompts the activity; conceptual work supplies the relation by which the observed bird explains the earlier noise.

Consciousness is the stage where observation and concepts meet. Thinking directed to an object yields object-consciousness; directed to the thinker it yields self-consciousness. Steiner argues that thinking cannot simply be labelled subjective, since the concepts subject and object are themselves formed through it. In his account the activity both connects the I with the world and distinguishes the I from the things it confronts.

The imagined observer with developed intelligence but no prior conceptual activity is a thought experiment for isolating contributions. It is not an instruction to become a concept-free learner. “Percept” names the encountered object or content of observation, not the act of observing. Its scope includes an inward feeling and thinking as first encountered, as well as colours and sounds. “Sensation” would be narrower in the physiological usage discussed.

Further percepts can correct our picture of the world. The avenue looks narrower at its distant end, and its appearance changes when we move: Steiner calls this mathematical dependence on viewpoint. He calls dependence on bodily organization qualitative, illustrated by hearing and differences in colour perception. The chapter distinguishes these conditions of appearance before asking what they establish about the object’s existence.

Self-perception introduces a further distinction. You perceive a tree and can also notice yourself as its observer; when the tree leaves view, an after-effect remains connected with your own history. Wilson calls this a mental picture. It is present as an inward content, while the tree was encountered as an outer object. The term does not require a vivid inner photograph. Translators’ “representation” may name the same Vorstellung, so compare definitions rather than invent a new faculty.

Steiner separates two positions. Berkeley’s account, as presented here, denies an independently material world and places its continuance in spirit, including the Eternal Spirit. The Kantian account presented through Volkelt allows a reality beyond mental pictures but treats it as inaccessible directly to our organization. The chapter’s passage from Volkelt is not a direct quotation from Kant. Keep both positions distinct from the chapter’s own proposed investigation.

The critical-idealist argument traces an external influence through sense organ, nerve, brain, sensation and the soul’s assembled mental picture. Steiner questions its proof in two ways. First, it relies on perceived eyes and nerves as objectively valid, then classifies all perceived objects as subjective pictures; applying that conclusion to its own foundations removes the support it borrowed. Second, outer observation reaches brain processes and inner observation begins with sensation, leaving their asserted transition unobserved in this argument.

The closing discussion applies the same consistency test to Schopenhauer’s eye-and-hand formulation. Eyes and hands are percepts too; the proof cannot exempt them without grounds. Steiner concludes that this route does not establish that the perceived world is only mental pictures and calls for another account of percept and representation. Rejecting that proof alone is not a demonstration that every naive appearance is accurate. Chapter 5 develops the positive alternative.

Words for the reading

Percept: content encountered through observation, including inward content. Concept: a thought-content related within a conceptual whole. Idea: a fuller or more comprehensive concept. Mental picture / representation: the inward after-effect connected with a particular encounter. Critical idealism: the position whose proof of knowledge restricted to mental pictures is examined here.

Distinguish appearance, conceptual relation and mental picture, and apply the same standard to an argument’s premises and conclusion.

More questions to explore

Is a percept the act of perceiving, or only an external sensation?

Show a suggested answer

Neither. It is the encountered content of observation; the chapter also includes feelings and thinking as first observed.

How do Berkeley and the Kantian account differ here?

Show a suggested answer

In Steiner’s presentation Berkeley denies independently material objects, while the Kantian account allows a reality beyond representations but limits direct knowledge to our inward changes.

What are the two objections to the critical-idealist proof?

Show a suggested answer

It grants objective status to perceived organs while denying that status to perceived objects generally, and it does not observe the asserted transition from brain process to sensation.
