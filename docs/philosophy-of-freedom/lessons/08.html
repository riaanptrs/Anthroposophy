Rudolf Steiner · The Philosophy of Freedom · GA 4 · Chapter 5: The Act of Knowing · PDF p. 66, column 2

The act of knowing is the synthesis of percept and concept. Only the percept and concept together constitute the whole thing.

Michael Wilson translation, Rudolf Steiner Press, 2012, eighth English edition. Line breaks and line-wrap hyphenation normalized.

Open the source or edition index →

What this passage means

This is the positive account developed after the criticism of the representation-only proof. A percept supplies the encountered side; thinking supplies its fitting conceptual context. In the rose and stone examples, identify both contributions. Shared conceptual content does not require identical encounters or feelings.

The focus of this lesson

See what we share when we understand a triangle.

See the idea in an example

Draw a tall, narrow triangle. A friend draws a wide one. The drawings differ, yet you both recognize a closed figure with three straight sides.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

Understand the idea

The shared element is not a third drawing hidden behind the first two. It is the relation you understand: three straight sides enclosing a space. Neither color nor size defines that relation.

Saying “triangle” is not enough. Someone can repeat the word while pointing to a square. Understanding shows itself when the person can explain why a new drawing does or does not fit.

Steiner distinguishes this concept from a representation tied to a particular encounter. Remembering your friend's blue triangle retains something of that individual drawing. Understanding triangularity can apply to drawings you have never seen.

This illustrates his claim that thinking can reach shared conceptual content. Agreement over a shape alone does not prove every claim about a common world of ideas. The stronger argument concerns what thinking itself apprehends, developed in the deeper reading.

Keep this idea

We can understand the same relation without having identical pictures, memories or feelings.

Optional: Brian Gray’s commentary
Study with the lecture

Shared meaning and a checked application

Brian Gray · Chapter 5 (00:00:18–01:20:54). Course synthesis comparing the lecture with Steiner’s book.

The lecturer uses the triangle to distinguish a concept's shared meaning from each person's encounter with it. Two people can understand the same relation through different drawings and with different feelings. The word alone does not establish that understanding. Steiner makes the stronger philosophical claim that thinking connects individual people with a common conceptual world; reconstruct its reasons rather than mistake matching vocabulary for evidence of it.

The lecture also stresses that an inappropriate concept can be corrected by further observation. Naming a moving path is different from establishing the conditions under which a mathematical description fits it. Steiner's parabola example concerns those conditions; applying that model to every ball without considering wind or air resistance would overreach. A shared concept and a mistaken application can coexist. The useful exercise is to specify the relation, the observed evidence and the conditions still needing examination.

For Steiner, intuition here is conceptual content apprehended inwardly, not the excitement of having an idea. Put a drawing away: its remembered encounter is now inward content, not another sight of the paper. The 1918 addition then asks about thinking itself: can you directly follow the activity through which you connect the elements? The lecturer's invitation to work through the difficulty becomes an exercise in understanding your own reasoning, rather than repeating an answer supplied by someone else.

Work through an example

Three learners compare sketches over a video call. Ana shows a narrow triangle and Luiz a broad one. Both explain a closed figure bounded by three straight sides. Bia calls a shape with one curved edge a triangle. Agreement on the word does not make her application fit their stated concept. They examine the edge and revise the classification. Later, Ana recalls her blue sketch; that remembered reference differs from the shared defining relation.

Check the distinction: What must you examine to distinguish shared conceptual understanding from agreement on a label?

Show an explained answer

Ask each learner to explain the relation and show how it applies to the encountered figure. Ana and Luiz understand the same definition despite different drawings; Bia's figure does not satisfy it. The correction concerns application, not whether each person may invent a private meaning unnoticed. Their particular memories and feelings can differ while the relation remains shared. Finally, trace the thinking that connected the definition with the evidence.

Optional source comparison question
How can conceptual content be shared despite different drawings or feelings?

Reveal the explanation

Steiner distinguishes conceptual relation from the particular percepts and feelings accompanying it. The drawings need not match for their makers to understand the same relation.

Check your understanding

Must the two drawings look alike?

Show a suggested answer

No. Their proportions can differ while each has three straight sides enclosing a space. The understood relation explains the shared classification.

Is remembering the blue triangle a new perception of it?

Show a suggested answer

No. It is a representation related to an earlier encounter. You would need to look again to learn whether the drawing has changed.

Does agreeing on a word establish understanding?

Show a suggested answer

No. Ask what makes the word applicable. A person may repeat a label without grasping the relation.

Go deeper: the chapter’s argument

This detailed explanation extends the first reading. The additional questions are optional.

Two students draw different triangles: one narrow, one broad. Both understand a closed figure bounded by three straight sides. Their drawings, encounters and feelings differ, but the defining conceptual relation can be the same. Ask each student to explain it; using the same word alone does not establish shared understanding.

Steiner distinguishes a concept's content from its individual appearance in consciousness. The triangle concept does not become two different concepts simply because two people grasp it. Their sensations and feelings give the encounter a personal coloring. From this distinction he develops the philosophical claim that thinking participates in a common world of ideas, while perceiving and feeling locate each person within a particular life.

Intuition names the inward apprehension of conceptual content. It does not mean excitement about an idea or a prediction without reasons. Trace a relation you actually understand, such as why the figure closes when its three sides meet. That activity and its understood content differ from remembering the label triangle. Steiner describes explanation as placing what initially seems separate into its context.

Now put one drawing away and recall it. The remembered encounter is present as an inward experience, not as a second sight of the paper. When noticed, that memory is a subjective percept. Chapter 5 locates this enduring modification; Chapter 6 defines representation as a concept retaining its reference to a particular percept. A verbal recollection can illustrate the distinction just as well as a vivid visual image.

The 1918 addition follows the tempting inference that, because a representation is required for my relation to a thing, I know only representations and the thing must remain inaccessible. Steiner asks us to work through this inference ourselves. The proposed hidden reality repeats the earlier picture of an independently existing object acting on a subject; relocating it beyond consciousness does not explain the relation anew.

Thinking is the proposed exception. Steiner argues that in observing thinking we encounter an activity in which we participate directly, rather than a mental picture that conceals its object. Reconstruct an actual connection, then observe how you made it. This exercise makes the premise intelligible; it does not turn a remembered answer or a feeling of certainty into proof that every judgment is true. State what the exception would contribute to the account of knowing if its argument succeeds.

Words for the reading

Conceptual content: the relation understood; individual apprehension: a person's act of understanding it; subjective percept: inward content made an object of observation; representation: a concept retaining reference to a particular encounter; naive realism of thinking: Steiner's proposed direct access to thinking's activity.

Shared meaning and individual experience can coexist; the addition asks how thinking itself is experienced.

More questions to explore

Must the drawings or feelings match for conceptual content to be shared?

Show a suggested answer

No. Different percepts and personal responses can accompany understanding of the same conceptual relation.

Can an observed memory be a percept without being a new sight of the object?

Show a suggested answer

Yes. It is an inward, subjective percept of the remembered encounter, not the present external percept.

What does the thinking exception add to the argument?

Show a suggested answer

It proposes a directly experienced activity through which the conceptual side of reality can be known, instead of treating every relation to reality as an obscuring representation.

Describe two different triangle drawings and their shared defining relation. Recall one out of sight, using words or an image, and distinguish concept, individual encounter, feeling and representation. Then reconstruct one conceptual connection you made and explain the 1918 addition's objection to inserting an inaccessible reality behind representations.
