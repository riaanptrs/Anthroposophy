Practice: a new response and its actual means

Apply the three roles of moral action and explain why an action's history does not settle its present motive.

What must be examined before treating a novel response as a free deed?

1 · Read the passage

Begin with the book

Rudolf Steiner · The Philosophy of Freedom · GA 4 · Chapter 12, Moral Imagination (Darwinism and Morality) · PDF p. 120, column 2

Human action does not create percepts, but transforms already existing percepts and gives them a new form.

Michael Wilson translation, Rudolf Steiner Press, 2012, eighth English edition. Line breaks and line-wrap hyphenation normalized.

Open the source or edition index →

What this passage means

This begins the account of technical realization: the imagined deed must work within circumstances and their laws. It does not deny creativity. Distinguish moral intuition, the concrete possible form, and the knowledge or skill needed to make that form effective.

The focus of this lesson

Apply the three roles of moral action and explain why an action's history does not settle its present motive.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

Understand the idea

Use moral intuition, imagination and technique as different questions. Why should the person's participation matter? What concrete arrangement could express that reason? What equipment, permissions, skills and checks would make the arrangement work? An audio version may require a reader and a usable delivery method. Alternatively, the member may explain that a particular accessible text format works better. Listening can revise the proposal without erasing the ethical aim.

Two people can contribute different capacities. One may imagine a suitable response and another know how to produce it. A competent technician need not have formed the original ethical insight; an imaginative organizer may lack technical skill. The book explicitly allows this difference. Evaluate the delivered result with the person who will use it, and separately examine what moved the contributors. Cooperation alone neither proves nor disproves their ethical freedom.

Now reconstruct the evolution argument without importing a scientific conclusion it does not establish. A later form can arise historically from an earlier one even when its complete concept cannot be deduced from the earlier concept alone. Likewise, finding influences on a new ethical response differs from showing that earlier doctrine logically determines its entire content. Steiner presents the individual's productive contribution as compatible with development. His nineteenth-century examples are historical elements of that philosophical account.

The old printed rule may originally have served a useful purpose. Its age does not determine whether you now understand and endorse it, and the audio proposal's novelty does not prove an ethical motive. It could seek praise or merely copy another institution. A recording blocked by failed equipment raises a further issue: an aim can be understood without being executable at that moment. Keep inherited history, present determining reason, and practical ability in separate columns.

The 1918 addition gives a demanding condition for freedom: ideal intuition itself must sustain the act. The three-role map helps investigate a deed, but ticking its boxes does not certify that condition. Review your response, consider a mixed-motive possibility, and explain which claim you can support by the person's account and the practical evidence. Then return to the core lesson's distinction between observation of the present action and an account of its development.

Keep this idea

Trace a deed's history, motive and means separately, then let actual feedback improve its concrete form.

Study with the lecture

Communicate the aim while allowing a better proposal

Brian Gray · Chapter 12 (00:00:27–00:17:23). Course synthesis comparing the lecture with Steiner’s book.

The lecturer's account of architect, engineer and builder highlights a practical problem: how does an idea remain understandable when several people help realize it? Instead of demanding identical mental images, state the ethical aim, the proposed arrangement and what evidence would show that it works. The book allows technical expertise and moral imagination to reside in different people (PDF 120).

Collaboration may improve the form rather than dilute it. A technician can reveal that the first plan excludes the person it was intended to help, and a participant can suggest a more suitable arrangement. Preserve the understood reason while examining those suggestions. Neither following someone else's technical advice nor inventing a new format establishes the origin of a free motive; an inherited response can also be understood afresh.

The lecturer sets aside most of the evolution argument. Keep this distinction from the book: a later moral contribution may develop through earlier experiences without being logically deducible in full from an earlier rule (PDF 121–124). Once a new arrangement exists, we can investigate its history and consequences. That retrospective explanation differs from observing whether ideal intuition determines the person's present act; technical success alone does not supply the answer.

Work through an example

An organizer proposes one long recording for the group's audio materials. A collaborator explains that short, clearly labeled files would let the participant locate sections more easily. The organizer accepts that revision because it better serves participation. They agree on the aim, test the files and keep a record of the practical changes instead of treating the first imagined form as untouchable.

Check the distinction: Does tracing this proposal to an earlier practice show that its whole content was predetermined?

Show an explained answer

No. A history of influences can explain how a response developed without deriving every new detail from an earlier rule. Reconstruct that history and separately examine the person's present determining reason and the result's usability.

Check your understanding

What must be examined before treating a novel response as a free deed?

Reveal the explanation

A new format may still be copied or pursued for praise. The lesson therefore asks about the determining motive and how its practical means realise the aim.

Original open question and answer

Does a new format prove a new free motive?

Show a suggested answer

No. A novel response can still be copied or pursued for praise; the determining reason must be examined.

Must the same person supply ethical insight, imagination and every technical skill?

Show a suggested answer

No. The chapter allows collaborators with different capacities, while preserving the question of each contributor's motive.

What does a failed recording show?

Show a suggested answer

It may show a practical or technical obstacle. It does not by itself show whether the intended deed arose from an individually apprehended ethical reason.
