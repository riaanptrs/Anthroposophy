In so far as man follows the impulses coming from this side, he feels himself to be free.

Open the source or edition index →

What this passage means

The preceding sentences identify 'this side' as the idea coming to manifestation in the individual. Read it with the following acknowledgement of partly free and partly unfree action. Feeling free alone is not the whole argument; the proposed determining source is ideal intuition.

The focus of this lesson

Explain how monism distinguishes imposed action from individual ethical insight while recognizing actual constraints.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

Understand the idea

The chapter follows moral authority through several forms. A person may obey an individual, the majority, a church, a deity imagined as a commanding person, or an inner voice treated as an absolute power. Moving a command inside does not, by itself, transform obedience into individually understood ethical content. Steiner calls the first positions naive realism and the projection of independently existing moral standards metaphysical realism. Identify the position before accepting or rejecting it.

Two metaphysical accounts appear opposed but have a similar consequence in his analysis. Materialistic determinism makes willing a necessary effect of material processes. Spiritualistic dualism makes willing an instrument of an absolute being whose intentions the individual must execute. Hartmann's project of redeeming a suffering God illustrates the second. Steiner challenges explanations that place the decisive source of action beyond what the person can experience through percept and thought. His objection is an argument to reconstruct, not a demonstration that every bodily influence is irrelevant.

Monism recognizes perceptible constraints. A locked workshop, missing equipment, an institution's penalties, habits and inherited practices can affect activity. It rejects adding an unknowable determining being behind percept and concept. The chapter asks someone alleging unfreedom to identify the relevant person, thing or institution within the experienceable world. This does not mean a constraint ceases to exist because its cause has not yet been discovered. It sets the author's proposed boundary for a justified explanation.

Human action is described as partly unfree and partly free. When an external order simply supplies my determining principle, I have not produced the ethical content individually. When an ideal intuition becomes my own reason for acting, the source is different. In the workshop, explaining why reliable access matters is a beginning; a fluent explanation could still disguise a wish for approval. Neither conformity nor disobedience settles the question. The criterion concerns the experienced determining motive, not the visible novelty of the deed.

The rosebud image presents the free spirit as a developmental possibility. Nature does not deliver a completed free individual; instinct and obedient conduct are described as preparatory phases. Physical and historical limitations continue to matter. Read the strong claim that genuinely moral action must overcome compulsion alongside the acknowledgement of mixed conduct. It is not a classification of people into permanently superior and inferior groups. A practice learned from others can later be understood and taken up through one's own ethical insight.

The first 1918 addition addresses a difficulty: if thinking apprehends universally valid ideas, how can moral action be individual? Steiner says the same intuitive activity enters shared conceptual content in knowing and individualizes a portion of that content in willing. Two members can understand why common access matters yet find different suitable contributions. This illustrates the distinction; it does not prove that every personal proposal belongs to a harmonious ethical whole. Universal meaning and individual realization are the two sides to explain.

The second addition criticizes concealed materialism: renouncing that label does not help if all explanatory concepts remain restricted to material necessity. Conversely, merely calling an act spiritual supplies no adequate account of its freedom. Bring the whole chapter back to the workshop: describe the real conditions, the principle initially received, and the ethical reason you now claim to understand. Then examine how that reason entered the present act. Freedom here is a developing capacity within the world, and its proposed foundation still depends on the earlier account of intuitive thinking.

Keep this idea

Freedom concerns how an ethical reason determines a deed; it develops amid actual constraints and mixed motives.

Study with the lecture

Separate an obstacle from the reason for acting

Brian Gray · Chapter 10 (00:00:46–00:20:47). Course synthesis comparing the lecture with Steiner’s book.

The lecturer makes a useful everyday distinction: a door closing on a project is an event; treating that event as a command from an unseen authority is an added interpretation. Begin with the observable condition and then ask what reason should determine your response. In the book, monism accepts actual physical and historical restrictions while challenging an inferred ruler behind them (PDF 109–112).

Persistence and freedom answer different questions. A person may press ahead to win admiration, while another may revise a plan because the understood ethical aim requires it. The lecture's encouragement to realize an idea helps make the chapter practical, but does not establish that every barrier can be overcome. The same outward response can express compliance, habit, fear or an individually understood reason.

The lecturer describes intuition through divine participation and co-creation. Keep that spiritual interpretation distinct from the argument you can reconstruct here: shared ideal content can receive an individual ethical realization, as the first 1918 addition explains (PDF 113). Neither feeling inspired nor declaring oneself independent settles whether that content actually determines the deed. Examine the reason, the conditions and your possible alternative motives together.

Work through an example

A group wants to offer free evening tutoring, but its room reservation is refused. Ana first calls the refusal a sign to abandon the project. She then finds that the room closes early, asks families about useful hours, and proposes afternoon sessions elsewhere. The changed arrangement can serve the same understood aim; the refusal alone does not tell her what she ought to want.

Check the distinction: Would refusing to change the evening plan show greater freedom?

Show an explained answer

No. Stubbornness could arise from pride, and a revision could express the ethical aim more faithfully. Identify the actual restriction and the reason determining the response; persistence alone cannot answer the freedom question.

See the idea in an example

A community workshop asks you to return borrowed tools before closing. At first you comply because the organizer may exclude you. Later you understand that other members need the same tools and make reliable return your own aim. The outward act can look identical. Ask what determines it now, and what remains uncertain about your reasons.

Check your understanding

What distinguishes free ethical content from an authority’s command moved inward?

Reveal the explanation

An internalised command may still operate as something independently imposed. The chapter asks whether individually apprehended ethical content determines the action.

Original open question and answer

Does moving an authority's command into an inner voice establish freedom?

Show a suggested answer

No. The voice may still function as an independent command. The question is whether individually apprehended ethical content determines the act.

Does monism deny physical and historical limitations?

Show a suggested answer

No. It accepts actual limitations and mixed free and unfree conduct while rejecting an unknowable determining reality added behind percept and concept.

How can shared ideas and individual deeds belong to the same account?

Show a suggested answer

The 1918 addition says intuitive thinking apprehends shared ideal content in knowing and individualizes a part of it as a particular ethical aim. Explaining that proposal is different from proving every personal aim ethical.
