What this passage means

This is the positive account developed after the criticism of the representation-only proof. A percept supplies the encountered side; thinking supplies its fitting conceptual context. In the rose and stone examples, identify both contributions. Shared conceptual content does not require identical encounters or feelings.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

Understand the idea

The chapter first returns to the argument that all perceived things are mental pictures. Its physiological explanation assumes that eyes, nerves and brain are real objects, then concludes that perceived objects are only representations. Steiner compares this proof to building an upper floor on a ground floor that collapses: rejecting the premises cannot leave their conclusion supported by those same premises. He explicitly distinguishes the truth of critical idealism from the validity of this particular proof.

Two positions follow. Absolute illusionism regards world and self alike as dreamlike appearances. Hartmann's transcendental realism instead seeks the independent realities that supposedly cause representations. Both undermine interest in connections within immediate experience: illusionism treats these as dreamlike, while transcendental realism searches for their hidden causes. Steiner offers a different comparison: as waking gives a new understanding of dreams, thinking gives a new understanding of mere perceiving. Even the claim that the world is only a representation already uses thinking.

Why treat thinking as an irrelevant addition to an otherwise complete world? Steiner argues that the world produces thinking through humans just as it produces blossoms through plants. The concept arises when a thinking being encounters the plant; that condition does not make the concept alien to the phenomenon. The argument concerns the reality disclosed in knowing. It does not say that thinking physically makes the flower grow.

A momentary appearance is a section of an unfolding process. The rose example makes that temporal incompleteness visible. The stone's parabolic path makes a different point: successive observed positions and their lawful relation belong to one phenomenon. Mathematics supplies a concept through which the path becomes intelligible. Steiner attributes the initial separation of percept and concept to our organization, which receives these elements from two sides.

Our limited place in space and time also separates what belongs together. Red appears with other qualities; an isolated thing occupies a wider context. Self-perception similarly presents personal qualities within a boundary, whereas thinking relates that self to the world process. The concept of a triangle has the same content when understood by different people, although each apprehends it individually. Steiner extends this example into his stronger claim that thinking connects individual beings with a universal conceptual whole.

Intuition here means conceptual content appearing inwardly through thinking. Observation gives the perceptual side; intuition gives the conceptual side. Knowing joins them, and explanation restores a phenomenon's context. The chapter rejects matter, force, a humanlike deity or Schopenhauer's experienced will as a sufficient universal principle: each is first encountered within a limited field. Schopenhauer's bodily will is also a self-percept whose nature, Steiner replies, must be understood through thinking.

The account does not dispense with sensory physiology. Vibrations, an eye, a sound and brain processes can all be investigated as percepts; thinking establishes their relations. Sensory dependence alone does not establish that an eye produces color out of an unknowable thing. A table later recalled is different: the remembered encounter is a subjective modification that remains after the external percept has disappeared. Confusing these two percepts generates the representation-only view under discussion. Chapter 6 will define representation more closely.

The 1918 addition asks readers to live through the reflective difficulty, rather than simply refute someone else's theory. Reflection naturally turns attention toward representations, but a hidden thing-in-itself can merely repeat naive realism's assumptions behind a new boundary. Thinking is Steiner's proposed exception: in examining its activity, no separate representation stands between the process and its observer. Reconstruct the reasons for this exception and its role in the synthesis of percept and concept before judging the whole argument.

Keep this idea

Steiner's positive account joins encountered content with its conceptual context; it does more than criticize the representation-only proof.

Study with the lecture

From an appearance to an understood relation

Brian Gray · Chapter 5 (00:00:18–01:20:54). Course synthesis comparing the lecture with Steiner’s book.

The lecturer's contrast between an apple before you and its remembered picture makes a useful first distinction: encountering something now differs from retaining its reference afterward. Wilson's chapter calls the latter a subjective percept; Chapter 6 explains it as an individualized concept. A percept can also be an inward memory or feeling when observed. A mental picture therefore need not be an inner photograph, and the external encounter must not simply be renamed a private representation.

The lecturer applies the developing rosebud to biography: one moment does not exhaust a plant or a person. Steiner's point is that thinking restores relations which a momentary appearance does not display by itself. Conditions matter: a bud deprived of water may never blossom. His two criticized positions remain distinct. Absolute illusionism treats experience as dreamlike; transcendental realism seeks independent causes behind representations. His alternative joins percept and fitting concept within knowing.

The lecture's waking analogy helps explain why thinking makes a percept intelligible, but the 1918 addition goes further. Steiner proposes that, when observing thinking, we participate directly in its activity without an intervening representation concealing it. This is an argument to reconstruct, not a guarantee that every judgment is correct. Nor does it dismiss physiology: eyes, vibrations and sensations are investigated as percepts, while their explanatory connections require thinking. Dependence on an organ alone does not prove production by an unknowable hidden cause.

Work through an example

Nina photographs a bean seed on three mornings: unchanged, split, then showing a root. She records when it was watered. The photographs give encountered stages; the concept of germination connects them under conditions. Predicting a shoot is different from observing one. A week later, Nina's account of that particular seed retains the reference to her encounter. If another seed never sprouts, she investigates its conditions rather than forcing it into the same story.

Check the distinction: Why does joining a percept with a concept differ from either declaring the world a dream or placing its explanation behind an inaccessible boundary?

Show an explained answer

Absolute illusionism treats accessible world and self as dreamlike, whereas transcendental realism seeks independent realities causing representations. Steiner instead locates knowing in the union of encountered content and its intelligible relation. In Nina's case, observed stages and the concept of germination belong together; missing conditions remain research questions. The inward origin of the concept does not make the seed a private image or justify an unobserved shoot.

See the idea in an example

Imagine three dated views of a rose: bud, opening flower and blossom. Each appearance belongs to a different moment. You understand them as stages of one plant's development by relating them through a concept. If the bud receives no water, some possible stages never occur. The concept of development must therefore include conditions, rather than turn an expected blossom into an observed fact.
