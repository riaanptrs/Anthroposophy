Every extension of the circle of my percepts compels me to correct my picture of the world.

Michael Wilson translation, Rudolf Steiner Press, 2012, eighth English edition. Line breaks and line-wrap hyphenation normalized.

Open the source or edition index →

What this passage means

A new viewpoint or encounter can revise the earlier picture. The next question concerns what this dependence establishes: it does not by itself prove that knowledge is limited to representations. Apply the same standard to the perceived eye and to the perceived object when reconstructing the later critical-idealist proof.

The focus of this lesson

Test an argument fairly before accepting or rejecting it.

See the idea in an example

Someone says: “I know that your eyes and brain produce pictures of the world. Therefore all perceived things are only pictures.” Before agreeing, ask how the speaker knows about those eyes and that brain.

Explore the chapter’s core ideas

Understand the idea

The argument uses perceived organs as real causes. Its conclusion then places all perceived objects among mental pictures. That includes the organs it used as evidence. Steiner’s first objection is this inconsistency: the conclusion removes the special status given to its own starting points.

A second question is different. Even if we describe the brain process and describe the experience of colour, have we explained the passage between them? Steiner says the offered argument has not supplied that link. A missing link and an inconsistent premise are two problems, not two names for one problem.

The position being tested is not simply Steiner’s own view. The detailed reading distinguishes Berkeley’s account from the account of Kant presented through Volkelt. Those historical voices should not be merged into a single claim attributed to Steiner.

Keep this idea

Finding a problem in a proof leaves work to do. It does not establish that every appearance is accurate. Return to the main lesson’s question: what account can explain observation, thinking and the remembered encounter together?

Optional: Brian Gray’s commentary
Study with the lecture

Apply the conclusion to the eye used in its own proof

Brian Gray · Chapter 4 (00:00:10–01:21:19). Course synthesis comparing the lecture with Steiner’s book.

The lecture makes Steiner’s consistency challenge concrete: an argument dismisses a perceived object as merely a representation, yet treats the perceived eye and nerve as dependable physical causes. Give the argument a second pass. If its conclusion concerns every perceived object, it must explain why the organs used as premises receive different treatment.

This challenge differs from the book’s separate observation of a gap. Describing an outward brain process and an inward sensation does not by itself show the proposed transition between them. An account can contain both inconsistent treatment of its premises and an asserted connection not established by the observations it offers. Keep those two criticisms distinct.

The lecturer rightly separates a position’s truth from whether this proposed proof establishes it. Berkeley’s denial of independently material objects also differs from the Kantian position presented through Volkelt, which allows reality beyond our representations. Rejecting the criticised proof does not make every naive appearance accurate. Chapter 5 still has to develop Steiner’s positive account of knowing.

Work through an example

A fictional explanation says: a red sign affects a real eye; the eye sends a real nerve signal; therefore all perceived objects are only mental pictures. Put sign, eye and nerve in one list. The conclusion cannot dismiss the first percept while silently exempting the others. Then ask separately how the described nerve activity is connected to the experienced red. That second question concerns the proposed transition, not the consistency of the exemptions.

Check the distinction: What are the two questions to ask, and what would answering them still leave open?

Show an explained answer

Ask why perceived organs receive the objective status denied to other perceived objects, and where the offered observations establish the transition from bodily process to sensation. Those questions test the proposed proof. Their force alone does not prove the opposite theory or supply the complete positive relation between percept, representation and knowledge.

Optional source comparison question
What borrowed premise does Steiner identify in the representation-only argument?

Reveal the explanation

Perceived organs and processes are treated as objectively valid causes, although the argument’s conclusion classifies perceived objects generally as mental pictures.

Check your understanding

What must the speaker explain about the eyes?

Show a suggested answer

Why these perceived objects may be treated as reliable physical causes if all perceived objects are declared only mental pictures. The same standard must apply to the premises and conclusion.

Would naming more brain parts answer both objections?

Show a suggested answer

No. More detail may help investigation, but it does not by itself resolve inconsistent use of evidence or explain the proposed transition to sensation.

If this argument fails, must its conclusion be false?

Show a suggested answer

Not necessarily. A conclusion might have another justification. Criticizing this proof means showing why it does not establish the conclusion, not declaring every possible argument defeated.

Go deeper: the chapter’s argument

This detailed explanation extends the first reading. The additional questions are optional.

Someone tells you, “Your eyes and brain transform what reaches you, so all you know is your own mental pictures.” Write the claim as an argument before answering. Which objects does the explanation rely on, and which conclusion does it draw about perceived objects?

The position examined traces an influence through a physical object, sense organ, nerve and brain to sensation. Different sensations then appear combined in a mental picture, such as that of a trumpet. This is the account under examination, not Steiner’s own positive explanation. His question concerns the support for its conclusion, not simply whether the sequence sounds plausible.

Label its borrowed premise: the account treats organs and bodily processes known through perception as objectively valid causes. Now apply the conclusion to those same organs. If every perceived object is only a mental picture, why can this perceived eye be used as a secure physical cause? Steiner argues that mental pictures of eye and colour cannot perform the physical interaction on which the proof depends.

His second objection concerns the transition itself. Outer observation follows bodily processes towards the brain, while inner observation begins with sensation. The offered argument does not observe the asserted link between these spheres. Distinguish that evidential gap from the first consistency objection. Neither issue is resolved by adding the word “therefore” between the two descriptions.

Use careful attribution. The passage announcing that knowledge is limited to mental pictures comes from Volkelt’s account of Kant, not a direct Kant quotation. Berkeley’s denial of independently material objects differs from accepting an inaccessible reality beyond representations. Understanding the argument includes identifying which position is speaking and what it claims, rather than merging them into one slogan.

A failed proof does not establish the opposite conclusion. Steiner rejects this route to critical idealism and then seeks a new account of percept and mental picture. You have completed the practice when you can reconstruct the argument fairly, locate both objections and state what the next positive account still needs to explain. Words for the reading

Premise: a statement used to support a conclusion. Consistency: applying the same standard to the argument’s foundations and result. Evidential gap: a claimed connection not established by the observations offered. Naive realism: accepting immediate percepts as independently existing just as they appear.

Reconstruct a position before judging it, and distinguish an inconsistent premise from an unsupported transition.

More questions to explore

What is the borrowed premise?

Show a suggested answer

Perceived organs and processes are treated as objectively valid causes, although the argument’s conclusion classifies perceived objects generally as mental pictures.

How does the transition objection differ?

Show a suggested answer

It asks where the proposed passage from outward brain process to inward sensation is observed or justified; the first objection concerns inconsistent status assigned to premises.

Does rejecting this proof establish naive realism?

Show a suggested answer

No. It removes one proposed support for critical idealism. An alternative account still must explain the relation of percept, mental picture and knowledge.

Write the “only mental pictures” argument in three or four steps. Circle the objects it treats as real causes and apply the final conclusion to them.
