GA 4 · Lesson 16 · Chapter 12

Moral intuition: understanding a reason to act

Distinguish an ethical insight from a hunch or a repeated rule.

Start here

A participant cannot read the small print used by your study group. You recognize a reason to make participation possible. But saying “inclusion matters” does not yet show whether you have understood that reason in this situation or are repeating a familiar phrase.

Understand the idea

Steiner calls the apprehension of ethical ideal content moral intuition. Here, intuition does not mean a guess or a powerful feeling. The question concerns the ethical content that can itself move a person to act. Recall the distinction between motive and driving force in Lesson 12 of this course, which covers Chapter 9 of the book.

An existing principle can be understood and made one’s own. A new proposal can also arise from a wish for praise. Neither familiarity nor novelty decides the source of the action. Ask what you understand, why the reason matters, and what role habit, pressure or approval may still play.

Recognizing a reason and deciding exactly how to respond are different. Ask the participant what would help. Their answer informs your understanding of the situation; it does not remove your responsibility for judgment. The next lesson considers how the ethical idea takes a concrete form.

Keep this idea

Examine the reason for acting before equating a helpful appearance with a free motive.

Try it

Explain the reason for helping without using only a slogan. Then write two possible motives for the same helpful act and one question that would help distinguish them. Do not assume you can read another person’s motive.

Check your understanding

Is a strong feeling enough to establish moral intuition?

Show a suggested answer

No. The feeling and the ethical content understood are different; intensity alone does not establish the latter.

Must a free ethical idea be unprecedented?

Show a suggested answer

No. The relevant question is what determines the action, not whether the idea has ever been expressed before.

Has saying “I want to help” supplied a practical plan?

Show a suggested answer

No. The particular response still needs to be imagined and its realization worked out.

How to assess your response

Check that you explained the distinction, gave reasons and used the assigned passage. Separate the example, Steiner’s argument and your assessment. Revisit what remains unclear. Well-supported disagreement can demonstrate understanding; agreement or reports of spiritual experiences are not required.
