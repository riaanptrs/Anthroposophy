Ethical individualism is not permission for every impulse

Detailed study 22 / 36 · The idea of freedom

Return to the full chapter lesson →

Reading by PDF capture: Wilson 2012, 173-capture file, 99–112; Amrine 2022, 84-capture abridgment, 36–42. Numbers identify file positions, not printed pages.

1 Ethical individualism places the source of a free deed in an ideal grasped by the individual. It does not say that whatever an individual happens to desire is therefore moral. An impulse received from appetite, fear or social pressure has not become free simply because the person calls it authentic.

2 Nor is the alternative mechanical obedience to a general rule. A rule can communicate valuable understanding, but repeating it does not show how this particular action becomes one's own. Steiner contrasts acting under the pressure of duty with acting through insight and love of the deed. A socially approved act can be unfree in origin; a familiar duty can also be understood and freely embraced.

3 The account raises a serious question: how do we distinguish genuine ethical insight from self-serving rationalization? The book's appeal is to experienced thinking and individual responsibility, not to a certificate of moral infallibility. In studying it, test your description of the motive, consider others' situations and leave your interpretation open to correction.

Make it concrete

A student copies a friend's assignment and says, “I follow my individual truth.” The phrase does not explain an ethical intuition. Avoiding effort or gaining approval may be the actual motive. An individual claim still needs intelligible content and an honest account of the deed.

Test the distinction

Can a general moral principle ever participate in a free action?

Read an explained answer

Yes, if its content is independently understood and becomes the person's individual motive in the situation. Its being general is not the decisive defect; mere submission to its authority is the problem Steiner examines.

Work with the idea

Compare obedience, impulse and understood commitment in three versions of one act. Avoid judging freedom from the outward act alone.
