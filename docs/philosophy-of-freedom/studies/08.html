Thinking, consciousness and the brain

Detailed study 8 / 36 · Thinking in the service of knowledge

Return to the full chapter lesson →

Reading by PDF capture: Wilson 2012, 173-capture file, 41–51; Amrine 2022, 84-capture abridgment, 16–19. Numbers identify file positions, not printed pages.

1 Steiner places thinking at the beginning of his inquiry because judgments about subject, object and consciousness already employ it. Saying “consciousness represents the world” presupposes concepts of consciousness, representation and world. His starting point concerns the order of justification: examine the activity used to form the theory before relying on the theory to explain that activity away.

2 A description of nervous processes and an understood conceptual relation answer different questions. A measurement may show what accompanies arithmetic in the brain; it does not, by itself, state why the arithmetic conclusion follows. Steiner uses the experienced relation between thoughts as evidence for the distinct character of thinking.

3 This is a philosophical argument with a substantial further claim: thinking is not adequately explained as a merely private bodily product. We should distinguish that claim from the observation that meanings and physiological descriptions differ. The latter distinction alone does not settle every question in neuroscience or prove later anthroposophical cosmology.

Make it concrete

A scan taken while someone understands a proof may locate neural activity. To assess the proof, we must still examine its premises and inferential steps. Conversely, a sound proof does not tell us everything about the biological conditions that enable the thinker to work.

Test the distinction

Why should “thinking is my brain” not end the chapter's investigation?

Read an explained answer

It is itself a thought requiring justification. The inquiry must distinguish biological conditions, experienced activity and the validity of a relation. Combining them in one phrase conceals rather than resolves these questions.

Work with the idea

Write one physiological question and one question about meaning for the same act of thinking. Explain what evidence each would require.
